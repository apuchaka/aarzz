## 0.1 Approach to Regional Limb Pain

> [!danger] Referred pain — the patterns that account for most diagnostic errors
> **· KNEE pain from the HIP.** **A child or adolescent with knee pain must have the hip examined — slipped upper femoral epiphysis and Perthes disease present as knee or thigh pain with a normal knee.** The same applies in adults with hip osteoarthritis. **This is the single most important referred pattern in musculoskeletal medicine.** Cross-refer [[M5]].
> **· SHOULDER pain from the DIAPHRAGM** (C3–C5): **subphrenic abscess, ruptured spleen, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, cholecystitis, perforated viscus** — and from the heart. **Shoulder-tip pain with abdominal signs is intra-abdominal until proven otherwise.** Cross-refer [[C1]] and [[C7]] 0.5.
> **· SHOULDER and arm pain from the CERVICAL SPINE** (C5–C6 radiculopathy) and from a **Pancoast tumour** at the lung apex — with **Horner syndrome and hand wasting.** Cross-refer [[D7]] 0.3.
> **· HIP and thigh pain from the LUMBAR SPINE** — L2–L4 radiculopathy and spinal stenosis. Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.6.
> **· ELBOW pain from the neck, and from cardiac ischaemia.**
> **· FOOT pain from lumbosacral radiculopathy.**
> **A joint that is not tender, has full passive range, and has a normal examination is unlikely to be the source of the pain.**

> [!tip] Localise by structure, using movement
> **· Pain on PASSIVE movement with global restriction → the JOINT itself** (arthritis, capsulitis, sepsis).
> **· Pain on RESISTED movement with preserved passive range → MUSCLE or TENDON.** Resisted testing of specific movements localises to the specific tendon.
> **· Point tenderness over a bony prominence → ENTHESIS or bursa.**
> **· Pain reproduced by nerve tension tests, with paraesthesia → NEURAL.**
> Cross-refer [[L1]] 0.1 for the intra-articular versus periarticular distinction.

> [!warning] Red flags in regional limb pain
> **· Night pain and rest pain, unrelated to activity — particularly around the knee or proximal humerus in a young person → BONE TUMOUR.**
> **· Systemic features — fever, weight loss.**
> **· A palpable mass, especially if deep, larger than a golf ball, or growing → SARCOMA. Refer BEFORE biopsy.** Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.8.
> **· Neurovascular deficit.**
> **· Inability to weight bear after trauma.**
> **· Bilateral, symmetrical girdle pain in someone over 50 → polymyalgia rheumatica.** Cross-refer [[L3]] 0.2.

> [!warning] Corticosteroid injection — useful, and not a long-term strategy
> **Injections give short-term symptomatic relief in many soft tissue conditions.**
> **But repeated injections damage tendon and cartilage**, and in some conditions — notably **lateral epicondylalgia** — **injection produces WORSE long-term outcomes than placebo or physiotherapy.**
> **Load management and progressive exercise are the definitive treatment for most tendinopathies.** Injection buys a window in which to do the rehabilitation, not a substitute for it.

## 0.2 Shoulder

> [!danger] The discriminator: is PASSIVE range preserved?
> **· PASSIVE range PRESERVED, active painful → rotator cuff related pain or tear, subacromial pathology, ACJ disease.**
> **· PASSIVE range GLOBALLY RESTRICTED, especially EXTERNAL ROTATION → adhesive capsulitis or glenohumeral arthritis.**
> **Loss of passive external rotation is the key finding**, and it takes ten seconds to test. It is what separates a frozen shoulder from a cuff problem, and the two are managed entirely differently.

> [!tip] Rotator cuff related shoulder pain
> **The commonest shoulder complaint.** Pain over the deltoid insertion, **a painful arc through mid-abduction**, pain on overhead activity, and **night pain when lying on the affected side** — which is often what brings people in.
> **Examination: painful arc, pain and weakness on resisted abduction (empty can) and resisted external rotation.**
> **Exercise-based physiotherapy is first-line and is as effective as surgery for most patients** — a well-established point that is frequently not acted upon. Subacromial injection can facilitate rehabilitation.

> [!warning] Rotator cuff tear
> **Weakness rather than just pain**, particularly of external rotation and abduction.
> **The DROP ARM sign** — inability to lower the arm smoothly from full abduction — indicates a significant tear.
> **Distinguish an ACUTE TRAUMATIC tear in a younger patient — which warrants early surgical referral for repair — from a DEGENERATIVE tear in an older patient, where conservative management is usually appropriate.** The distinction is age and mechanism, and the referral timeframe differs.
> **A massive cuff tear causes pseudoparalysis** — the patient cannot initiate abduction at all, which can be mistaken for a neurological problem.

> [!tip] Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)
> **Global restriction of passive movement, with external rotation lost first and most.**
> Runs through phases: **freezing (painful, progressively stiffening) → frozen (stiff, less painful) → thawing (gradual recovery)**, over **one to three years** — a long and frustrating course that patients must be told about honestly.
> **Strongly associated with DIABETES** — and it is more severe and more prolonged in diabetics — as well as with **thyroid disease, Dupuytren contracture, and any period of shoulder immobilisation.**
> **Check the HbA1c in anyone presenting with frozen shoulder** — it is occasionally how diabetes is first found.
> Management: analgesia, **physiotherapy within the pain-free range**, intra-articular corticosteroid injection (most useful in the painful phase), and hydrodilatation. Surgery for refractory cases.

> [!danger] Posterior shoulder dislocation is missed on the AP film
> **Anterior dislocation is far commoner** — arm held abducted and externally rotated, loss of the deltoid contour. **Check AXILLARY NERVE function before and after reduction — regimental badge sensation and deltoid contraction — and document it.**
> **POSTERIOR dislocation is the one that is missed.** It occurs after **SEIZURES, ELECTROCUTION, and electroconvulsive therapy** — the powerful internal rotators overwhelm the external rotators — and after posteriorly directed trauma.
> **The arm is held internally rotated and the patient cannot externally rotate it at all.**
> **On the AP film it can look almost normal — the "light bulb sign" (the humeral head appears symmetrically rounded because it is internally rotated) is the clue.** **An axillary or scapular-Y view is required**, and posterior dislocations are missed for weeks or months when only an AP is taken.
> **Any patient with shoulder pain after a seizure needs adequate views.**

> [!tip] The others
> **· Acromioclavicular joint pain** — localised over the ACJ, worse on **cross-body adduction**, and pain in the highest arc of abduction rather than the mid-arc.
> **· Glenohumeral osteoarthritis** — older patients, global loss of both active and passive range with crepitus.
> **· Calcific tendinopathy** — abrupt onset of severe pain, sometimes with visible calcification on radiograph, and it can be exquisitely painful.
> **· Biceps tendinopathy and long head rupture** — with the "Popeye" deformity, usually managed conservatively.
> **· Referred and systemic causes: cervical radiculopathy, Pancoast tumour, diaphragmatic irritation, myocardial ischaemia, and polymyalgia rheumatica (bilateral).**
> **· Septic arthritis** — less common at the shoulder but it occurs, particularly in the elderly and immunosuppressed. Cross-refer [[L1]] 0.2.

**Ix:** **Examination of passive external rotation** (*why:* the single most discriminating test; *what:* restricted in capsulitis and arthritis, preserved in cuff disease). Radiographs including an **axillary or scapular-Y view where dislocation is suspected** (*why:* posterior dislocation is invisible on AP; *what:* dislocation, calcification, arthritis, cuff arthropathy). Ultrasound (*why:* an excellent, accessible test for cuff integrity and can be dynamic; *what:* tear, tendinopathy, bursal thickening). MRI (*why:* labral and intra-articular pathology, and pre-surgical planning; *what:* labral tear, cuff tear size and retraction). **HbA1c in adhesive capsulitis** (*why:* strong diabetes association; *what:* undiagnosed diabetes). **Chest radiograph where an apical cause is possible** (*why:* Pancoast tumour presents as shoulder pain; *what:* apical mass).

## 0.3 Elbow

> [!danger] Lateral epicondylalgia — and why injection is the wrong reflex
> **"Tennis elbow" is a TENDINOPATHY — a degenerative failed-healing response — not a tendinitis.** There is little inflammation, which is why anti-inflammatory approaches disappoint.
> **Pain over the lateral epicondyle, reproduced by resisted wrist extension and by gripping.**
> **Corticosteroid injection provides short-term relief but is associated with WORSE outcomes at one year than physiotherapy or watchful waiting** — one of the clearest examples in musculoskeletal medicine of a treatment that helps this week and harms next year.
> **Management: load modification, progressive eccentric and resistance exercise, a counterforce brace, and time.** Most resolve over months.

> [!tip] The rest of the elbow
> **· Medial epicondylalgia ("golfer's elbow")** — resisted wrist flexion and pronation. Same principles.
> **· Olecranon bursitis** — a fluctuant swelling over the olecranon **with a freely mobile elbow joint**, which distinguishes it from septic arthritis. **Aspirate where infection is suspected** — septic olecranon bursitis is common, particularly after abrasion. **Gout also causes it**, sometimes with visible tophi. Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.3.
> **· Ulnar neuropathy at the cubital tunnel** — cross-refer [[D4]] 0.4.
> **· Distal biceps tendon rupture** — a sudden pop on eccentric loading, weakness of supination, a "reverse Popeye" with the muscle belly retracted proximally, and a palpable gap. **Distal rupture requires PROMPT surgical referral for repair** — unlike proximal long head rupture, which is managed conservatively. **This distinction matters and the window is short.**

> [!warning] The posterior fat pad sign is always pathological
> **On a lateral elbow radiograph, a visible POSTERIOR fat pad indicates a joint effusion — and in the context of trauma, that means an occult fracture until proven otherwise**, most often a **radial head fracture** in adults or a **supracondylar fracture** in children.
> **An anterior fat pad can be normal; an enlarged "sail-shaped" anterior fat pad is also abnormal.**
> **A patient with elbow trauma, a posterior fat pad, and no visible fracture is treated as having a fracture** — immobilised and re-imaged. Cross-refer [[L7]].

> [!tip] Pulled elbow in a toddler
> **Radial head subluxation from a sudden longitudinal pull on a pronated, extended arm** — lifting a child by the hand, or swinging them.
> **The child holds the arm slightly flexed and pronated and refuses to use it, with no swelling and no bony tenderness.**
> **Radiographs are not required if the history and examination are typical**, and reduction is a simple manoeuvre with immediate return of function. Cross-refer [[M5]].

**Ix:** Radiographs including a true lateral (*why:* fat pad sign and occult fracture; *what:* effusion, fracture). Ultrasound (*why:* tendon and bursal assessment, and guides aspiration; *what:* tendinopathy, tear, collection). Aspiration of the bursa (*why:* distinguishes septic from inflammatory bursitis; *what:* cell count, Gram stain, culture, crystals).

## 0.4 Hip

> [!danger] Where the pain is tells you what it is
> **· GROIN pain (± referral to the anterior thigh and knee) → the TRUE HIP JOINT.**
> **· LATERAL pain over the greater trochanter → GREATER TROCHANTERIC PAIN SYNDROME (gluteal tendinopathy), not the hip joint.**
> **· BUTTOCK pain → the LUMBAR SPINE or SACROILIAC JOINT.**
> **Patients say "hip" for all three, and pointing to where it hurts resolves it immediately.** **The "C-sign" — the patient cupping the hand around the lateral hip and groin — indicates true intra-articular pain.**

> [!tip] Hip osteoarthritis
> **Groin pain worse on weight bearing and at the end of the day**, with morning stiffness under 30 minutes, difficulty with socks and shoes, and reduced walking distance.
> **The earliest and most reliable examination finding is loss of INTERNAL ROTATION**, tested with the hip flexed.
> Management: **exercise, weight management, analgesia, walking aids, and joint replacement for refractory disease with concordant imaging.**

> [!warning] Greater trochanteric pain syndrome is gluteal tendinopathy, not bursitis
> **Lateral hip pain, tender directly over the greater trochanter, worse lying on that side at night, worse crossing the legs and climbing stairs.**
> **The old label "trochanteric bursitis" is inaccurate — the pathology is predominantly gluteus medius and minimus TENDINOPATHY**, which matters because it means **the treatment is load management and progressive abductor strengthening, not repeated injection.**
> **Trendelenburg sign** and **single-leg stance test** support it.

> [!danger] Avascular necrosis — the radiograph is normal early
> **Groin pain, often insidious, with risk factors: CORTICOSTEROIDS (the commonest), alcohol, sickle cell disease, trauma, SLE and antiphospholipid syndrome, radiotherapy, and dysbarism.**
> **Plain radiographs are NORMAL in the early stages, and by the time they show collapse the joint is often unsalvageable.**
> **MRI is the investigation, and it should be requested on clinical suspicion in an at-risk patient with a normal radiograph** — early intervention can preserve the joint.

> [!danger] Occult hip fracture — a normal radiograph does not exclude it
> **An older person with hip or groin pain after a fall, WHO MAY STILL BE ABLE TO WEIGHT BEAR, can have an undisplaced or impacted neck of femur fracture with normal or equivocal plain films.**
> **If the pain and the mechanism fit and the patient cannot mobilise normally, get further imaging — CT or MRI — rather than discharging them.**
> **Discharged occult hip fractures displace, and the outcome is far worse.** Cross-refer [[L7]] and [[GER1]] 0.5.

> [!tip] The rest of the hip
> **· Femoroacetabular impingement and labral tear** — younger, active patients; groin pain with flexion and rotation, clicking or catching; **FADIR test.** MRI arthrogram.
> **· Referred from the lumbar spine** — cross-refer [[L4]].
> **· Psoas abscess** — fever, back and groin pain, hip held flexed, pain on extension. Cross-refer [[C7]] 0.5.
> **· Inguinal hernia, adductor-related groin pain, pubic symphysis pathology, and — in women — gynaecological causes.**
> **· Paediatric: developmental dysplasia, Perthes disease, slipped upper femoral epiphysis, transient synovitis and septic arthritis.** **SUFE — an adolescent, often overweight, with knee or thigh pain, loss of internal rotation and OBLIGATE EXTERNAL ROTATION ON FLEXION — is an urgent, non-weight-bearing referral.** Cross-refer [[M5]].

**Ix:** **Radiographs — AP pelvis AND a lateral of the affected hip** (*why:* a single AP misses subtle femoral neck and SUFE changes, and the pelvis view allows comparison; *what:* fracture, arthritis, slip). **MRI where the radiograph is normal but suspicion persists** (*why:* occult fracture, avascular necrosis, labral pathology and stress fracture are all radiographically silent early; *what:* marrow oedema, fracture line, necrosis). CT (*why:* alternative for occult fracture and for surgical planning; *what:* fracture). Inflammatory markers and aspiration where infection is suspected (*why:* septic arthritis; *what:* infection).

## 0.5 Knee

> [!danger] Examine the hip in every patient with knee pain
> **Repeated because it is the commonest missed diagnosis in this region.** Hip pathology — osteoarthritis in adults, SUFE and Perthes in children — refers to the knee, and the knee examination is normal.

> [!tip] Osteoarthritis, and the evidence about arthroscopy
> Activity-related pain, brief morning stiffness, crepitus, effusion, and progressive varus deformity.
> **Exercise therapy and weight loss are first-line and genuinely effective** — a modest weight reduction produces a large reduction in knee load.
> **ARTHROSCOPIC surgery for degenerative meniscal tears and for knee osteoarthritis is NOT indicated** — multiple high-quality trials show no benefit over placebo or conservative care, and it remains one of the most consistently over-performed operations. **A degenerative meniscal tear on MRI in a middle-aged or older patient is usually part of the osteoarthritic process, not a separate surgical problem.**

> [!warning] Acute knee injury — what the effusion timing tells you
> **· Immediate, tense swelling within a few hours → HAEMARTHROSIS → ACL rupture, intra-articular fracture, patellar dislocation, or peripheral meniscal tear.** Cross-refer [[L1]] 0.4.
> **· Swelling developing over 12–24 hours → a reactive effusion → meniscal injury more likely.**
> **· No swelling → less likely a significant intra-articular injury.**
> **Ask specifically how quickly it swelled** — it is one of the most useful questions in knee trauma.

> [!tip] The specific injuries
> **· ACL rupture** — a pivoting or deceleration mechanism, an audible "pop", immediate haemarthrosis, and subsequent giving way. **The LACHMAN test is more sensitive than the anterior drawer.** MRI confirms.
> **· Meniscal tear** — twisting on a loaded flexed knee, with joint line tenderness, catching, and **locking (a true mechanical block to full extension)** in a bucket-handle tear, which is a surgical indication.
> **· Collateral ligament injury** — valgus or varus stress with localised tenderness.
> **· Patellar dislocation** — almost always lateral, often reduces spontaneously with knee extension, with a positive apprehension test afterwards.
> **· Patellofemoral pain** — anterior knee pain in younger people, worse on stairs and after prolonged sitting (the "theatre sign"). **Managed with exercise-based rehabilitation**, not imaging.

> [!danger] Extensor mechanism rupture — test the straight leg raise
> **Quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture presents with sudden pain, inability to extend the knee against gravity, and a palpable gap above or below the patella.**
> **The test is a STRAIGHT LEG RAISE — the patient cannot perform one, or cannot maintain the extended knee against gravity.**
> **It is missed because the knee is swollen and painful and the examination is abbreviated, and because the patient may still be able to walk with the knee locked.**
> **It requires surgical repair, and delay worsens the outcome.** **Test the straight leg raise in every acute knee injury.**

> [!tip] The rest
> **· Prepatellar and infrapatellar bursitis** — anterior swelling with a mobile knee joint. **Septic prepatellar bursitis is common** after abrasion or kneeling occupations.
> **· Baker cyst** — posterior knee swelling. **A ruptured Baker cyst causes acute calf pain, swelling and sometimes bruising, and mimics DVT closely ("pseudothrombophlebitis")** — and the two coexist, so **imaging is required rather than assuming.** Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.3.
> **· Osgood-Schlatter and Sinding-Larsen** in adolescents.
> **· Osteosarcoma — night pain and rest pain around the knee in an adolescent or young adult.** Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.8.
> **The Ottawa knee rules** identify who needs a radiograph after acute injury. `UNVERIFIED — the criteria.`

**Ix:** **Straight leg raise, and assessment of the timing of the effusion** (*why:* the two highest-yield bedside findings; *what:* extensor mechanism integrity, haemarthrosis). Radiographs per decision rule (*why:* fracture; *what:* fracture, lipohaemarthrosis, degenerative change). **MRI** (*why:* ligamentous, meniscal and osteochondral injury, which radiographs cannot show — **but not indicated for degenerative change in an older patient where it will not change management**; *what:* soft tissue injury). Ultrasound (*why:* Baker cyst, bursitis, tendon rupture, and guides aspiration; *what:* fluid collection, tendon gap). Aspiration where infection or crystal disease is possible (*why:* septic arthritis; *what:* cell count, culture, crystals — cross-refer [[L1]] 0.6).

## 0.6 Wrist and Hand

> [!danger] Scaphoid fracture — the radiograph is normal in a significant proportion
> **A fall on the outstretched hand with pain in the radial wrist.**
> **Examination: tenderness in the ANATOMICAL SNUFFBOX, tenderness over the SCAPHOID TUBERCLE, and pain on AXIAL COMPRESSION of the thumb.**
> **INITIAL RADIOGRAPHS ARE NORMAL IN A SIGNIFICANT PROPORTION OF SCAPHOID FRACTURES**, even with dedicated scaphoid views.
> **So: if the clinical findings fit, IMMOBILISE and either re-image after an interval or obtain MRI or CT** — do not discharge on the strength of a normal film.
> **The consequence of missing it is real: the scaphoid's blood supply enters distally and runs retrograde, so a proximal fracture risks AVASCULAR NECROSIS and NON-UNION**, leading to wrist collapse and arthritis in a young person. **This is one of the classic medicolegal misses.**

> [!warning] The other fractures and injuries not to miss
> **· Distal radius fracture (Colles, Smith)** — **check MEDIAN nerve function before and after reduction**, and be alert to acute carpal tunnel syndrome from swelling.
> **· Boxer's fracture (fifth metacarpal neck)** — and **any wound over the MCP joints from a punch is a FIGHT BITE until proven otherwise**, requiring exploration, washout and antibiotics. Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.5 and [[K2]] 0.5.
> **· Mallet finger** — loss of active DIP extension after forced flexion; splinting in extension continuously for weeks.
> **· Jersey finger** — flexor digitorum profundus avulsion; inability to flex the DIP; needs surgical repair.
> **· Gamekeeper's / skier's thumb** — ulnar collateral ligament injury at the thumb MCP from forced abduction. **A complete tear with a Stener lesion will not heal without surgery**, so instability on examination warrants referral.

> [!danger] Hand infections are surgical emergencies more often than they appear
> **Flexor tenosynovitis — Kanavel signs: fusiform digit swelling, held in flexion, tenderness along the flexor sheath, pain on passive extension.** Urgent hand surgical drainage. Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.3.
> **Felon, paronychia, and any deep hand infection** — the hand has closed compartments and functionally critical structures, and **the threshold for referral should be low.**

> [!tip] The common non-traumatic conditions
> **· Carpal tunnel syndrome** — cross-refer [[D4]] 0.4, including the point that **sensation over the thenar eminence is preserved.**
> **· De Quervain tenosynovitis** — pain over the radial styloid, **Finkelstein test positive**; common in new mothers (lifting an infant) and with repetitive thumb use.
> **· Trigger finger** — catching or locking with a palpable nodule at the A1 pulley. **Strongly associated with diabetes.**
> **· Dupuytren contracture** — palmar cords with progressive MCP and PIP flexion contracture. **Associated with diabetes, alcohol, family history, and antiepileptic drugs.** The **tabletop test** assesses severity and guides referral timing.
> **· First carpometacarpal (thumb base) osteoarthritis** — very common, particularly in postmenopausal women, with pain on pinch and grip and a positive **grind test.** Splinting, activity modification and injection help.
> **· Ganglion** — a smooth, transilluminable swelling, usually benign, often resolving spontaneously.

**Ix:** **Scaphoid views and, if normal with concordant clinical findings, MRI or delayed re-imaging** (*why:* the fracture is radiographically occult in a significant proportion and the consequences of missing it are severe; *what:* fracture line, marrow oedema). Radiographs for other suspected fractures (*why:* fracture and alignment; *what:* fracture). **Documented neurovascular examination before and after any reduction** (*why:* median nerve injury and compartment problems, and the documentation matters medicolegally; *what:* sensation, motor function, perfusion). Ultrasound (*why:* tendon and ganglion assessment; *what:* tenosynovitis, cyst). HbA1c (*why:* diabetes underlies trigger finger, Dupuytren contracture and adhesive capsulitis; *what:* hyperglycaemia).

## 0.7 Ankle and Foot

> [!danger] Achilles rupture — the ability to plantarflex does NOT exclude it
> **The classic error.** After a complete Achilles rupture, **the patient can still plantarflex the ankle using tibialis posterior, the peroneals and the toe flexors** — so "can you point your foot down?" is a useless test and produces false reassurance.
> **The tests that work:**
> **· SIMMONDS (Thompson) calf squeeze test** — with the patient prone and feet over the end of the couch, squeezing the calf produces plantarflexion if the tendon is intact and NO movement if it is ruptured. **This is the test.**
> **· A palpable gap in the tendon.**
> **· Loss of the normal resting tension asymmetry between the two feet.**
> **History: a sudden sensation of being struck or kicked in the back of the ankle, often with an audible snap, during push-off.**
> **Risk factors worth knowing: FLUOROQUINOLONE antibiotics and CORTICOSTEROIDS — both systemic and injected — predispose to tendon rupture**, and this is a genuine and avoidable prescribing consideration in patients with Achilles symptoms.

> [!danger] Always examine the proximal fibula in an ankle injury
> **A MAISONNEUVE fracture is a proximal fibular fracture accompanying a syndesmotic and medial ankle injury.**
> **If you only radiograph the ankle, you miss it** — and the injury is unstable and requires fixation.
> **Palpate the whole length of the fibula in every significant ankle injury**, and image the proximal fibula if it is tender.

> [!tip] Ankle sprain and the decision rules
> **Lateral ligament sprain — most commonly the anterior talofibular ligament — from an inversion injury.**
> **The Ottawa ankle and foot rules** identify who requires radiography, based on bony tenderness at specific sites and the ability to weight bear. `UNVERIFIED — the criteria.`
> Management: **early mobilisation and functional rehabilitation, not prolonged immobilisation** — with proprioceptive retraining to reduce recurrence.

> [!tip] The common foot conditions
> **· Plantar heel pain (plantar fasciitis)** — **pain on the first steps in the morning and after periods of rest**, easing with walking then worsening with prolonged activity; tenderness at the medial calcaneal tubercle. **Self-limiting over months to a couple of years.** Managed with **stretching, load management, footwear and orthoses**; injection is second-line and carries a risk of fat pad atrophy and fascial rupture.
> **· Morton neuroma** — burning pain and numbness between the toes, most often the third web space, with a **Mulder click.**
> **· Hallux valgus and hallux rigidus.**
> **· Gout at the first MTP** — cross-refer [[L1]] 0.3.
> **· Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction** — progressive adult acquired flatfoot, with the **"too many toes" sign** from behind and inability to perform a single-heel raise.
> **· Tarsal tunnel syndrome** — burning sole pain from posterior tibial nerve entrapment.

> [!warning] Stress fracture — the radiograph is normal for weeks
> **Insidious activity-related bone pain with focal bony tenderness, in someone who has recently increased training load, changed surface or footwear, or is under-fuelled.**
> **Common sites: metatarsals (the "march" fracture), tibia, navicular, calcaneus and femoral neck.**
> **Radiographs are typically NORMAL for the first weeks — MRI is the investigation.**
> **Consider relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S) and the associated menstrual and bone health consequences**, particularly in female athletes and dancers — **and ask about eating patterns, menstrual history and training load.** Cross-refer [[N8]] and [[GER1]] 0.6.
> **Femoral neck and navicular stress fractures are high-risk and can progress to complete fracture** — they need non-weight-bearing and urgent referral, not a return to training.

> [!warning] The diabetic foot
> **Charcot neuroarthropathy — a hot, swollen, often painless foot in a neuropathic diabetic — is misdiagnosed as cellulitis or gout while the patient keeps walking on it and destroys the architecture of the foot.**
> **Immediate offloading and non-weight-bearing.** Cross-refer [[I2]] 0.6 and [[D4]] 0.2.

**Ix:** **Simmonds calf squeeze test** (*why:* the test that actually detects Achilles rupture; *what:* absent plantarflexion). **Palpation of the entire fibula** (*why:* Maisonneuve fracture; *what:* proximal tenderness). Radiographs per decision rule, **including the proximal fibula where indicated** (*why:* fracture and syndesmotic injury; *what:* fracture, widened mortise). Ultrasound (*why:* Achilles integrity, plantar fascia, and it is dynamic and accessible; *what:* tendon gap, thickening). **MRI where stress fracture is suspected and radiographs are normal** (*why:* radiographs are normal for weeks and continuing to load a high-risk site risks complete fracture; *what:* marrow oedema, fracture line). Assessment for RED-S where a stress fracture occurs in an athlete (*why:* it recurs unless the energy availability and hormonal issues are addressed; *what:* menstrual history, energy intake, bone density).

## Shoulder

### Biceps Brachii Tendon Rupture

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Ruptured Biceps Brachii Head," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite the closely related rotator cuff and shoulder pathology throughout this file; placed under Shoulder given the large majority of ruptures involve the proximal tendon, though the distal presentation (at the elbow) is covered here too given the two share the same underlying muscle and are naturally taught together.

- **D:** rupture of one of the biceps brachii's tendons — the **proximal long head accounts for ~96% of ruptures**, the distal tendon ~3%, and the proximal short head ~1%, making "biceps rupture" essentially synonymous with the long head proximal presentation in the great majority of cases, though the distal variant is disproportionately important clinically given it's the one that generally needs surgery.
- **R:** middle-aged men most typically; tendinopathy, anabolic steroid use, or a pre-existing rotator cuff tear predispose (given a proximal rupture often occurs on a background of pre-existing tendon degeneration rather than in a previously healthy tendon) — when associated with tendinopathy, only minimal additional force may be needed to complete the rupture. Mechanisms include heavy lifting and traumatic falls.
- **A/P:** when a biceps tendon ruptures, the muscle belly — no longer held under tension at both its attachments — retracts toward its remaining intact attachment, producing the visible/palpable deformity below.

> [!tip] Proximal vs distal — genuinely different presentations, exam findings, and management, not two versions of the same injury:

**Proximal (long head) rupture — the common presentation:**
- **S/Smx:** acute pain, swelling, and bruising in the upper arm/shoulder; patients often describe an audible or felt "pop"; the classic **"Popeye sign"** — the muscle belly balls up and retracts distally toward the elbow, producing a visible bulge in the lower arm (may not be obvious in obese patients, or if scarring elsewhere limits retraction). Acute elbow flexion strength loss of roughly 30% has been described, but **there is usually little to no appreciable chronic weakness** — the brachialis and other elbow flexors substantially compensate over time, which is part of why proximal rupture is often managed conservatively (see Mx below).
- Given the strong association with rotator cuff pathology, **specifically examine for concurrent rotator cuff tear and shoulder impingement** — a proximal biceps rupture should prompt a full shoulder assessment, not just confirmation of the biceps finding in isolation.
- **Mx:** generally **conservative** in most patients, particularly older/lower-demand individuals, given the good functional compensation described above; surgical repair (tenodesis) reserved for younger, higher-demand patients or those bothered by the cosmetic deformity or persistent cramping/fatigue.

**Distal rupture — less common but disproportionately important, given surgical urgency:**
- **S/Smx:** bruising and swelling specifically in the antecubital fossa (front of the elbow); painful popping sound/sensation at the time of injury; **reverse Popeye sign** — the muscle belly retracts proximally (up toward the shoulder) rather than distally, the opposite direction to a proximal rupture, given the distal (not proximal) attachment has failed; weakness of elbow flexion and, more specifically and diagnostically, **weakness of forearm supination** (reported reductions of up to ~40% flexion and ~50% supination strength) — supination weakness is a genuinely useful distinguishing clinical clue given the biceps is the primary forearm supinator.
- **The hook test** — the standard, most sensitive and specific bedside test for distal rupture: with the elbow flexed to 90° and the forearm supinated, the examiner attempts to hook a finger from lateral to medial around the biceps tendon in the antecubital fossa — an intact tendon can be hooked; its absence (an "empty" hook, no palpable cord to catch) supports a complete distal rupture. Comparing with the uninjured contralateral side improves confidence in an equivocal exam.
- **Mx:** **distal ruptures are generally treated surgically**, given non-operative management results in a disproportionate loss of supination and flexion strength compared to the proximal presentation — this is the key practical distinction from proximal rupture, where conservative management is usually adequate. **Early referral matters** given surgical repair is time-sensitive — ideally within about 2 weeks of injury, since the retracted tendon becomes progressively harder to mobilise and repair back to its anatomical insertion the longer repair is delayed.

**Ix:** primarily a clinical diagnosis for a straightforward, complete rupture with a clear Popeye/reverse-Popeye deformity — imaging (ultrasound or MRI) is useful where the diagnosis is unclear, a partial rather than complete tear is suspected, or for surgical planning in a distal rupture being referred for repair.

### Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)

- **D:** chronic fibrosing condition causing slowly progressing restriction of the shoulder's range of movement.
- **R:** age 40–70, diabetes, prior history of adhesive capsulitis, previous shoulder surgery, conditions that predispose to shoulder immobility.
- **S/Smx:** external rotation affected more than internal rotation or abduction; both active and passive movement affected; 3 phases — freezing, adhesive, recovery; bilateral in 20%; episode typically lasts 6–24 months.
- **Dx:** clinical ± imaging (e.g. MRI).
- **Mx:** NSAIDs, physiotherapy, oral corticosteroids and intra-articular steroids.

### Rotator cuff injury

- **D:** spectrum of injuries including tendinopathy, partial and full tears — subacromial impingement, calcific tendonitis, rotator cuff tears, rotator cuff arthropathy.
- **R:** >60 years old.
- **A:** acute trauma, or repetitive ± vigorous overhead activity, or age-related degeneration.
- **P:** trauma + poor blood supply (and therefore lack of healing) → chronic injury.
- **S/Smx:** shoulder pain worse on abduction; painful arc of abduction (typically 60° to 120°); pain over the anterior acromion.
- **Ix:** XR, US/MRI.
- **Mx:** surgical if significant impact on ADLs and fit for surgery. Others: conservative (NSAIDs, physiotherapy), arthroscopic debridement.

### Acromioclavicular joint injury

> [!note] Gap-filled — this entry had the grading but lacked S/Smx and Ix, both genuinely important given the classification depends on distinguishing exam and imaging findings, not the mechanism alone.

- **A:** trauma, usually due to a fall directly onto the shoulder (adducted arm) or a fall on an outstretched hand; common in contact/collision sports.
- **S/Smx:** pain and tenderness localised over the AC joint (the point where the clavicle meets the acromion, palpable at the top-outer shoulder); pain worse with overhead movement or moving the arm across the body; **a "step deformity"** (a visible/palpable bump where the distal clavicle sits proud relative to the acromion) may be present and becomes more pronounced with more severe injury — examine with the patient standing or sitting, since gravity on the arm accentuates the deformity.
- **Ix:** plain X-ray (AP view) is generally adequate for diagnosis and to exclude other traumatic shoulder pathology, though milder AC injuries aren't always clearly visible on standard views; comparison with the contralateral shoulder view can help assess the coracoclavicular (CC) interspace, which is what the Rockwood grades are actually measured against; ultrasound or MRI reserved for continued diagnostic uncertainty.
- **Graded I to VI** (Rockwood classification) — genuinely worth knowing the grading isn't a simple linear severity scale despite the numbering, since it's based on which structures are torn (AC ligament, CC ligament, deltotrapezial fascia) and the degree/direction of clavicular displacement, not injury severity alone:
  - **I:** AC ligament sprain only, CC ligament intact, no radiographic displacement.
  - **II:** AC ligament torn, CC ligament sprained, mild displacement.
  - **III:** both AC and CC ligaments torn, clavicle displaced above the acromion's superior border.
  - **IV:** as III, with posterior displacement of the clavicle into or through the trapezius.
  - **V:** as III, with much greater (>100%) displacement and detachment of the deltotrapezial fascia.
  - **VI:** rare — inferior dislocation of the clavicle to a subacromial or subcoracoid position.
- **Mx:** Grade I–III — conservative, sling, analgesia, early physiotherapy. Grade IV–VI — surgical fixation, given the degree of instability and soft tissue disruption involved.

### Shoulder dislocation

- **D:** dislocation of the humeral head from the glenoid cavity of the scapula (accounts for 50% of all major joint dislocations).
- **Anterior (>95% of cases):** arm is usually abducted and externally rotated + prominent acromion.
- **Posterior (2–4%):** arm is in adduction and internal rotation + patient cannot rotate externally.
- **S/Smx:** as above; check for neurovascular compromise (e.g. axillary nerve injury); rule out associated fractures.
- **Mx:** reduction ± sedation. Can generally be done in the ED, with some contraindications (e.g. humeral fracture, arterial injury, etc).

### Humeral fractures

- **Proximal humeral fractures:** usually seen in older patients with osteoporosis after a fall on an outstretched hand; associated with axillary nerve injury.
- **Mid-shaft fractures:** associated with radial nerve injury (both due to the injury itself and secondary to surgical manipulation) — 85–90% improve over 3 months of observation. Radial nerve injury presents as wrist drop.

### Supracondylar fracture of the humerus

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Supracondylar Fracture of humerus," High yield) — genuinely absent from the source material despite being one of the most classically-taught, genuinely dangerous fractures in orthopaedics, particularly in children.

- **D:** a fracture of the distal humerus just above the condyles — the most common elbow fracture in children (typically age 5–7), though it occurs in adults too.
- **A/P:** classically a fall onto an outstretched hand with the elbow hyperextended (extension-type, ~95% of paediatric cases) — the distal fragment displaces posteriorly; flexion-type (rarer) results from a direct fall onto a flexed elbow, with anterior displacement.
- **S/Smx:** elbow pain and swelling following a fall, reluctance to move the elbow, visible deformity if significantly displaced.
- **Ix:** XR (AP and lateral elbow) (*why:* confirms the fracture and its displacement pattern, directly informing the Gartland classification and Mx below; *what:* the fracture line above the condyles ± an abnormal "fat pad sign" — a posterior fat pad visible on the lateral view is abnormal and suggests an occult fracture even when the fracture line itself isn't clearly visible, given elevated intra-articular pressure from a haemarthrosis displaces the fat pad; an anterior "sail sign" can be a similar subtle clue). **Neurovascular assessment is genuinely the most important part of the initial evaluation, not an afterthought** — assess and document the radial pulse and capillary refill, and specifically test the three main nerves at risk: **median nerve** (anterior interosseous branch specifically — test by asking the patient to make an "OK sign"/pinch the thumb and index finger tip-to-tip, given this tests the specific muscles the AIN supplies), **radial nerve** (wrist/finger extension), and **ulnar nerve** (finger abduction/adduction) — the specific nerve at risk varies with fracture displacement direction, but all three should be assessed and clearly documented given the medicolegal and clinical importance of a baseline exam before any reduction attempt.

> [!info] Gartland classification (extension-type, the more common pattern)
> - **Type I:** undisplaced.
> - **Type II:** displaced with intact posterior cortex (hinged).
> - **Type III:** completely displaced, no cortical contact.

> [!danger] The genuinely dangerous complication to actively watch for is **compartment syndrome of the forearm, and specifically brachial artery injury/compromise** — a significantly displaced supracondylar fracture can kink or injure the brachial artery, given its close anatomical proximity, risking **Volkmann's ischaemic contracture** (a permanent, severely disabling flexion contracture of the forearm/hand from muscle ischaemia and subsequent fibrosis) if not recognised and treated promptly — this is exactly the same "6 Ps" compartment syndrome vigilance principle from [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Compartment syndrome, not repeated here, but specifically relevant and actively screened for in this fracture given how commonly the brachial artery is affected.
- **Mx:** **Type I:** immobilisation in a cast/splint, typically without surgery. **Type II and III (displaced):** closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP) under general anaesthesia is the standard treatment for displaced fractures, given the need for accurate reduction to avoid the neurovascular complications above and to prevent malunion (cubitus varus — the classic "gunstock deformity" — is a recognised late complication of inadequately reduced fractures). **Any evidence of vascular compromise (absent/diminished pulse, poor capillary refill) after closed reduction warrants urgent vascular surgical review and possible exploration** — a genuinely time-critical escalation given the ischaemic contracture risk above, not something to observe and reassess later.

### Biceps (tendon) rupture

- **D:** separation of the long or short tendon of the biceps from its attachment site, or a full-width tear of one of these tendons.
- **R:** M>F (3:1), increasing age.
- **A:** heavy overhead activities, shoulder overuse, smoking, corticosteroids.
- **S/Smx:** sudden pop or tear at the shoulder → pain, bruising, swelling; weakness in the shoulder; "Popeye" deformity — bulge of muscle in the middle of the upper arm.
- **Ix:** exam + MRI.
- **Mx:** RICE + NSAIDs; conservative, especially if elderly; surgery.

### Shoulder labral tear (including SLAP lesion)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Labrum at shoulder INJURY," High yield) — genuinely absent despite the hip labral tear equivalent being covered in [[11_04_Ortho_-_Hip]] Acetabular labral tear (hip labral tear), and despite this injury's direct anatomical relationship to the biceps tendon pathology immediately above (the long head of biceps anchors directly onto the superior labrum, which is exactly why the two conditions are so often discussed together and can coexist).

- **D:** a tear of the glenoid labrum (the fibrocartilaginous rim deepening the shoulder socket) — **SLAP lesion** (Superior Labrum, Anterior to Posterior) specifically refers to a tear involving the superior labrum at the site of the long head of biceps tendon anchor, the most commonly discussed labral tear pattern given its direct link to the biceps pathology above; other labral tear patterns (e.g. a Bankart lesion — anteroinferior labral tear associated with anterior shoulder dislocation, see Shoulder dislocation above) occur at different sites with different mechanisms.
- **A/P:** repetitive overhead activity (throwing athletes — a classically over-represented group given the repetitive biceps traction on the superior labrum), acute traumatic injury (a fall onto an outstretched arm, or a sudden traction/pull on the arm), or as part of the same continuum as anterior shoulder instability/dislocation for non-SLAP labral tears.
- **S/Smx:** deep, poorly-localised shoulder pain — often vague and hard for the patient to pinpoint, unlike the more clearly localised pain of rotator cuff pathology above, which is itself a useful (if imperfect) distinguishing clue; mechanical symptoms (catching, popping, or a sensation of instability/the shoulder "giving way") during overhead or rotational movement; pain classically worse with overhead activity; may coexist with biceps tendon symptoms given the shared anatomical attachment above.
- **Ix:** clinical examination — several provocative tests exist (e.g. O'Brien's active compression test, Speed's test) though **no single clinical test has both high sensitivity and specificity for labral tears**, meaning a negative test doesn't reliably exclude the diagnosis and clinical suspicion should still prompt imaging if the history is compatible; MRI, ideally **MR arthrography** (contrast injected into the joint before MRI) given this significantly improves sensitivity for labral tears over standard MRI alone, which can under-detect subtle tears — the imaging modality choice matters more here than for many other shoulder pathologies given plain MRI's comparatively limited sensitivity for this specific structure.
- **Mx:** conservative management first-line for many cases — activity modification, physiotherapy (strengthening the surrounding rotator cuff and scapular stabilisers to reduce mechanical load on the labrum), NSAIDs; arthroscopic surgical repair is considered for persistent symptomatic tears not responding to an adequate conservative trial, particularly in younger, high-demand athletes where mechanical symptoms significantly limit function — the specific surgical technique and rehabilitation timeline depends on tear pattern and biceps tendon involvement, given the direct anatomical relationship to the biceps pathology above.

## Elbow

### Cubital tunnel syndrome

- **D:** compression of the ulnar nerve (C8 & T1) as it passes through the cubital tunnel.
- **S/Smx:** numbness, tingling ± pain of the 4th and 5th finger — intermittent then constant; ± weakness and muscle wasting; pain worse when leaning on the elbow; ± history of OA or trauma to the area.
- **Ix:** clinical diagnosis ± nerve conduction studies.
- **Mx:** avoid aggravating motions; physiotherapy, steroid injections, surgery.

### Radial tunnel syndrome

- **D:** compressive neuropathy of the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) at the level of the proximal forearm.
- **S/Smx:** pain 4–5cm distal to the lateral epicondyle, ± worse on extending the elbow and pronating the forearm.
- **Ix:** primarily a clinical diagnosis — a genuinely important, commonly-tested point is that this condition **frequently mimics or coexists with lateral epicondylitis** (tennis elbow, below), with the key distinguishing feature being the point of maximal tenderness (further distal, over the radial tunnel itself, rather than directly at the lateral epicondyle) — this overlap means radial tunnel syndrome is often under-recognised or misdiagnosed as refractory tennis elbow. Nerve conduction studies can support the diagnosis in ambiguous cases, though they're often normal even in genuine radial tunnel syndrome given the compression is often intermittent/positional rather than a fixed lesion.
- **Mx:** conservative measures first-line — activity modification, splinting, physiotherapy, NSAIDs; corticosteroid injection may be trialled; surgical decompression of the radial tunnel is considered for refractory cases, particularly given how easily this is misdiagnosed as treatment-resistant tennis elbow, making an accurate diagnosis (rather than repeated tennis-elbow-directed treatment) the genuinely important step before considering surgery.

### Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)

- **D:** chronic tendinosis of the wrist flexors and pronators that attach to the medial epicondyle.
- **S/Smx:** pain at the medial epicondyle, worse on wrist flexion and pronation ("door knock" movement); ± numbness, tingling in the 4th and 5th finger due to ulnar nerve involvement.
- **Ix:** clinical diagnosis, based on the characteristic pain location and provocation pattern above — imaging (US or MRI) is reserved for atypical presentations or where the diagnosis is unclear, given it's not typically needed to confirm a straightforward case.
- **Mx:** the same general conservative-first approach as lateral epicondylitis below (rest, ice, physiotherapy, NSAIDs) — not repeated in full here given the identical underlying principle (tendinosis, not true inflammation, responding best to load management and time rather than aggressive anti-inflammatory treatment alone); corticosteroid injection can be considered for persistent symptoms, though evidence for durable benefit is mixed and it doesn't address the underlying tendinosis; surgical release is reserved for genuinely refractory cases after an adequate trial of conservative management (typically 6–12 months).

### Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow)

- **D:** tendinosis of the common extensor tendons.
- **S/Smx:** pain when extending the wrist and elbow against resistance; acute pain lasting 6–12 weeks, with a chronic phase between 6 months and 2 years.
- **Mx:** rest, ice, physiotherapy, NSAIDs.

### Olecranon bursitis

- **D:** swelling over the posterior aspect of the elbow. Typically in middle-aged males.
- **S/Smx:** associated with pain, warmth, redness.
- **Ix:** primarily clinical, but **the key diagnostic task is distinguishing simple/aseptic bursitis from septic bursitis**, given the management differs substantially — aspiration of bursal fluid for microscopy, Gram stain, and culture is indicated if there's any suspicion of infection (marked warmth/erythema extending beyond the bursa itself, fever, or a break in the overlying skin) — see [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Septic arthritis for the general principle of joint/periarticular sepsis assessment this parallels, not repeated here (though septic bursitis is a distinct entity from septic arthritis itself, given the bursa doesn't communicate with the joint space).
- **Mx:** **aseptic bursitis:** conservative — rest, ice, avoiding pressure/repeated trauma to the area (e.g. elbow padding), NSAIDs; aspiration can be therapeutic for large, symptomatic effusions even when aseptic, though recurrence is common without addressing the underlying repetitive trauma. **Septic bursitis:** antibiotics covering the likely organism (commonly *S. aureus*, similar to the septic arthritis aetiology in [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]]) ± aspiration/drainage, with the same general "don't delay treatment for a hot, swollen periarticular structure" urgency principle as septic arthritis, though the specific anatomical distinction (bursa vs joint) matters for both prognosis and the extent of intervention needed.

### Pulled elbow

- **D:** subluxation of the radial head. Most common in children <6 years old.
- **S/Smx:** elbow pain, limited supination and extension of the elbow.
- **Mx:** analgesia + passive supination of the elbow joint while the elbow is flexed to 90°.

## Distal radius / forearm fractures

### Colles' fracture

- Classically follows a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH).
- Distal radius fracture with dorsal displacement of fragments.
- **Classical features:** transverse fracture of the radius, 1 inch proximal to the radiocarpal joint, dorsal displacement and angulation.
- **Ix:** XR (AP and lateral wrist) (*why:* confirms the fracture and its displacement/angulation, directly guiding the reduction-vs-surgery decision below; *what:* the classic dorsally-displaced/angulated distal radius fracture pattern described above, sometimes described as a "dinner fork" deformity clinically before imaging). Assess and document neurovascular status specifically (*why:* median nerve injury is a recognised early complication, per the box below, so a baseline exam matters both for immediate management and for medicolegal documentation if a deficit is found later, particularly after any reduction attempt; *what:* screens for the acute carpal tunnel syndrome pattern noted below).
- **Mx:** closed reduction under appropriate analgesia/anaesthesia (e.g. haematoma block, Bier's block, or procedural sedation) for displaced fractures, followed by immobilisation in a below-elbow backslab/cast; surgical fixation (e.g. ORIF, or K-wires) for fractures that are unstable, significantly comminuted, intra-articular, or that fail to hold reduction — see [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Management of closed fractures for the general closed-fracture principles (immobilise proximal and distal joints, monitor neurovascular status) this fits into, not repeated here.

> [!warning] Early complications
> - Median nerve injury — acute carpal tunnel syndrome presenting with weakness or loss of thumb or index finger flexion.
> - Compartment syndrome.
> - Vascular compromise.
> - Malunion.
> - Rupture of the extensor pollicis longus tendon.

> [!note] Late complications: osteoarthritis; complex regional pain syndrome.

### Smith's fracture

- **Mechanism:** falling on the dorsal (back) side of the hand when the wrist is flexed — the hand is "inward".
- Volar (palmar) angulation of the distal radius — "garden spade" deformity.
- **Ix:** XR (AP and lateral wrist), confirming the opposite (volar) displacement direction from Colles' fracture above — the same imaging principle applies, not repeated here.
- **Mx:** **genuinely more likely to require surgical fixation than Colles' fracture**, given Smith's fractures are inherently unstable (the volar displacement direction makes closed reduction harder to hold in a cast) — closed reduction and casting may be attempted for minimally displaced fractures, but ORIF is more often needed than for the equivalent Colles' fracture pattern, a useful distinguishing point between the two.

### Barton's fracture

- Distal radial fracture (Colles' or Smith's) associated with radiocarpal dislocation.
- **Mechanism:** fall onto an extended and pronated wrist.
- **Ix:** XR (AP and lateral wrist), specifically looking for the intra-articular fracture line extending to involve the radiocarpal joint with associated subluxation/dislocation — the defining feature distinguishing this from an isolated Colles'/Smith's fracture.
- **Mx:** **inherently unstable given the intra-articular and dislocation component** — almost always requires surgical fixation (ORIF) rather than closed reduction/casting alone, unlike many isolated Colles' fractures which can be managed conservatively.

### Monteggia's fracture

- Ulna fracture and dislocation of the proximal radio-ulnar joint.
- **Mechanism:** FOOSH + forced pronation.
- **Ix:** XR of the full forearm including both wrist and elbow joints (*why:* the radial head dislocation can be easily missed if imaging is limited to just the obvious ulna fracture site — a genuinely important pitfall, given the combination injury is the whole point of this fracture pattern; *what:* ulna shaft fracture with radial head dislocation, best assessed by ensuring a line drawn through the radial shaft/head passes through the capitellum on all views).
- **Mx:** surgical fixation (ORIF) of the ulna fracture is typically required in adults, with the radial head dislocation usually reducing once the ulna is anatomically fixed, given the two injuries are mechanically linked; closed reduction and casting may be sufficient in children given their greater healing/remodelling potential.

### Galeazzi fracture

- Radial shaft fracture and dislocation of the distal radio-ulnar joint.
- **Mechanism:** fall on the hand with rotational force.
- **Ix:** XR of the full forearm including both wrist and elbow joints, for the same "don't miss the dislocation" reason as Monteggia's fracture above — sometimes taught as the mirror-image counterpart to Monteggia's (radial shaft fracture + distal dislocation, vs Monteggia's ulna shaft fracture + proximal dislocation).
- **Mx:** surgical fixation (ORIF) of the radial shaft fracture is typically required in adults, again with the distal radio-ulnar joint dislocation usually reducing once the radius is anatomically fixed; closed management may be considered in children.

> [!tip] Monteggia's and Galeazzi's fractures are commonly taught as a mirror-image pair — Monteggia's = ulna shaft fracture + **proximal** radio-ulnar dislocation; Galeazzi's = radial shaft fracture + **distal** radio-ulnar dislocation — a useful memory anchor given how similar the names and injury patterns are.

## 0.1 Hand

### 0.1.1 Carpal tunnel syndrome

- **D:** compression of the median nerve in the carpal tunnel.
- **R:** age 40–60, ↑BMI, F>M, fractured wrist, diabetes, pregnancy, rheumatoid arthritis.
- **S/Smx:** pain, pins and needles in the thumb, index and middle finger — classically worse at night, patient might shake their hand to obtain relief; weakness of thumb abduction; wasting of the thenar eminence.

> [!info] Clinical tests
> [!note] **Moved to `[[Exam_04_System_File_Examination_Fragments]]` Part 2 (2026-09-01):** `- Tinel's sign — tapping the carpal tunnel causes paraesthesia.` — reproduced there verbatim under a `SOURCE:` divider naming this file.


- **Ix:** electrophysiology will show prolongation of the action potential.
- **Mx:** 6-week trial of conservative treatment (corticosteroid injection, wrist splint at night). If severe or refractory, surgical decompression (flexor retinaculum division).

### 0.1.2 De Quervain's tenosynovitis

- **D:** inflammation of the sheath containing the extensor pollicis brevis and abductor longus tendons.
- **S/Smx:** pain on the radial side of the wrist; tenderness over the radial styloid; abduction of the thumb against resistance is painful.

> [!tip] Finkelstein's test ("teapot test") positive.

- **Mx:** analgesia ± steroid injection; immobilisation with a thumb splint; surgery if refractory.

### 0.1.3 Trigger finger

- **D:** stenosing tenosynovitis of the fingers ± thumbs.
- **R:** F>M, rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes.
- **S/Smx:** more common in the thumb, middle, or ring finger. Initially stiffness, then snapping when extending a flexed digit ("trigger"); ± nodule at the base of the finger.
- **Mx:** steroid injection ± finger splint; surgery if refractory.

### 0.1.4 Scaphoid fracture

- Classically follows a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH) or contact sports.
- **S/Smx:** pain along the radial aspect of the wrist, loss of grip/pinch strength.
  - Maximal tenderness over the anatomical snuffbox — 90–95% sensitive, but poorly specific.
  - ± wrist joint effusion.
  - Pain elicited by telescoping the thumb (pain on longitudinal compression).
  - Tenderness on the scaphoid tubercle.
  - Pain on ulnar deviation of the wrist.
- **Ix:** XR (↑sensitivity after 1 week) ± MRI.
- **Mx:** immobilisation with a Futuro splint or standard below-elbow backslab; refer to orthopaedics for imaging in 7–10 days. Undisplaced fractures: cast for 6–8 weeks + imaging later on. Displaced fractures: surgical fixation. Proximal scaphoid pole fractures: surgical fixation.

> [!warning] Complications: non-union, avascular necrosis. 80% of the blood supply of the scaphoid is derived from the dorsal carpal branch (a branch of the radial artery) in a retrograde manner — disruption risks avascular necrosis of the scaphoid.

### 0.1.5 Dupuytren's contracture

- **D:** myofibroblastic disease affecting the palmar and digital fascia of the hand (most commonly the fourth and fifth fingers).
- **A:** genetic disorder (autosomal dominant), manual labour, phenytoin, alcoholic liver disease, diabetes, trauma to the hand.
- **Mx:** surgical treatment when the patient cannot flatten their hand on a table (Hueston table top test).

### 0.1.6 Boxer fracture

- **D:** minimally displaced fracture of the fifth metacarpal, usually after punching a hard surface.
- **S/Smx:** sunken knuckle.
- **Mx:** conservative — usually involving immobilisation; refer to hand surgeons.

### 0.1.7 Ganglion

- **D:** synovial cyst filled with gelatinous mucoid material.
- **S/Smx:** firm, well-circumscribed mass that transilluminates; most commonly on the dorsal aspect of the wrist; F>M (3:1).
- **Mx:** conservative — most resolve spontaneously. Surgical excision if severe symptoms or neurovascular symptoms.

## 0.2 Foot

### 0.2.1 Club foot (talipes equinovarus)

- **D:** fixed inversion and plantarflexion of the foot. Incidence 1:1000 births, 50% bilateral.
- **R:** M>F (2×), spina bifida, cerebral palsy, Edward's syndrome, oligohydramnios, arthrogryposis (congenital joint contracture).
- **Dx:** clinical — no imaging needed.
- **Mx:**
  - Ponseti method — manipulation and progressive casting soon after birth; usually results in correction after 6–10 weeks → maintain correction with night-time braces until the child is 4 years old. Also requires Achilles tenotomy in 85% of cases, which can be done under local anaesthesia.
  - Surgery, especially if the child presents late.

### 0.2.2 Metatarsal fracture

- **D:** fracture of one or more of the metatarsal bones — the proximal 5th metatarsal is most commonly fractured.
- Stress fracture: secondary to repeated mechanical stress.

**5th metatarsal fracture:**
- Proximal avulsion fracture (proximal tuberosity, Zone 1) — usually associated with a lateral ankle sprain, after inversion injuries of the ankle. **Excellent blood supply and heals reliably with conservative management** — a compressive dressing initially, then a short leg walking boot for ~2 weeks with progressive mobility, typically without needing surgery.
- Jones fracture (Zone 2, at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction) — transverse fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction. **Lies in a genuine watershed blood supply zone, giving it a significantly higher non-union/delayed union rate (as high as 15–30%) than the avulsion fracture above** — this is the single most important distinguishing point between the two fracture types, given they can look superficially similar but require genuinely different management. Non-operative management (non-weight-bearing cast for 6–8 weeks, with healing sometimes taking 10–12 weeks) is an option, particularly in less active patients, but **early intramedullary screw fixation is preferred in athletes/high-demand patients** specifically because of the non-union risk above — misclassifying a Jones fracture as a simple avulsion and treating it too conservatively is a recognised, clinically significant pitfall given the consequences (prolonged recovery, refracture) of undertreatment.

> [!tip] Distinguishing the two on X-ray: the avulsion fracture line runs roughly perpendicular to the long axis of the metatarsal (transverse to the tuberosity); the true Jones fracture line runs roughly perpendicular to the shaft at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction, more distally — this distinction, not just "proximal 5th metatarsal fracture" as a single entity, is what actually determines management.

**Metatarsal stress fractures:**
- Usually in otherwise healthy athletes. Most commonly at the 2nd metatarsal shaft.
- **S/Smx:** pain and bony tenderness, swelling, antalgic gait (limp).
- **Ix:** XR (AP & lateral view). Repeat XR may be needed for stress fractures, as they may appear normal at first but 2–3 weeks later periosteal reaction may show. Isotope scan or MRI may show a stress fracture.
- **Mx:** activity modification/rest from the precipitating activity, protected weight-bearing (e.g. stiff-soled shoe or boot) — most 2nd metatarsal shaft stress fractures heal well with conservative management given a better blood supply than the proximal 5th metatarsal Jones-fracture zone above; gradual return to activity guided by symptom resolution rather than a fixed timeline alone.

### 0.2.3 Plantar fasciitis

- **D:** inflammation of the plantar fascia.
- **S/Smx:** pain worse around the medial calcaneal tuberosity, usually on the onset of activity.
- **Mx:** rest where possible; wear shoes with good arch support and cushioned heels; feet stretching before exercise.

## Hip / neck of femur (NOF) fractures

- **R:** osteoporosis, osteopenia, ↑age, falls, ↓BMI, F>M, high energy trauma.
- **S/Smx:** pain ± rhabdomyolysis/AKI; shortened, externally rotated leg; if the fracture is not too severe, the patient may still be able to weight bear.

> [!info] Classification by location
> - **Intracapsular (subcapital):** from the edge of the femoral head to the insertion of the capsule of the hip joint.
> - **Extracapsular:** trochanteric or subtrochanteric — the lesser trochanter is the dividing line.

> [!info] Garden system classification
> - **Type I:** stable fracture, with impaction in valgus.
> - **Type II:** complete fracture but undisplaced.
> - **Type III:** displaced fracture, usually rotated and angulated, but still has bony contact.
> - **Type IV:** complete bony disruption.
>
> Blood supply disruption occurs in Types III and IV.

### Management

- **Suspected NOF fracture:** primary survey + determine nature of injury; request AP pelvis and lateral view XR; pain score + appropriate analgesia; IV access + bloods (including G&S). If NOF fracture confirmed or still suspected — MRI (gold standard) or CT (if MRI contraindicated or not available within 24h).
- **Undisplaced intracapsular fractures:** internal fixation, or hemiarthroplasty if unfit.
- **Displaced intracapsular fractures:** arthroplasty (THR or hemiarthroplasty). THR favoured if the patient is able to walk independently out of doors with only a stick, has no cognitive impairment, and is medically fit for anaesthesia and the operation.
- **Extracapsular fractures:** stable intertrochanteric fractures — dynamic hip screw. Reverse oblique, transverse, or subtrochanteric fractures — intramedullary device.
- Also: pain management, rehabilitation, falls risk reduction, medication review.

> [!danger] Complications: VTE, avascular necrosis, non-union.
> **P:** 30% mortality at 1 year; up to 75% may not regain pre-morbidity level of function.

## Hip dislocation

- **Posterior dislocation (90%):** shortened, adducted, internally rotated.
- **Anterior dislocation:** abducted and externally rotated. No leg shortening.
- **Mx:** ABCDE + analgesia; reduction under general anaesthesia within 4h to reduce risk of avascular necrosis; long-term physiotherapy.
- **Complications:** sciatic or femoral nerve injury; avascular necrosis; OA (especially in older patients); recurrent dislocation due to ligament damage.
- **P:** 2–3 months for healing; best prognosis when reduced <12h post-injury, with less damage to the joint.

## Avascular necrosis of the hip

- **D:** loss of blood supply resulting in death of bone tissue.
- **A:** long-term steroid use, chemotherapy, alcohol excess, trauma.
- **S/Smx:** initially asymptomatic; pain in the affected joint.
- **Ix:** XR may be normal at first — later shows osteopenia, microfractures. Crescent sign when there is collapse of the articular surface. MRI is the imaging of choice.
- **Mx:** joint replacement.

## Acetabular labral tear (hip labral tear)

- **A:** trauma, degeneration, etc.
- **S/Smx:** hip/groin pain; snapping sensation around the hip ± sensation of locking.
- **Ix:** XR ± MRI.
- **Mx:** conservative (rest, NSAIDs, physiotherapy) ± steroid injection.

## Meralgia paraesthetica

- **D:** entrapment neuropathy of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve.
- **R:** age 30–40, M>F, diabetes, obesity, tense ascites, trauma. Can also be caused iatrogenically (e.g. laparoscopic hernia repair).
- **S/Smx:** burning sensation over the anterolateral aspect of the thigh; also numbness, deep muscle ache, coldness, shooting pain. Symptoms worse on standing, relieved by sitting.

> [!tip] Symptoms reproducible by deep palpation over the ASIS (pelvic compression test is highly sensitive) and extension of the hip.

- **Ix:** clinical ± nerve conduction studies.
- **Mx:** injection of the nerve with local anaesthetic will abolish pain (also confirms the diagnosis) — ultrasound-guided injection.

## Pubic symphysis dysfunction (pelvic girdle pain)

- **D:** syndrome arising from pubic symphysis ligament laxity, most commonly in pregnancy due to hormonal changes.
- **S/Smx:** pain over the pubic symphysis with radiation to the groins and medial aspect of the thighs; ± waddling gait.
- **Mx:** usually resolves on giving birth. Analgesia, physiotherapy (e.g. pelvic floor physical therapy).

## Idiopathic transient osteoporosis of the hip

- **D:** temporary loss of bone in the femoral head and neck.
- **R:** M>F (3:1), men aged 40–55, women in the 3rd trimester of pregnancy.
- **S/Smx:** absence of trauma in history; progressive hip and groin pain over several weeks; may not be able to bear weight; local tenderness + usually normal hip range of movement.
- **Ix:** XR, MRI.
- **Mx:** avoid weight bearing to avoid stress fractures; resolves spontaneously in 6–8 months.

## Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (trochanteric bursitis)

- **A:** repetitive activity, mechanical overload.
- **P:** repeated movement of the fibroelastic iliotibial band.
- **S/Smx:** pain on the lateral side of the hip or thigh + tenderness on palpation of the greater trochanter.
- **Ix:** XR to rule out other pathology.
- **Mx:** activity modification, physiotherapy, weight loss, steroid injection, NSAIDs.

## 0.1 Knee — ligament and meniscal injuries

> [!info] Unhappy triad — usually secondary to a lateral blow to the knee. ACL + MCL + meniscus (medial or lateral) injury together.

| Structure | Key features |
|---|---|
| **ACL** | Secondary to twisting injuries; positive anterior drawer & Lachman's test |
| **PCL** | Dashboard injuries |
| **MCL** | Damage commonly from skiing and valgus stress; usually causes passive knee abduction |
| **LCL** | Isolated injury uncommon |
| **Menisci** | Secondary to twisting injuries; locking and giving way common; Thessaly's test — weight bearing at 20° of knee flexion, positive if pain on twisting the knee |

- **Ix:** MRI needed to visualise ligaments and menisci.
- **Mx:** refer to orthopaedics — arthroscopic or open surgical management.

## 0.2 Bursitis & cysts of the knee

- **Infrapatellar bursitis:** associated with kneeling ("Clergyman's knee").
- **Prepatellar bursitis:** associated with kneeling ("Housemaid's knee").
- **Ix (both):** primarily clinical, but the same key diagnostic task as olecranon bursitis in [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] applies — distinguishing aseptic from septic bursitis, given repetitive kneeling can cause skin breaches that predispose to infection; aspirate for microscopy, Gram stain, and culture if there's any suspicion of infection (marked warmth/erythema, fever, or an obvious skin breach overlying the bursa).
- **Mx (both):** the same aseptic-vs-septic distinction and management principle as olecranon bursitis in [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] applies here — not repeated in full, but in brief: **aseptic** — activity modification (avoiding/padding kneeling), ice, NSAIDs, aspiration for symptomatic relief of large effusions; **septic** — antibiotics ± aspiration/drainage, treated with the same urgency as other periarticular soft tissue infections.
- **Baker cyst:**
  - **D:** distension of the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa. Primary — no underlying pathology. Secondary — underlying problem, e.g. OA.
  - **S/Smx:** bulge behind the knee; if rupture occurs, may cause symptoms similar to DVT (but can also be asymptomatic).
  - **Ix:** clinical diagnosis in typical cases; ultrasound can confirm and distinguish from other popliteal masses, and is particularly useful when a ruptured cyst is mimicking DVT given the two are a classic diagnostic confusion — compression ultrasound can assess for both simultaneously, which is often the actual clinical question being asked.
  - **Mx:** in children, no treatment required. In adults, treat the underlying cause (e.g. OA management if secondary); aspiration ± corticosteroid injection can be considered for symptomatic cysts, though recurrence is common without addressing the underlying joint pathology driving fluid production.

## 0.3 Patella fracture

- **A:** direct injury (e.g. fall, dashboard injury); indirect injury caused by forceful contraction of the quadriceps (e.g. when trying to prevent a fall).
- **S/Smx:** swelling and bruising; look for associated fractures; pain and tenderness around the knee, well localised to the patella; test whether the patient can still extend their leg.
- **Ix:** XR (AP & lateral).
- **Mx:** undisplaced + intact extensor mechanism — does not require surgery; hinged knee brace for 6 weeks + ask the patient to fully weight bear. Otherwise, surgical management is likely required.

## 0.4 Iliotibial band syndrome

- **D:** inflammation of the iliotibial band.
- **R:** runners (1 in 10 people who run regularly).
- **S/Smx:** burning pain at or just under the lateral femoral epicondyle; tenderness 2–3cm above the lateral knee joint line.
- **Mx:** activity modification and iliotibial band stretches ± physiotherapy.

## 0.5 Ankle fractures

> [!info] Ottawa Rules for XR of ankles
> - Pain in the malleolar zone.
> - Bony tenderness at the lateral malleolar zone (from the tip of the lateral malleolus to include the lower 6cm of the posterior border of the fibula).
> - Bony tenderness at the medial malleolar zone (from the tip of the medial malleolus to the lower 6cm of the posterior border of the tibia).
> - Inability to walk four weight-bearing steps immediately after the injury and in the Emergency Department (ED).

> [!info] Weber classification
> - **A:** below the syndesmosis.
> - **B:** fracture starts at the level of the tibial plafond and may extend proximally to involve the syndesmosis.
> - **C:** above the syndesmosis, which may itself be damaged.
>
> A subtype known as a **Maisonneuve fracture** may occur with a spiral fibular fracture that leads to disruption of the syndesmosis with widening of the ankle joint — surgery is required.

- **Mx:** depends on stability of the ankle joint and patient co-morbidities.
  - All ankle fractures should be promptly reduced to remove pressure on the overlying skin and subsequent necrosis.
  - Young patients with unstable, high velocity, or proximal injuries will usually require surgical repair.
  - Elderly patients — conservative management is usually better.

## 0.6 Ankle sprains

- **D:** a sprain is a stretching, partial, or complete tear of a ligament.
  - High ankle sprains — involving the syndesmosis.
  - Low ankle sprains — involving the lateral collateral ligaments.

### 0.6.1 Low ankle sprains (90%)

- Injury to the ATFL is most common. Inversion injury is the most common mechanism.
- **S/Smx:** pain, swelling, tenderness over affected ligaments ± bruising; patients are usually able to weight bear unless severe.

> [!info] Grading
> - **Grade I (mild):** microtear of ligament.
> - **Grade II (moderate):** partial tear.
> - **Grade III (severe):** complete tear.

- **Ix:** XR by Ottawa rules ± MRI.
- **Mx:** RICE ± orthosis (cast, crutches). Severe — consider surgery.

### 0.6.2 High ankle sprains (10%)

- Usually secondary to an eversion mechanism.
- Weight bearing is difficult.

> [!tip] Hopkin's squeeze test — pain when the tibia and fibula are squeezed together mid-calf.

- **Ix:** XR by Ottawa rules ± MRI.
- **Mx:** if no separation — non-weight-bearing orthosis or cast until pain subsides. If separation — ORIF.

## 0.7 Achilles tendon

- **R:** quinolones (e.g. ciprofloxacin) associated with tendon disorders. Hypercholesterolaemia associated with tendon xanthomata.

### 0.7.1 Tendinitis

- Gradual onset of posterior heel pain, worse following activity; morning pain and stiffness.
- **Mx:** supportive — analgesia, reduce precipitating activities, physiotherapy (calf eccentric exercises).

### 0.7.2 Tendon rupture

> [!note] Gap-filled — this entry was genuinely thin relative to its High-yield CSV status: no examination technique, no Ix, and no Mx at all.

- **S/Smx:** sudden pop + sudden pain + inability to walk/continue playing, often during forceful push-off (classically sport); palpable gap in the tendon on examination; weak or absent active plantarflexion.
- **Ix — Simmonds-Thompson test (the "calf squeeze" test), the key bedside diagnostic tool:** patient prone with the foot hanging freely off the end of the bed; squeeze the calf — **normal plantarflexion of the foot is a negative test; absent/minimal plantarflexion compared to the unaffected side is positive**, indicating rupture. Genuinely high accuracy (~96% sensitivity, ~93% specificity) — more sensitive for acute rupture than imaging. A **false negative can occur if the plantaris tendon remains intact**, since it can independently produce some plantarflexion despite a complete Achilles rupture — a genuinely important pitfall given it can falsely reassure. Ultrasound or MRI reserved for equivocal clinical findings or to measure the tendon gap for surgical planning, not needed routinely when the clinical picture and calf squeeze test are clearly positive.
- **Mx:** genuinely an area of ongoing debate rather than one settled answer — **current evidence shows no significant difference in long-term functional outcomes between surgical repair and conservative (functional rehabilitation with early mobilisation in a boot) management** for most patients, a meaningful shift from the older belief that surgery was clearly superior. Conservative management carries a **higher re-rupture risk** but avoids surgical complications (wound infection, sural nerve injury); surgical repair carries lower re-rupture risk but the complication profile above. The traditional cast-immobilisation-alone approach (without early functional rehabilitation) is now recognised as inferior to both modern options, given a high historical re-rupture rate (10–30%). Management should be individualised (patient age, activity level, tendon gap size) and discussed with orthopaedics rather than defaulting to either option as a fixed rule.

> [!info] Simmond's triad — ask the patient to lie down, feet hanging off the bed:
> 1. Rupture may lead to ↑dorsiflexion.
> 2. Feel for a gap in the tendon.
> 3. Squeeze the calf muscle — rupture will cause the muscle to stay in a neutral position.

- **Dx:** by ultrasound.
- **Mx:** urgent referral to orthopaedics.

## Upper limb peripheral nerve lesions — sensory and motor findings

| Nerve | Sensory loss | Motor weakness | Special notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median (at the wrist — carpal tunnel syndrome) | Palmar aspect of thumb, index, middle, and half of ring finger | Thenar muscle weakness (abductor pollicis brevis) | "Ape hand" deformity in longstanding/severe cases (thenar wasting, thumb falls in line with the fingers); positive Tinel's sign (tapping over the carpal tunnel reproduces symptoms) and Phalen's test (wrist flexion for 60 seconds reproduces symptoms) |
