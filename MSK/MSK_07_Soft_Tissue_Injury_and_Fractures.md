## 0.1 Soft Tissue Injury and Healing

**A/P:** Injury to **tendon (muscle to bone), ligament (bone to bone), muscle, fascia, bursa or cartilage**, healing through three overlapping phases:
**1. INFLAMMATORY — the first days.** Haematoma, cytokine release, cellular recruitment.
**2. PROLIFERATIVE / REPAIR — days to weeks.** Fibroblast activity laying down disorganised type III collagen.
**3. REMODELLING — months to a year or more.** Collagen reorganises along lines of stress and matures into type I.

> [!danger] Load is the stimulus for healing, not the enemy of it
> **Connective tissue adapts to the load placed on it. Complete rest causes atrophy, disorganised collagen, stiffness and weakness; appropriate progressive load drives organised remodelling and restores strength.**
> **This inverts the intuitive approach** — patients (and clinicians) reach for rest, immobilisation and avoidance, which produce a weaker tissue and a longer recovery.
> **The clinical task is finding the level of load that stimulates without re-injuring**, and progressing it. That is what physiotherapy actually does.

> [!warning] Set the timeframe honestly at the first consultation
> **Tendon and ligament have poor blood supply and remodel slowly. Full remodelling takes MONTHS TO A YEAR.**
> **Patients who are told "six weeks" and are still symptomatic at three months conclude something is wrong, seek imaging, and enter the investigation cycle.** **Telling them the real timeframe at the outset prevents that**, and is one of the more valuable things said in the consultation.

> [!tip] What impairs healing
> **Age · DIABETES · smoking · corticosteroids (systemic and injected) · FLUOROQUINOLONE antibiotics · poor nutrition and low protein intake · peripheral vascular disease · obesity · and prolonged immobilisation.**
> **Diabetes and metabolic syndrome are genuinely associated with tendinopathy and with poorer healing**, which is worth knowing when a tendinopathy is not responding as expected.

## 0.2 Acute Soft Tissue Injury Management

> [!danger] RICE has been superseded — the current framework is PEACE and LOVE
> **PEACE — in the first days after injury:**
> **· PROTECT** — unload and restrict movement briefly, **for days rather than weeks.**
> **· ELEVATE.**
> **· AVOID ANTI-INFLAMMATORIES** — see the caveat below.
> **· COMPRESS.**
> **· EDUCATE** — set expectations, and steer away from passive treatments and unnecessary imaging.
> **LOVE — after the first days:**
> **· LOAD** — progressive mechanical loading as symptoms allow.
> **· OPTIMISM** — **patient expectations independently predict outcome**, and this is not a soft add-on. Catastrophising and fear-avoidance measurably prolong recovery.
> **· VASCULARISATION** — early pain-free cardiovascular activity.
> **· EXERCISE** — restoring mobility, strength and proprioception.

> [!warning] The NSAID and ice question is genuinely contested
> **The rationale for avoiding early NSAIDs and prolonged ice is that inflammation is a necessary part of tissue repair, and there is evidence that NSAIDs may impair tendon, ligament, muscle and bone healing.**
> **But this is not settled**, and **adequate analgesia matters** — a patient in severe pain will not mobilise, and immobility causes its own harm.
> **A pragmatic position: prefer paracetamol and topical agents first, use oral NSAIDs at the lowest effective dose for the shortest period where analgesia genuinely requires it, and do not use them routinely for weeks. Avoid opioids.**
> `UNVERIFIED — this is an area of active debate; check current sports medicine guidance rather than treating either position as settled.`

> [!danger] Prolonged immobilisation causes harm
> **For most soft tissue injuries, early controlled movement produces better outcomes than immobilisation** — less stiffness, less muscle loss, faster return to function.
> **Prolonged use of slings, boots, casts and collars beyond what the injury requires produces joint stiffness, muscle atrophy and, in the shoulder, adhesive capsulitis.**
> **Where immobilisation is genuinely required — a fracture, a complete tendon rupture — it should be for a defined period with a plan for what follows.**

> [!tip] When to image, and when to refer
> **Image for: a decision rule indicating fracture risk, suspicion of complete rupture, inability to weight bear, significant deformity, neurovascular deficit, or failure to progress as expected.**
> **Refer for: complete tendon or ligament rupture, joint instability, neurovascular compromise, open injury, suspected compartment syndrome, and failure of appropriate conservative management.**
> **Do NOT image a straightforward sprain that is following the expected course.**

**Ix:** Radiographs per decision rules (*why:* excludes fracture where indicated; *what:* fracture). Ultrasound (*why:* dynamic assessment of tendon and muscle integrity, accessible and without radiation; *what:* tear, gap, tendinopathy). MRI (*why:* ligamentous, cartilage and deeper structures where the diagnosis will change management; *what:* the structural injury).

## 0.3 Tendinopathy

> [!danger] It is a degenerative process, not an inflammatory one — the name matters
> **Histology of chronic tendinopathy shows COLLAGEN DISORGANISATION, increased ground substance, NEOVASCULARISATION and cellular changes — with FEW OR NO INFLAMMATORY CELLS.**
> **This is why anti-inflammatory approaches disappoint, why "tendinitis" is the wrong word, and why the treatment is mechanical rather than pharmacological.**
> A useful model describes a **continuum: reactive tendinopathy (acute overload, reversible) → tendon disrepair → degenerative tendinopathy (structural change, less reversible)** — with the practical implication that **early recognition and load management can reverse it, while established degenerative change cannot be undone but can be worked around.**

**S/Smx:** **Load-related pain** with a characteristic pattern — **pain at the start of activity that "warms up" and eases, then returns worse in the hours afterwards and the next morning.** Localised tenderness at the tendon, pain on resisted contraction, and sometimes thickening.

> [!tip] Load is the treatment
> **Progressive loading is the best-evidenced management, and it works across sites.**
> A typical progression: **ISOMETRIC loading first (which is often analgesic in the reactive phase) → heavy slow resistance / isotonic loading → energy-storage and sport-specific loading.**
> **It takes months and adherence is the main determinant of success** — which is why explaining the rationale matters. A patient who believes rest is the treatment will not do the exercises.
> **Alongside: load management (reducing the aggravating volume without stopping entirely), footwear and ergonomic modification, and addressing metabolic contributors.**

> [!danger] What does not work, and what causes harm
> **· COMPLETE REST** — deconditions the tendon and the whole kinetic chain, and symptoms recur immediately on return.
> **· REPEATED CORTICOSTEROID INJECTION** — short-term relief with **worse long-term outcomes**, most clearly demonstrated in lateral epicondylalgia. Cross-refer [[L5]] 0.3.
> **· NEVER inject corticosteroid INTO the Achilles or patellar tendon** — it risks rupture in a load-bearing tendon.
> **· Passive modalities alone** — ultrasound therapy, massage and taping may provide symptomatic relief but do not produce tendon adaptation.

> [!danger] Fluoroquinolone-associated tendinopathy and rupture
> **Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, moxifloxacin) cause tendinopathy and tendon RUPTURE, most often of the Achilles, and it can occur within days of starting and up to months after stopping.**
> **Risk is higher with: concurrent CORTICOSTEROIDS, age over 60, renal impairment, and transplant recipients.**
> **If a patient on a fluoroquinolone develops tendon pain, STOP THE DRUG immediately, avoid loading the tendon, and review.**
> **This should also inform prescribing** — a fluoroquinolone is a poor choice in an older patient on prednisolone with a history of Achilles problems, and there is usually an alternative. Cross-refer [[L5]] 0.7.

**Ix:** **Usually a clinical diagnosis.** Ultrasound (*why:* confirms tendinopathy and excludes a tear where the diagnosis is uncertain — **but structural change on imaging correlates poorly with symptoms and is common in asymptomatic tendons**, so it should not drive management; *what:* thickening, hypoechoic change, neovascularity, tear). HbA1c and lipids (*why:* metabolic factors are associated with tendinopathy and poor response; *what:* diabetes, dyslipidaemia). **Medication review for fluoroquinolones and corticosteroids** (*why:* a reversible and dangerous contributor; *what:* culprit drug).

## 0.4 Muscle Injury

> [!tip] Strains and the sites that matter
> Graded by the extent of fibre disruption and the presence of a palpable defect. `UNVERIFIED — the grading system.`
> **· HAMSTRING** — the commonest sporting muscle injury, and it has a **high recurrence rate** because athletes return before rehabilitation is complete. **Return should be criteria-based, not time-based** — see 0.5.
> **· Medial gastrocnemius ("tennis leg")** — sudden calf pain on push-off, with bruising tracking to the ankle over days. **Must be distinguished from DVT and from Achilles rupture** — do the calf squeeze test and consider imaging, because the three coexist in the differential and a calf that is swollen and painful after an injury can still have a DVT.
> **· Quadriceps and adductor strains.**

> [!warning] Myositis ossificans
> **Heterotopic bone formation within a muscle after significant blunt contusion — classically the quadriceps after a direct knee to the thigh.**
> Presents as **persistent pain, a hard palpable mass and restricted range weeks after the injury**, and it can be mistaken radiologically for a sarcoma.
> **Aggressive early massage, forceful stretching and heat are thought to increase the risk** — so a large thigh contusion is managed with gentle early range of motion in flexion, not vigorous physiotherapy.

> [!tip] Delayed onset muscle soreness versus injury
> **DOMS: bilateral or diffuse, appears 24–48 hours after unaccustomed eccentric exercise, peaks at 48–72 hours, and improves with gentle activity.** It is normal.
> **Injury: immediate onset, focal, with a specific mechanism, and worse with activity.**
> **Distinguishing them prevents both unnecessary investigation and inappropriate reassurance.**

> [!danger] The two muscle emergencies
> **· ACUTE COMPARTMENT SYNDROME — pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment.** **Pulses are present and the limb is pink until very late — waiting for pulselessness means waiting too long.** Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6.
> **· RHABDOMYOLYSIS** — after crush injury, prolonged immobility, extreme exertion, seizures, or drug toxicity. Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6.
> **CHRONIC EXERTIONAL compartment syndrome** is different — exercise-induced pain and tightness that resolves predictably with rest, diagnosed by compartment pressure testing, and managed with load modification, gait retraining or fasciotomy.

**Ix:** Ultrasound or MRI where a significant tear is suspected (*why:* grading affects prognosis and return timeline, and identifies a complete rupture requiring surgery; *what:* tear extent, haematoma). **Doppler ultrasound where calf injury could be DVT** (*why:* they mimic each other and coexist; *what:* thrombus). CK and renal function where rhabdomyolysis is possible (*why:* acute kidney injury; *what:* raised CK, rising creatinine). **Compartment pressure measurement** in suspected chronic exertional compartment syndrome (*why:* the diagnostic test, performed with exercise; *what:* elevated post-exercise pressures).

## 0.5 Rehabilitation and Return to Activity

> [!tip] The sequence
> **1. Restore RANGE of motion and reduce pain and swelling.**
> **2. Restore STRENGTH — progressively, including eccentric loading.**
> **3. Restore PROPRIOCEPTION and neuromuscular control** — **this is the step most often skipped, and it is what prevents re-injury**, particularly after ankle sprain and ACL injury.
> **4. Sport- or task-SPECIFIC training** — replicating the demands the tissue will face.
> **5. Return, with ongoing maintenance.**

> [!danger] Return should be criteria-based, not time-based
> **"Six weeks" is not a criterion. The questions are: is the range full? is strength symmetrical? can they perform the specific tasks required? is there pain during or after?**
> **Time-based return is the main reason hamstring and ankle injuries recur.**
> **Psychological readiness matters too** — after ACL reconstruction, fear of re-injury is a significant and measurable predictor of failure to return to sport, independent of physical recovery.

> [!warning] Rapid increases in load cause overuse injury
> **The commonest cause of overuse injury is a sudden increase in training volume, intensity, frequency or surface — a new running program, a return after a break, a change of footwear, or a competition block.**
> **Ask about what changed in the weeks before the symptoms started** — it is usually there, and modifying it is the treatment.
> **Graded progression, and avoidance of large week-to-week jumps in load, is the preventive principle.**

> [!tip] Certification and return to work
> **Certificates should specify what the person CAN do, not only what they cannot** — restricted duties, modified hours, avoiding specific tasks — because **prolonged complete absence from work worsens outcomes and reduces the likelihood of ever returning.**
> **Early graded return to work is protective**, and the same fear-avoidance principles apply as in back pain. Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.1, [[A10]] 0.6 and [[GER3]].
> In South Australia, work-related injury involves **ReturnToWorkSA** processes. `UNVERIFIED — current certification requirements and forms.`

## 0.6 Immobility, Mobility Aids and Functional Assessment

> [!danger] Immobility is a diagnosis with its own complications, and most of them are preventable
> **Muscle loss begins within DAYS of bed rest and is disproportionately severe in older people.**
> **The consequences: falls · pressure injury · venous thromboembolism · pneumonia and atelectasis · constipation · urinary retention and infection · delirium · bone loss · orthostatic intolerance · and permanent functional decline leading to residential placement.**
> **On every ward round, ask: has this patient been out of bed today, and what is preventing it?** Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.4.

> [!warning] Fitting a walking stick — three things that are commonly wrong
> **1. HELD IN THE OPPOSITE HAND to the affected leg.** This is counterintuitive and is the most frequent error. Holding it on the affected side does almost nothing; holding it opposite widens the base of support and genuinely offloads the affected limb.
> **2. HEIGHT — the handle should be at the level of the WRIST CREASE (or the ulnar styloid) when the person stands upright with the arm relaxed at their side**, giving about 15–20 degrees of elbow flexion.
> **3. TECHNIQUE — the stick and the affected leg move TOGETHER**, then the good leg steps through.
> **A stick that is too tall or too short, or held in the wrong hand, increases falls risk rather than reducing it — an incorrectly fitted aid is worse than none.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the precise fitting measurements.`

> [!tip] The other aids
> **· Axillary crutches — weight must go through the HANDS, not the axilla.** **Weight-bearing through the axilla causes crutch palsy — a radial nerve injury with wrist drop.** There should be a two-finger gap between the axilla and the crutch top.
> **· Forearm (elbow) crutches** — better for longer-term use and for those with adequate upper limb strength.
> **· Pick-up frame** — provides most stability but must be lifted, so it requires upper limb strength and interrupts gait rhythm.
> **· Wheeled frame or four-wheel walker** — better for those who cannot lift a frame, allows continuous gait, and a seat allows rest. **Requires adequate cognition and braking ability** — a four-wheel walker given to someone who cannot manage the brakes is a hazard.
> **Aids should be prescribed and fitted by a physiotherapist where possible**, and reviewed as function changes.

> [!info] Weight-bearing status — know the terms
> **· NWB — non-weight-bearing:** no contact with the ground at all.
> **· TTWB / TDWB — touch or toe-touch weight-bearing:** the foot rests on the ground for balance only, taking minimal load.
> **· PWB — partial weight-bearing:** a specified proportion of body weight.
> **· WBAT — weight-bearing as tolerated.**
> **· FWB — full weight-bearing.**
> **Getting this right matters: an order for non-weight-bearing in an older patient often means they cannot mobilise at all**, with all the consequences above — so it should be specified deliberately by the surgical team and questioned if it seems inconsistent with the injury.

> [!tip] Functional assessment — the measures that are quick and predictive
> **· Timed Up and Go** — time to rise from a chair, walk a short distance, turn, return and sit. **Quick, repeatable, and predicts falls.**
> **· Gait speed** — one of the best single predictors of adverse outcomes in older people.
> **· Sit-to-stand test** — lower limb strength and functional capacity.
> **· ADL and IADL assessment** — cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.3, remembering that **instrumental activities decline first.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the cut-off values for each measure.`

> [!warning] Rehabilitation settings and who benefits
> **· Inpatient rehabilitation** — for those with rehabilitation potential and goals who cannot yet manage at home.
> **· Geriatric evaluation and management (GEM)** — for older patients with multiple problems.
> **· Transition care** — time-limited support after hospital, allowing recovery before permanent decisions are made. **Under-used, and it prevents premature residential placement.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.3.
> **· Community and outpatient rehabilitation, and home-based programs.**
> **The key principle: assess rehabilitation potential rather than assuming it from age**, and **do not make permanent placement decisions from an acute bed.**

**Ix:** **Physiotherapy and occupational therapy assessment** (*why:* they determine aid prescription, home safety and rehabilitation potential more accurately than a medical assessment does; *what:* mobility, transfers, ADL performance, equipment needs). **Home visit or home assessment** (*why:* function in a corridor bears little relation to function at home with stairs, thresholds, bathrooms and no handrails; *what:* hazards, feasibility). Timed Up and Go and gait speed (*why:* objective baseline and falls risk; *what:* the values). Cognitive assessment (*why:* determines the safety of certain aids and the capacity for rehabilitation; *what:* impairment).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Mobilise. Remove unnecessary tethers — catheters, lines, telemetry. Analgesia adequate to permit movement.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Rehabilitation matched to need, correctly fitted aids, and home modification.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Ongoing exercise, falls prevention, review of aids as function changes, and carer support. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.5.

## 0.1 Describing a Fracture

> [!tip] The systematic description — examinable, and it forces you to see everything
> **1. The film itself** — patient details, date, which side, which views, adequacy.
> **2. WHICH BONE and WHERE** — proximal, middle or distal third; or epiphysis, metaphysis, diaphysis.
> **3. SIMPLE (two fragments), COMMINUTED (more than two), or SEGMENTAL.**
> **4. PATTERN — transverse · oblique · spiral · avulsion · impacted · greenstick · buckle (torus).**
> **5. DISPLACEMENT — always described by where the DISTAL fragment has gone relative to the proximal.** This convention is universal and getting it backwards makes the description meaningless.
> **6. ANGULATION** — conventionally by the direction the APEX points, though many describe the distal fragment's tilt; **state which convention you are using.**
> **7. ROTATION and SHORTENING.**
> **8. INTRA-ARTICULAR extension** — because it changes management: intra-articular fractures need anatomical reduction to prevent post-traumatic arthritis.
> **9. OPEN or CLOSED.**
> **10. ASSOCIATED findings** — dislocation, effusion or lipohaemarthrosis, foreign body, and the **neurovascular status**, which is clinical rather than radiological but belongs in the same assessment.

> [!danger] Two views at right angles, and the joint above and below
> **A single view misses displacement, angulation and sometimes the fracture entirely.** **Minimum two orthogonal views.**
> **Imaging the joint above and below is what catches the associated dislocations** — a Monteggia or Galeazzi injury is missed when only the mid-shaft is imaged, and the dislocation is the part that causes long-term disability. See 0.3.

## 1.7 Limb X-Ray and Fracture Description — Systematic Approach

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Fracture lines in FRACTUREs," High yield, and "deformities (displacement vs angulation vs rotation)," Low yield) — genuinely absent as a unifying systematic approach despite CXR and AXR both having one above, and despite individual fracture entries throughout [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] and elsewhere in Orthopaedics describing displacement/angulation for specific fractures without a single reference explaining the underlying descriptive framework itself.

**Why this matters:** unlike CXR/AXR, a limb X-ray read is less about spotting one classic pattern and more about **precisely and reproducibly describing what's found**, since that description directly drives the reduction-vs-surgery decision for the specific fracture — the same displacement/angulation vocabulary is used throughout the individual fracture entries elsewhere in this project, so understanding it here as a standalone skill makes each of those entries easier to interpret rather than needing to be re-explained fracture by fracture.

**Practical basics first:** at least **two orthogonal views** (typically AP and lateral) are required to properly assess a fracture — a single view can hide displacement, angulation, or rotation that's only apparent from the second plane, and can make a foreshortened-but-actually-normal-length bone look shortened when it's really just angulated out of the imaging plane. Include the joint above and below a long bone fracture on the request where clinically relevant, given a second, less obvious injury can otherwise be missed.

**The PLACES framework — a systematic structure for describing any fracture:**

1. **Pattern:** the configuration of the fracture line itself, which reflects the mechanism of injury and gives a clue to stability —
   - **Transverse** (straight across, perpendicular to the long axis — typically a direct blow).
   - **Oblique** (diagonal across the bone — typically an angulatory force).
   - **Spiral** (curves around the bone — typically a rotational/torsional force; a genuinely important pattern to recognise given an unexplained spiral fracture, particularly in a non-ambulatory child, raises concern for non-accidental injury — see [[15_24a_Paeds_-_Non-Accidental_Injury_and_Sexual_Abuse]], not repeated here).
   - **Comminuted** (more than two fragments — implies a higher-energy mechanism and is generally less stable).
   - **Avulsion** (a bone fragment pulled off at a tendon/ligament attachment by traction, rather than a direct fracturing force through the bone shaft).
   - **Buckle/torus and greenstick** (incomplete fractures specific to the more pliable, still-growing paediatric skeleton — these patterns occur because a child's bone bends and buckles rather than breaking cleanly the way an adult's more brittle bone does).
2. **Location:** described by the specific bone and the region within it — long bones are conventionally divided into **epiphysis** (the end, at/near the joint), **metaphysis** (the flared region between epiphysis and shaft), and **diaphysis** (the shaft) — and further specified as proximal/middle/distal third where relevant. Note specifically whether the fracture is **intra-articular** (extends into the joint surface) versus **extra-articular**, given intra-articular involvement generally raises the surgical threshold given the risk of post-traumatic arthritis if the joint surface isn't accurately restored.
3. **Alignment (displacement, angulation, rotation, and length) — the core of the "deformity" description:**
   - **Displacement:** the offset of the distal fragment relative to the proximal fragment, described as a percentage of bone width or in millimetres, and by direction (e.g. "50% dorsal displacement") — **translation** specifically means side-to-side offset, while **distraction** means the fragments have separated with a gap between them.
   - **Angulation:** the angle between fragments, described by the direction the distal fragment's apex points relative to the bone's long axis (e.g. dorsal/volar for the wrist, valgus/varus more generally — valgus meaning the distal fragment points away from the midline, varus meaning it points toward the midline).
   - **Rotation:** twisting of the distal fragment relative to the proximal fragment along the bone's long axis — often better assessed clinically (e.g. finger cascade/alignment when the hand is examined in flexion) than on plain X-ray, and best seen radiographically on oblique views specifically; a genuinely important pattern to actively check for in spiral fractures of the hand and forearm, given rotational malalignment is easy to miss if not specifically sought.
   - **Length:** shortening (from impaction or fragment overriding/"bayonet apposition") or lengthening (from distraction) — an oblique fracture shortens more readily than a transverse one, since a transverse fracture would need to fully "off-end" before length is lost, a useful point for anticipating which fracture patterns are prone to shortening.
   - **Convention:** displacement, angulation, and rotation are all described **of the distal fragment relative to the proximal fragment** — this consistent convention is what makes fracture descriptions reproducible between clinicians, and confusing the direction (describing the proximal fragment's position relative to the distal one) is a common source of miscommunication.
4. **Closed vs open (Compound):** whether the overlying skin is breached, communicating the fracture site with the external environment — a genuinely important distinction changing management urgency (open fractures need urgent debridement/antibiotics given the markedly higher infection risk) and classification (e.g. Gustilo-Anderson grading for open fractures) — see the individual fracture entries throughout Orthopaedics for open-fracture-specific management where relevant, not repeated here.
5. **Examination findings:** neurovascular status specifically (distal pulses, capillary refill, and the specific nerves at risk for that fracture's location and displacement direction) — genuinely the most clinically important part of the assessment for many fractures, not just a box-ticking addition to the imaging description, given nerve/vascular injury needs urgent recognition and can be missed if the assessment focuses on the X-ray alone.
6. **Special investigations:** further imaging where plain film is insufficient — CT for complex intra-articular fractures needing precise 3D characterisation before surgical planning, or MRI where an occult fracture is suspected clinically despite a normal/equivocal X-ray (e.g. suspected scaphoid fracture with a normal initial film — see [[11_03_Ortho_-_Hand_and_Foot]] for the disease-level detail on this specific scenario, not repeated here).

**Practical pitfall worth naming explicitly:** subtle but significant findings can be missed without deliberately looking for them — e.g. a posterior fat pad sign at the elbow (suggesting an occult fracture even when no fracture line is clearly visible, given a haemarthrosis elevates the fat pad) — see [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] for this specific sign in context, not repeated here; a systematic PLACES-based read, rather than only scanning for an obvious fracture line, is what catches these subtler findings.

## 0.2 Fracture Healing and Complications

**A/P:** **Secondary (indirect) healing — the usual route with cast or relative stability — proceeds through haematoma → inflammation → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling.** **Primary (direct) healing occurs only with rigid anatomical fixation and produces NO visible callus** — which is why an absence of callus after plating is expected rather than concerning.

> [!tip] What impairs union
> **· Poor blood supply and soft tissue stripping · inadequate stability · infection · a gap between fragments · SMOKING, which substantially impairs union and is worth addressing explicitly · diabetes · corticosteroids · malnutrition and low vitamin D · NSAIDs (debated) · and older age.**
> **Smoking cessation is a legitimate and evidence-based part of fracture management**, particularly before elective fusion or reconstruction.

> [!warning] Early complications
> **· NEUROVASCULAR INJURY — assess and DOCUMENT before and after every reduction and every splint application.**
> **· COMPARTMENT SYNDROME** — cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6. **Highest risk in tibial shaft and forearm fractures, and in crush injuries.** **Pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch; pulses remain present until very late.**
> **· Haemorrhage** — femoral shaft and pelvic fractures can cause substantial blood loss into the thigh or retroperitoneum.
> **· Open fracture and infection** — see 0.7.
> **· FAT EMBOLISM SYNDROME** — typically **24 to 72 hours after a long bone or pelvic fracture**, with the triad of **respiratory distress and hypoxia, neurological change (confusion, agitation, reduced consciousness) and a PETECHIAL RASH** classically over the axillae, conjunctivae and upper chest. **The rash is the discriminating feature and is easily missed if the patient is not undressed.** Management is supportive; early fracture fixation reduces the risk.
> **· Venous thromboembolism** — high risk with lower limb fracture and immobilisation; prophylaxis required.

> [!danger] Late complications, and the AVN sites worth memorising
> **· MALUNION and NON-UNION** — atrophic (poor biology, no callus) versus hypertrophic (poor stability, abundant callus).
> **· AVASCULAR NECROSIS — the sites where blood supply is retrograde or precarious:**
> **— SCAPHOID (proximal pole) · FEMORAL HEAD (intracapsular neck of femur fracture) · TALUS (neck) · LUNATE (Kienböck) · humeral head.**
> **In each of these, the blood supply enters distally and runs back toward the fragment that gets separated** — which is the unifying principle and makes them memorable rather than a list.
> **· Post-traumatic osteoarthritis** — especially after intra-articular fractures.
> **· Growth arrest** in children — see 0.5.
> **· Myositis ossificans** — cross-refer [[L6]] 0.4.
> **· Osteomyelitis.**

> [!warning] Complex regional pain syndrome
> **Pain and other features DISPROPORTIONATE to the inciting injury, persisting beyond the expected course**, typically after a distal limb fracture, crush or surgery.
> **Features: burning pain, ALLODYNIA (pain from light touch) and hyperalgesia · oedema · asymmetry of skin colour and TEMPERATURE · sweating changes · and later trophic changes in skin, nails and hair, with stiffness and contracture.**
> **Diagnosed clinically using the Budapest criteria; there is no confirmatory test.** `UNVERIFIED — the criteria.`
> **Management: early mobilisation, physiotherapy and desensitisation, adequate analgesia including neuropathic agents, and referral to a pain service.** **Prolonged immobilisation makes it worse**, and reassurance that the limb is safe to use is part of treatment.
> **Prevention matters: avoid tight casts, mobilise early, and treat pain adequately after distal limb injury.**

**Ix:** Radiographs at intervals (*why:* assesses union and alignment; *what:* callus, alignment, hardware position). CT (*why:* assesses union where radiographs are equivocal, and intra-articular step-off; *what:* bridging bone, articular congruity). **Vitamin D, calcium and bone profile in delayed union or fragility fracture** (*why:* correctable metabolic contributors; *what:* deficiency). Inflammatory markers and imaging where infection is suspected (*why:* osteomyelitis and infected non-union; *what:* infection).

## 0.3 Upper Limb Fractures and Eponyms

> [!info] Distal radius — the four names
> **· COLLES — extra-articular distal radius with DORSAL displacement and angulation.** The **"dinner fork" deformity.** **FOOSH in an older, often osteoporotic patient — and it is a FRAGILITY FRACTURE that should trigger osteoporosis assessment.** **Check MEDIAN nerve function before and after reduction.**
> **· SMITH — distal radius with VOLAR displacement.** The "reverse Colles", from a fall onto a flexed wrist. More often unstable and requiring fixation.
> **· BARTON — an intra-articular distal radius fracture with SUBLUXATION of the carpus**, dorsal or volar. Unstable.
> **· CHAUFFEUR'S (Hutchinson) — radial styloid fracture.**

> [!danger] Monteggia and Galeazzi — the fracture is obvious, the dislocation is missed
> **· MONTEGGIA — fracture of the ULNAR shaft with dislocation of the RADIAL HEAD.**
> **· GALEAZZI — fracture of the RADIAL shaft with dislocation of the DISTAL RADIOULNAR JOINT.**
> **Mnemonic: GRUM — Galeazzi = Radius; Ulna = Monteggia.**
> **These are the archetypal reason to image the joint above and below.** A forearm shaft fracture with an unexamined and unimaged elbow or wrist is how the dislocation is missed, and **a missed radial head dislocation leads to permanent loss of forearm rotation.**
> **Check the radiocapitellar line on every paediatric and adult elbow film** — a line through the radial neck should pass through the capitellum on every view.

> [!warning] The nerve at risk depends on where the humerus breaks
> **· SURGICAL NECK of humerus → AXILLARY nerve.** Test **regimental badge sensation and deltoid contraction.** Also at risk in anterior shoulder dislocation.
> **· HUMERAL SHAFT (spiral groove) → RADIAL nerve → WRIST DROP.** **Usually a neurapraxia that recovers over weeks to months**, but it must be documented before and after any manipulation, and the wrist splinted in the interim.
> **· SUPRACONDYLAR humerus → median nerve and its ANTERIOR INTEROSSEOUS branch, radial nerve, and the BRACHIAL ARTERY.** See 0.5.
> **· Medial epicondyle → ULNAR nerve.**
> **Document the specific nerve, not "NV intact"** — a generic entry is worthless when the deficit is discovered later.

> [!tip] The rest of the upper limb
> **· SCAPHOID** — cross-refer [[L5]] 0.6. **Radiographically occult in a significant proportion; immobilise and re-image or MRI. Proximal pole fractures risk AVN and non-union.**
> **· BENNETT — an intra-articular fracture-dislocation of the base of the FIRST metacarpal**, with the abductor pollicis longus pulling the shaft proximally. **ROLANDO** is the comminuted, Y- or T-shaped version. Both usually need fixation.
> **· BOXER'S — fifth metacarpal neck.** Angulation is well tolerated at this site. **But any wound over the MCP joints is a FIGHT BITE until proven otherwise** — cross-refer [[A7]] 0.5.
> **· RADIAL HEAD** — often subtle; **a posterior fat pad sign with elbow trauma means an occult fracture** — cross-refer [[L5]] 0.3.
> **· OLECRANON** — triceps insertion, so displaced fractures lose active extension and need fixation.
> **· CLAVICLE** — middle third commonest; most are managed conservatively in a sling. **Check for skin tenting, neurovascular compromise, and pneumothorax with high-energy mechanisms.**
> **· SCAPULA** — implies high energy; **look for associated chest injury.**

## 0.4 Lower Limb Fractures and Eponyms

> [!danger] Neck of femur — the intracapsular/extracapsular distinction determines the operation
> **The femoral head's blood supply runs RETROGRADE up the femoral neck.**
> **· INTRACAPSULAR (subcapital, transcervical) fractures disrupt that supply → high risk of AVN and non-union → the head is REPLACED (hemiarthroplasty, or total hip replacement in more active patients).**
> **· EXTRACAPSULAR (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric) fractures spare the blood supply → the fracture is FIXED (dynamic hip screw or intramedullary nail).**
> **This single anatomical fact explains the entire surgical decision**, and it is examinable.
> **Clinical presentation: a SHORTENED, EXTERNALLY ROTATED leg** with inability to weight bear after a fall. **The Garden classification** grades displacement. `UNVERIFIED — the classification.`
> **Undisplaced and impacted fractures may still allow weight bearing and may be radiographically occult** — cross-refer [[L5]] 0.4.

> [!warning] The hip fracture care bundle — where an intern makes the difference
> **· ANALGESIA EARLY, and a regional block.** **A fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block provides excellent analgesia, reduces opioid requirement and reduces delirium** — and it is under-used. Do not manage a hip fracture with escalating opioids alone.
> **· SURGERY WITHIN A DEFINED SHORT WINDOW** — delay increases mortality, delirium, pressure injury and pneumonia. **Do not delay for marginal "medical optimisation"** — most patients are best optimised by having the fracture fixed. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended time-to-surgery target.`
> **· ORTHOGERIATRIC co-management** — improves mortality and function, and is the standard of care.
> **· Delirium prevention · pressure area care · early mobilisation on day one post-operatively · nutrition · VTE prophylaxis · continence care.**
> **· BONE PROTECTION and FALLS ASSESSMENT before discharge** — this is the fragility fracture that should trigger osteoporosis treatment, and it is the one most often missed. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.5–0.6.
> **· Discharge planning from admission.**
> **Mortality in the year after hip fracture is high, and a proportion never return to their previous residence or function** — which makes an honest prognostic and goals-of-care conversation appropriate. Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.5.

> [!danger] Knee dislocation — check the vascular supply even if the pulses are normal
> **Tibiofemoral dislocation carries a high risk of POPLITEAL ARTERY injury**, because the artery is tethered at the adductor hiatus and the soleal arch.
> **Normal distal pulses DO NOT exclude an intimal injury that will thrombose over the following hours.**
> **Measure the ankle-brachial index, and obtain CT angiography if it is abnormal or if there is any clinical concern.** **A missed popliteal injury results in amputation.**
> **Note also that a knee dislocation may have spontaneously reduced before arrival** — so a knee with gross multiligamentous laxity after high-energy trauma should be treated as a reduced dislocation.

> [!tip] The lower limb eponyms and patterns
> **· PELVIC FRACTURE** — high energy; **haemorrhage is the immediate threat.** **Apply a pelvic binder at the level of the GREATER TROCHANTERS, not the iliac crests.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.2 and [[L8]].
> **· FEMORAL SHAFT** — significant blood loss, fat embolism risk, and it implies high energy in a young patient (or pathological fracture in an older one).
> **· TIBIAL PLATEAU** — Schatzker classification; **high compartment syndrome risk**, and associated ligamentous and meniscal injury.
> **· TIBIAL SHAFT — the highest risk site for both COMPARTMENT SYNDROME and OPEN fracture**, because the bone is subcutaneous along its anteromedial border.
> **· ANKLE — WEBER classification by the level of the fibular fracture relative to the syndesmosis: A (below, usually stable), B (at the level, variable), C (above, syndesmosis disrupted, unstable).** **Bimalleolar and trimalleolar fractures are unstable.** **Always palpate the whole fibula for a MAISONNEUVE fracture** — cross-refer [[L5]] 0.7.
> **· TALAR NECK — high AVN risk**; the **Hawkins sign** (subchondral lucency at 6–8 weeks) indicates preserved vascularity.
> **· CALCANEUS — axial load, classically a fall from height. ALWAYS IMAGE THE LUMBAR SPINE AND THE OTHER CALCANEUS** — the same axial force causes vertebral compression fractures and bilateral injury, and the spine fracture is missed because attention is on the painful heel. **Böhler angle** is reduced.
> **· LISFRANC (tarsometatarsal) injury — subtle and frequently missed.** **Midfoot swelling with PLANTAR ECCHYMOSIS is highly suggestive.** **Non-weight-bearing films can look normal — weight-bearing or stress views, or CT, are needed**, and a missed Lisfranc injury causes midfoot collapse and disabling arthritis.
> **· FIFTH METATARSAL — distinguish the AVULSION fracture of the tuberosity (from peroneus brevis, heals well, treated symptomatically) from the JONES fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction, which has a POOR BLOOD SUPPLY, a high non-union rate, and often needs fixation, particularly in athletes.**
> **· PATELLA — test the STRAIGHT LEG RAISE**; loss of active extension means the extensor mechanism is disrupted and needs fixation. Cross-refer [[L5]] 0.5.

## 0.5 Paediatric Fractures

> [!tip] Children's bones behave differently
> **· Thicker, more active PERIOSTEUM** — greater stability and faster healing.
> **· GROWTH PLATES (physes)** — a zone of weakness, so ligaments often survive while the physis fails. **A "sprain" in a skeletally immature child is more likely a physeal injury.**
> **· REMODELLING POTENTIAL** — considerable, especially near a growth plate, in the plane of joint movement, and in younger children. **Angulation that would be unacceptable in an adult may be accepted.** **Rotation does NOT remodel.**
> **· More porous bone** → **greenstick (incomplete, one cortex) and buckle/torus (compression of one cortex) patterns.**

> [!info] Salter-Harris classification — SALTR
> **I — Straight across the physis** (may be radiographically normal — diagnosed clinically by physeal tenderness).
> **II — Above: through the physis and up into the METAPHYSIS.** **The commonest.**
> **III — beLow: through the physis and down into the EPIPHYSIS. INTRA-ARTICULAR.**
> **IV — Through: crossing metaphysis, physis and epiphysis. INTRA-ARTICULAR.**
> **V — cRush: compression of the physis. Worst prognosis, frequently missed initially, and often diagnosed retrospectively when growth arrest appears.**
> **Types III and IV are intra-articular and require anatomical reduction. Types IV and V carry the highest risk of GROWTH ARREST**, producing limb length discrepancy or angular deformity — **so physeal injuries need follow-up, and the family should be told why.**

> [!danger] Supracondylar humeral fracture — the paediatric fracture with the most to lose
> **The commonest paediatric elbow fracture, usually from a FOOSH with hyperextension.** Graded by the **Gartland** classification. `UNVERIFIED — the classification.`
> **Neurovascular assessment is critical and must be documented:**
> **· ANTERIOR INTEROSSEOUS NERVE — test the "OK sign" (flexion of the thumb IP and index DIP joints). It is a PURELY MOTOR nerve, so there is no sensory loss and the injury is missed unless specifically tested.**
> **· Median, radial and ulnar nerves.**
> **· BRACHIAL ARTERY — a pulseless, poorly perfused hand requires URGENT reduction and vascular assessment.**
> **· COMPARTMENT SYNDROME → VOLKMANN ISCHAEMIC CONTRACTURE**, a devastating permanent flexion deformity of the forearm and hand.
> **Increasing pain, pain on passive finger extension, and a child requiring escalating analgesia after a supracondylar fracture is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise.**

> [!danger] Non-accidental injury — the features that must trigger action
> **· A history that is INCONSISTENT with the injury, CHANGES between tellings, or is inconsistent between carers.**
> **· A DELAY in presentation without adequate explanation.**
> **· An injury inconsistent with the child's DEVELOPMENTAL STAGE** — **"if they can't cruise, they shouldn't bruise": a femoral or long bone fracture in a NON-AMBULANT infant is highly suspicious.**
> **· MULTIPLE fractures of DIFFERENT AGES.**
> **· Specific high-specificity fractures: METAPHYSEAL CORNER or "bucket-handle" fractures · POSTERIOR RIB fractures · scapular, sternal and spinous process fractures · and classic sites of inflicted injury.**
> **· Other injuries — bruising in unusual sites, burns, torn frenulum, retinal haemorrhage.**
> **· Disclosure by the child, or a carer's demeanour that does not fit.**
> **You do not need to be certain — you need to be concerned.** **Mandatory reporting obligations apply in South Australia, and reporting is a legal duty, not a discretionary judgement.** Cross-refer [[GER4]] and [[M6]]. `UNVERIFIED — the SA mandatory reporting requirements and pathway.`
> **Also consider the differential: osteogenesis imperfecta, rickets, and metabolic bone disease** — which is why a skeletal survey and metabolic workup form part of the assessment rather than an accusation.

> [!tip] The paediatric-specific injuries
> **· TODDLER'S FRACTURE** — an undisplaced spiral fracture of the tibia in a newly ambulant child, often with a trivial or unwitnessed mechanism, presenting as a refusal to weight bear. **May be radiographically subtle or invisible initially.**
> **· BUCKLE (torus) fracture of the distal radius** — very common, stable, and **treated with a removable splint with minimal follow-up**, which is a change from historical casting.
> **· PULLED ELBOW** — cross-refer [[L5]] 0.3.

## 0.6 Fragility Fractures

> [!danger] A minimal trauma fracture over 50 IS a diagnosis of osteoporosis
> **A fracture from a fall from standing height or less identifies a person at high risk of the next fracture — with the risk highest in the period immediately following.**
> **The treatment gap is one of the clearest quality failures in Australian practice: the majority of people who sustain a fragility fracture are never investigated or treated for osteoporosis.** The fracture is fixed and they are discharged.
> **The concrete intern-level action: identify it as a fragility fracture, start calcium and vitamin D, arrange bone density assessment, and NAME the responsible clinician for osteoporosis management in the discharge summary.** Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.6.
> **Fracture liaison services exist to close this gap** — refer where available.

> [!warning] Vertebral fragility fractures are frequently silent
> **Many are found incidentally on a chest radiograph or CT done for another reason, or present only as height loss and progressive kyphosis.**
> **An incidental vertebral fracture is a diagnosis of osteoporosis that is routinely not acted upon** — and it confers a high risk of further fracture. Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.6.

> [!tip] Practical considerations in the older fracture patient
> **· Anticoagulation** — determines the timing of surgery and of any regional anaesthetic. Clarify the agent and the last dose. Cross-refer [[A9]] 0.4 and [[AN1]].
> **· Delirium** — anticipate and prevent. Cross-refer [[D2]] 0.2.
> **· Polypharmacy review** — the fall usually has a cause, and it is frequently in the drug chart. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.4.
> **· Consider a PATHOLOGICAL fracture** — a fracture from minimal trauma in an unusual site, with pre-existing pain, or in a patient with a malignancy history. **Myeloma and metastatic disease present this way.** Cross-refer [[J4]] and [[B6]] 0.8.

## 0.7 Open Fractures and Orthopaedic Emergencies

> [!danger] Open fracture management — the sequence
> **1. A–E, with control of haemorrhage.**
> **2. Assess and DOCUMENT neurovascular status.**
> **3. PHOTOGRAPH the wound once** — so that repeated exposure is unnecessary.
> **4. Remove gross contamination only. Do NOT scrub or irrigate extensively in the emergency department** — definitive debridement happens in theatre.
> **5. Cover with a saline-soaked sterile dressing, then an occlusive layer, and DO NOT KEEP TAKING IT OFF.** Repeated exposure increases infection risk, and each new clinician wanting a look adds to it.
> **6. ANTIBIOTICS EARLY — time to antibiotics is a key determinant of infection risk.**
> **7. TETANUS assessment and prophylaxis.** Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.5.
> **8. SPLINT and realign** — restoring gross alignment improves perfusion and reduces pain.
> **9. URGENT orthopaedic referral, with plastic surgery involvement for significant soft tissue loss.**
> **The Gustilo-Anderson classification** grades severity by wound size, contamination and soft tissue injury. `UNVERIFIED — the classification, the antibiotic regimen, and the time targets for antibiotics and debridement.`

> [!danger] The orthopaedic emergencies — the list worth knowing cold
> **· COMPARTMENT SYNDROME** — pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch. **Pulses present until late.** Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6.
> **· OPEN FRACTURE** — as above.
> **· SEPTIC ARTHRITIS** — cross-refer [[L1]] 0.2.
> **· CAUDA EQUINA SYNDROME** — cross-refer [[L4]] 0.2.
> **· NECROTISING SOFT TISSUE INFECTION** — cross-refer [[K2]] 0.2.
> **· ANY DISLOCATION OR FRACTURE WITH NEUROVASCULAR COMPROMISE** — reduce urgently; realignment often restores perfusion.
> **· HIP DISLOCATION — native or prosthetic. AVN risk rises with time to reduction**, so this is a same-hour problem. **Posterior dislocation risks SCIATIC nerve injury — test ankle dorsiflexion and sensation.**
> **· KNEE DISLOCATION — popliteal artery injury, with normal pulses not excluding it.**
> **· SUPRACONDYLAR FRACTURE with a pulseless hand.**
> **· UNSTABLE PELVIC FRACTURE with haemorrhage** — binder, and activate the massive transfusion protocol. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.2 and [[A9]] 0.1.
> **· SPINAL CORD INJURY, and any spinal injury in a FUSED spine.** Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.7.
> **· METASTATIC SPINAL CORD COMPRESSION** — cross-refer [[L4]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Document neurovascular status before and after everything
> **Before and after: reduction, splinting, casting, and transfer.**
> **Name the specific nerves and their function** — "radial nerve: wrist extension intact, first web space sensation intact" — rather than "NV intact".
> **A deficit found later, with no baseline documentation, cannot be attributed and is a medicolegal problem as well as a clinical one.**

**Ix:** **Radiographs — two orthogonal views including the joint above and below** (*why:* the fundamental requirement, and it catches associated dislocation; *what:* fracture, alignment, dislocation). CT (*why:* intra-articular fractures, complex anatomy (pelvis, calcaneus, spine), and pre-operative planning; *what:* articular step-off, comminution). MRI (*why:* radiographically occult fractures — scaphoid, hip, stress fractures — and associated soft tissue injury; *what:* marrow oedema, fracture line). **Ankle-brachial index and CT angiography where vascular injury is possible** (*why:* normal pulses do not exclude intimal injury in knee dislocation and penetrating trauma; *what:* arterial injury). FBC, group and hold or crossmatch, coagulation, UEC (*why:* blood loss, and preparation for surgery; *what:* baseline). **Calcium, vitamin D and bone profile in fragility fracture** (*why:* the fracture is the diagnosis of osteoporosis; *what:* deficiency and secondary causes). **Skeletal survey and metabolic bone workup where non-accidental injury is a concern** (*why:* identifies occult fractures of different ages and excludes metabolic bone disease; *what:* multiple fractures, healing at different stages).
