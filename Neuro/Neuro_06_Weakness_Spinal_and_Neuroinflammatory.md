## Weakness — Differential Diagnosis

### No objective weakness
Most often presents as a global pattern, ?generalised fatigue. Causes: cardio-pulmonary disease, anaemia, chronic infection (e.g. TB), malignancy, depression, deconditioning, arthritis, fibromyalgia, endocrine disease.

### With objective weakness — perform neurological exam

**UMN signs (↑tone, ↑reflexes):**
- Unilateral: intracranial haemorrhage, brainstem stroke, spinal cord stroke (all acute-onset)
- Bilateral: multiple sclerosis, motor neuron disease, myelopathy (disc prolapse, transverse myelitis, space-occupying lesion), brainstem stroke

**LMN signs (↓tone, ↓reflexes):** further divided by whether there are sensory changes.

**Mixed UMN & LMN signs:** ALS, cervical myelo-radiculopathy, syringomyelia

**No sensory changes — motor neuron and motor neuropathy:**
- Lead toxicity — classically affects wrist and finger extensors first, then spreads proximally
- Progressive muscular atrophy — MND subset involving only LMN
- Hodgkin's lymphoma — secondary to neoplastic infiltration
- Polio — CNS infection destroys motor neurons, causing acute flaccid paralysis
- Multifocal motor neuropathy — acquired progressive disorder with asymmetric weakness and no sensory problems (UL>LL)
- Spinal muscular atrophy — autosomal recessive, 4 subtypes with varying severity

**Neuromuscular junction:**
- Myasthenia gravis — antibody against post-synaptic nicotinic ACh receptor; associated with thymoma; fatiguability (increasing contractions → decreasing strength)
- Lambert-Eaton syndrome — antibody against pre-synaptic voltage-gated Ca channel; associated with small cell lung cancer; increasing contractions → increasing strength (contrast with MG)
- Botulism — secondary to *Clostridium botulinum* toxin; causes flaccid paralysis
- Congenital myasthenic syndromes

**Myopathy:**
- Polymyositis and dermatomyositis — proximal muscle weakness ± tenderness; associated with malignancies
- Duchenne muscular dystrophy — X-linked recessive, dystrophin gene damage; progressive weakness from age 5 + calf pseudohypertrophy (Becker MD is a less severe variant)
- Statin toxicity — usually proximal muscles first ± myalgia ± dark urine
- Viral infection — infectious or post-infectious myositis (e.g. HIV, influenza, EBV)

**With sensory changes — does it follow a nerve root/nerve distribution?**

**Radiculopathy** (follows nerve root distribution — mechanical compression of a nerve root): disc prolapse (?acute), spondylosis, spinal cord tumour, infection (e.g. discitis, abscess)

**Mononeuropathy:** compression (carpal tunnel syndrome, cubital tunnel syndrome, radial tunnel syndrome, peroneal nerve); other (trauma, space-occupying lesion, ischaemia — all can be acute)

**Mononeuritis multiplex** (nerve lesions in ≥2 named nerves in separate body parts): vasculitis (e.g. affecting right peroneal and median nerves → right foot drop + carpal tunnel syndrome), diabetes

**Polyneuropathy:**
- Nutritional (e.g. thiamine deficiency)
- Alcohol (reduced nutrition ± inflammatory effect on neurons)
- Toxins (e.g. chemotherapy, isoniazid) — generally length-dependent
- Paraproteinaemic (e.g. secondary to haematologic malignancy)

### Diabetic Neuropathy

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely conspicuous by its absence given diabetic retinopathy (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Diabetic Retinopathy) and diabetic nephropathy (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Diabetic Nephropathy) both have dedicated entries as the other two classic diabetic microvascular complications, while neuropathy — the third — was previously only a passing mention here (diabetic amyotrophy specifically) and within [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Diabetic Foot. Verified against the American Diabetes Association's position statement on diabetic neuropathy and current international expert consensus, Aug 2026.

**D:** nerve damage attributable to diabetes, encompassing several genuinely distinct clinical patterns rather than a single uniform condition — the classification below matters because each pattern has a different natural history and, in some cases, different management.

> [!info] Classification — four genuinely distinct patterns:
> - **Distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN):** by far the most common pattern, affecting up to half of people with diabetes over their lifetime — the "glove and stocking" distribution already referenced in the Diabetic Foot entry, not repeated here.
> - **Autonomic neuropathy:** affects the autonomic nervous system rather than sensorimotor function — cardiovascular (resting tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension — see Orthostatic syncope above, not repeated here), gastrointestinal (gastroparesis, diabetic diarrhoea/constipation), genitourinary (erectile dysfunction, neurogenic bladder), and sudomotor (reduced sweating contributing to dry, fissure-prone skin — see Diabetic Foot in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]], not repeated here) manifestations.
> - **Mononeuropathy:** compression neuropathies (e.g. carpal tunnel syndrome) occur with genuinely increased frequency in diabetes, alongside acute cranial mononeuropathies — classically **CN III palsy with pupillary sparing**, given diabetic microvascular disease preferentially affects the nerve's central fibres while sparing the peripherally-located pupillomotor fibres (already established in [[05_Ophthalmology]] Ocular Manifestations of Systemic Disease — Consolidated Reference, not repeated here).
> - **Proximal diabetic neuropathy (diabetic amyotrophy):** affects nerves supplying the thighs, hips, buttocks ± lower limbs → muscle wasting, weakness, and pain, secondary to lumbosacral plexus damage — genuinely different from the other patterns given its often asymmetric, subacute presentation rather than the classic symmetric distal pattern.

**S/Smx (DSPN specifically, given it's the pattern most commonly tested and encountered):** sensory symptoms predominate — burning, tingling, electric-shock-like sensations ("positive" symptoms), alongside numbness and sensory loss ("negative" symptoms) in the classic glove-and-stocking distribution, worse at night; a genuinely important safety point already established in Diabetic Foot is that sensory loss means injury can go unnoticed, directly driving the foot ulceration risk built in that entry.

**Ix:** primarily clinical — annual foot/sensory screening (already established as part of Diabetic Foot surveillance, not repeated here) using simple bedside tools (10g monofilament testing, vibration sense); nerve conduction studies reserved for atypical presentations, asymmetric findings, or where an alternative cause is genuinely suspected (given routine DSPN doesn't require electrophysiological confirmation).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** optimise glycaemic control — genuinely the only intervention with evidence for slowing progression of the underlying neuropathy itself, though this doesn't reverse established nerve damage; the remaining Mx below is symptomatic (pain control) rather than disease-modifying.
- **Definitive (pharmacological management of painful DSPN):** current international consensus (including the ADA's own position statement) recommends **pregabalin or duloxetine as first-line**, given both have the strongest evidence base and are specifically approved for this indication; gabapentin is a reasonable alternative first-line option, particularly where cost or drug interactions favour it over pregabalin. Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline) are also effective but used with more caution given a higher risk of significant side effects, particularly in older patients or those with cardiac disease. **Suboptimal pain relief on monotherapy is genuinely common** (response achieved in only around half of patients) — combination therapy (a gabapentinoid plus an antidepressant) is a reasonable next step for inadequate response to a single first-line agent, rather than simply escalating the dose of the same drug indefinitely.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing foot care and injury-prevention education given the established sensory loss (see Diabetic Foot, not repeated here); management of the specific autonomic manifestations as they arise (e.g. prokinetics for gastroparesis, fludrocortisone/midodrine for significant orthostatic hypotension) rather than a single unified autonomic-neuropathy treatment.

**P:** established sensorimotor neuropathy is generally **not reversible** even with subsequent excellent glycaemic control — this is precisely why early detection (via the annual screening above) and prevention (glycaemic control from diagnosis) matter more than treatment once neuropathy is established, and why patient education about the irreversibility is genuinely important for motivating early, sustained glycaemic control rather than only after symptoms appear.


- Inherited (e.g. Charcot-Marie-Tooth)
- Inflammatory (e.g. Guillain-Barré syndrome)

**Plexopathy:** brachial neuritis (immune-mediated inflammatory process, 80% unilateral onset, pain + muscle atrophy), diabetes (e.g. lumbosacral plexopathy), space-occupying lesion

**Poly-radiculopathy:** spondylolysis (vertebral fracture), chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, space-occupying lesion, infection

## Spinal Disorders

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Neurology category, High/Medium yield) — Cauda Equina Syndrome and Malignant Spinal Cord Compression were not covered in the source notes despite being genuine spinal emergencies distinct from the anatomical/localising syndromes below; added here as full entries given their clinical importance.

### Cauda Equina Syndrome

**D:** compression of the cauda equina — the bundle of lumbosacral nerve roots (L2–S5) below the level the spinal cord itself ends (~L1/L2) — causing a distinct LMN (not UMN) pattern of deficit, since these are peripheral nerve roots rather than spinal cord.

**R:** large central disc prolapse (most common cause), spinal stenosis, spinal trauma, spinal tumour (primary or metastatic), spinal epidural abscess/haematoma, ankylosing spondylitis, post-surgical/iatrogenic.

**A/P:** mechanical compression of multiple lumbosacral nerve roots simultaneously within the narrow confines of the spinal canal below the conus medullaris.

> [!note] [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] Cauda equina syndrome carries a second full entry, consistent with this one. Linked by the pairs audit (2026-08-29).

**S/Smx:**
> [!danger] Red flag symptoms — ask directly, since patients may not volunteer these
> Bilateral leg pain/sciatica; saddle anaesthesia (numbness of the perineum, buttocks, inner thighs — ask specifically, e.g. "would you feel a wipe after using the toilet?"); bladder dysfunction (urinary retention with overflow incontinence is the most sensitive sign, though the patient may report incontinence rather than retention); bowel dysfunction (reduced anal tone, faecal incontinence); sexual dysfunction (new erectile dysfunction, loss of sensation); bilateral lower limb weakness and/or sensory loss; reduced/absent lower limb reflexes (LMN pattern, distinguishing this from a cord-level compressive lesion causing UMN signs).

**Ix:** urgent MRI whole spine (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test, and genuinely time-critical — this is one of the few neurological presentations where imaging delay directly translates to worse functional outcome; *what:* identifies the compressive lesion, its level, and cause). Bladder scan (*why:* quantifies post-void residual volume, supporting the diagnosis and providing an objective, trackable marker — a residual >100–200mL is concerning; *what:* elevated post-void residual supports bladder dysfunction from cauda equina compression rather than an incidental finding). PR exam (*why:* assesses anal tone and perianal sensation directly — a reduced/absent anal tone is a significant red flag; *what:* reduced tone or saddle sensory loss supports the diagnosis).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** this is a **surgical emergency** — urgent same-day neurosurgical/orthopaedic spinal referral for emergency decompression (typically discectomy), since the window for meaningful neurological recovery is time-dependent; analgesia; catheterise if in retention.
- **Definitive:** surgical decompression, ideally within 24–48h of symptom onset — earlier decompression is associated with better recovery of bladder/bowel/sexual function, though some recovery is possible even with delayed surgery.
- **Chronic/long-term:** ongoing bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction rehabilitation if deficits persist post-decompression (a genuine possibility even with prompt treatment) — pelvic floor physiotherapy, urology follow-up, psychological support given the significant quality-of-life impact.

**P:** even with prompt surgery, some patients have permanent bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction — earlier decompression improves but doesn't guarantee full recovery, making this a genuine "time is function" emergency similar in spirit to "time is brain" in stroke.

### Malignant Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC)

**D:** compression of the spinal cord itself (above the level of the cauda equina, i.e. involving the cord rather than nerve roots) by a malignant process — most commonly vertebral metastases, though can also arise from direct extension or intramedullary tumour.

**R:** known malignancy (breast, prostate, lung, myeloma, and renal cell carcinoma are the most common primaries causing vertebral metastases), new back pain in a patient with a cancer history, no known cancer history but with other red flags for malignancy.

**A/P:** vertebral body metastasis → collapse or direct extension into the epidural space → mechanical compression of the spinal cord and/or its vascular supply → oedema, ischaemia, and ultimately irreversible cord damage if untreated.

**S/Smx:** progressive back pain (often the earliest symptom, may precede neurological signs by weeks — classically worse lying down/at night, and worse with coughing/straining/movement, distinguishing it from mechanical back pain); UMN signs below the level of compression (weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia, upgoing plantars — this UMN pattern is the key distinguishing feature from cauda equina syndrome's LMN pattern, since MSCC compresses the cord itself); sensory level (a defined level below which sensation is impaired); bladder/bowel dysfunction (typically a later sign than in cauda equina syndrome, though can still occur).

> [!danger] Any patient with known malignancy presenting with new back pain should be assumed to have MSCC until proven otherwise — even in the absence of neurological signs, since pain frequently precedes deficit and early treatment before deficit develops gives the best chance of preserving function.

**Ix:** urgent whole-spine MRI (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test — whole spine imaging is needed since metastatic disease is frequently multifocal, and a lesion may exist at a different level from where symptoms localise; *what:* identifies the compressive lesion(s), extent, and any additional asymptomatic sites requiring inclusion in the treatment field). Bloods incl. calcium (*why:* screens for malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia, a relevant differential/co-existing emergency in cancer patients with neurological symptoms; *what:* may be elevated, particularly with myeloma or extensive bony metastases).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** high-dose dexamethasone started immediately on clinical suspicion, before imaging confirmation, given the drug's low harm profile and the time-critical nature of preserving neurological function — do not wait for MRI to start steroids if MSCC is clinically suspected; urgent oncology/spinal surgery/radiation oncology referral for definitive Mx planning; analgesia; VTE prophylaxis given reduced mobility.
- **Definitive:** radiotherapy (the mainstay for most patients, particularly with radiosensitive tumours and where surgery isn't indicated) ± surgical decompression/stabilisation (considered for spinal instability, single-level disease with good prognosis, or radioresistant tumours) — the specific approach depends on tumour type, extent, patient fitness, and prognosis, decided by a multidisciplinary team.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation for any residual deficit; ongoing oncological management of the underlying malignancy; palliative care involvement given this usually indicates advanced/metastatic disease.

**P:** neurological function at the time treatment starts is the strongest predictor of outcome — patients who are still ambulatory at treatment onset are far more likely to remain so than those who have already lost the ability to walk, reinforcing why pain alone (before deficit develops) should prompt urgent action rather than waiting for neurological signs to appear.

### Brown-Séquard Syndrome

**D:** hemisection of the spinal cord (incomplete transverse injury).

**Tracts affected:**
- Lateral corticospinal tract — ipsilateral weakness below the lesion
- Dorsal columns — ipsilateral loss of proprioception and vibration sensation
- Lateral spinothalamic tract — contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation

### Subacute Combined Degeneration of the Spinal Cord

> [!warning] FLAG 2026-09-01 — B₁₂ deficiency straddles Neuro and Heme Onc
> `Heme Onc_merged 10_06a ## Vitamin B12 deficiency` owns the haematology, and
> `NEW_Investigations_Haematology` owns the B12/MMA/homocysteine/anti-IF work-up.
> **The neurological presentation justifies this entry staying.** Flagged, not moved.

**D:** demyelination of the lateral and posterior columns of the spinal cord.

**A:** vitamin B12 deficiency, recreational nitrous oxide inhalation (deactivates B12 from its active form).

> [!info] The mechanism — genuinely worth understanding, given it explains both why B12 deficiency causes this and folate deficiency essentially never does, despite both nutrients feeding into the same methionine cycle. **B12 (cobalamin) is a cofactor for two separate enzymes**, and this dual role is the key to the distinction: (1) **methionine synthase** (cytoplasmic) converts homocysteine to methionine, using folate (as 5-methyltetrahydrofolate) as the methyl donor — methionine then becomes S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), required for methylating myelin basic protein and maintaining the myelin sheath; (2) **methylmalonyl-CoA mutase** (mitochondrial) converts methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA, a step required for normal fatty acid metabolism and myelin lipid synthesis. **Folate is only involved in the first reaction, not the second** — so folate deficiency impairs SAM/myelin methylation similarly to B12 deficiency (which is why both cause identical megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia), but **doesn't touch the methylmalonyl-CoA mutase pathway at all**, sparing this second route of myelin damage that only B12 deficiency causes. This is also why **methylmalonic acid (MMA) is elevated specifically in B12 deficiency, not folate deficiency** — a genuinely useful distinguishing lab test between the two when the diagnosis is unclear.
> **This also explains the danger of giving folic acid to a patient with undiagnosed B12 deficiency** (already flagged in [[10_06a_Haemonc_-_Macrocytic_Anaemia]] Vitamin B12 deficiency, not repeated here): folic acid accelerates DNA synthesis and cell division, increasing demand for methionine — this further depletes an already-scarce SAM pool in a B12-deficient patient (since methionine synthase, the B12-dependent step, is already impaired), worsening myelin methylation and precipitating or accelerating SCD, even while apparently "treating" the anaemia.
> **A genuine, honest caveat**: while SCD is overwhelmingly a B12-deficiency disease, rare case reports describe SCD from folate deficiency alone — so "only B12 causes this" is a very strong rule of thumb rather than an absolute one.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Dorsal columns — tingling, burning, or sensory loss (symmetrical, lower limb>upper limb); impaired proprioception and vibration sense
- Lateral corticospinal tracts — muscle weakness, hyperreflexia, spasticity; UMN signs lower limb first, then upper limb (brisk knee reflexes, absent ankle jerks, extensor plantars)
- Spinocerebellar tracts — sensory ataxia and gait changes, positive Romberg's sign

> [!note] Gap-filled — this entry had an unusually thorough mechanism explanation above but genuinely no Ix section, jumping straight from S/Smx to Mx.

**Ix:** serum B12 level (*why:* the first-line test given the mechanism above; *what:* low, though a genuinely important caveat is that levels can be falsely normal/borderline in early or functional deficiency). Serum methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine (*why:* directly reflects the two B12-dependent enzyme pathways established above — elevated MMA specifically indicates B12 deficiency rather than folate deficiency, per the mechanism box; *what:* both elevated in true B12 deficiency, useful when serum B12 itself is equivocal). FBC and blood film (*why:* screens for the associated megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia, though a genuinely important point is that the neurological features of SCD can occur *without* anaemia being present, so a normal FBC doesn't exclude the diagnosis; *what:* macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils if the haematological manifestation is also present). MRI spine (*why:* not required for diagnosis in a straightforward case, but supports it and excludes structural mimics if the picture is atypical or diagnostic doubt remains; *what:* may show characteristic dorsal/lateral column T2 hyperintensity, an "inverted V" sign on axial imaging).

**Mx:** stop trigger, B12 replacement.

### Friedreich's Ataxia

**D:** autosomal recessive trinucleotide repeat disorder.

**S/Smx:** onset 10–15yo. Cerebellar ataxia and spinocerebellar tract degeneration — sensory ataxia, gait changes, positive Romberg's sign. Optic atrophy. Hyporeflexia — absent ankle jerks and extensor plantar reflexes. Other: cardiomyopathy (90% of patients, most common cause of death), DM, kyphoscoliosis, foot deformities, high-arched palate.

**Ix:** genetic testing (*why:* confirms the diagnosis definitively, identifying the GAA trinucleotide repeat expansion in the FXN gene; *what:* confirms the diagnosis and repeat length, which correlates loosely with severity/age of onset). Echocardiogram (*why:* screens for the cardiomyopathy that is the leading cause of death in this condition, warranting active surveillance rather than waiting for symptoms; *what:* may show hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). Glucose/HbA1c (*why:* screens for the associated diabetes; *what:* may show impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes).

**Mx:** supportive and multidisciplinary — physiotherapy for gait/mobility, cardiology surveillance and management for the cardiomyopathy (the main driver of mortality, so this is not an incidental association to overlook), diabetes management if present, orthopaedic input for scoliosis/foot deformities; genetic counselling for family members given the autosomal recessive inheritance.

### Anterior Spinal Artery Occlusion

**D:** ischaemic infarction of the anterior spinal artery (artery of Adamkiewicz) or generalised hypoperfusion. The artery of Adamkiewicz is the only major arterial supply to the anterior spinal artery along the lower two-thirds of the spine.

**A:** aortic surgery, atherosclerotic disease, emboli, vasculitis (many causes).

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Lateral corticospinal tracts — bilateral spastic paresis
- Lateral spinothalamic tracts — bilateral loss of pain and temperature sensation
- Other: acutely can cause spinal shock; later findings include neurogenic bladder/bowel, sexual dysfunction

**Ix:** MRI spine (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the characteristic infarct pattern and excluding a compressive alternative cause; *what:* T2 hyperintensity in the anterior cord distribution, sparing the dorsal columns — consistent with the vascular territory and correlating with the dorsal-column-sparing clinical picture above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** treat the underlying cause where identifiable (e.g. manage the aortic pathology if surgery-related, address the embolic source); supportive care for spinal shock if present.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation; Mx of neurogenic bladder/bowel and other chronic spinal cord injury complications.

### Syringomyelia

**D:** collection of CSF within the spinal cord (= syrinx). Syringobulbia = fluid-filled cavity within the medulla of the brainstem.

**A:** Chiari malformation (type 1), trauma, tumours, idiopathic.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Ventral horns — flaccid paresis (usually starting with intrinsic hand muscles)
- Lateral spinothalamic tract — loss of pain and temperature sensation, classically in a 'cape-like' pattern (neck, shoulders, arms)
- Other: spastic weakness of lower limbs, neuropathic pain, upgoing plantars, autonomic features (neurogenic bowel/bladder), scoliosis in the long term

**Ix:** full spine MRI + brain MRI (to look for Chiari malformation).

**Mx:** treat underlying cause; if persistent or symptomatic, shunt into the syrinx to drain.

### Tabes Dorsalis

**D:** syndrome secondary to CNS infection by *Treponema pallidum*.

**Tracts affected + S/Smx:**
- Dorsal columns and roots — loss of proprioception and vibratory sense; ataxia, lancinating pains, bladder dysfunction, paraesthesias
- Other neuro symptoms: pupillary abnormalities (Argyll Robertson pupils), ocular palsies, decreased reflexes, Charcot joints

> [!note] Classically known as "the great imitator" — requires a degree of clinical suspicion.

**Ix:** serology — treponemal (e.g. TPPA) and non-treponemal (e.g. RPR/VDRL) tests, LP with CSF VDRL (*why:* confirms neurosyphilis specifically, since tabes dorsalis is a late manifestation of untreated syphilis and CSF involvement must be demonstrated to guide the correct (neurosyphilis-dose) antibiotic regimen; *what:* positive serology supports syphilis exposure, CSF findings confirm CNS involvement).

**Mx:** IV benzylpenicillin (neurosyphilis-specific high-dose regimen, given standard-dose penicillin for early syphilis doesn't adequately penetrate the CNS) — supportive Mx for established neurological deficits, which may not fully reverse even with adequate treatment given the demyelination is often established by the time of diagnosis.

### Neurofibromatosis

**D:** neurocutaneous genetic disorder, autosomal dominantly inherited — NF1 (more common) and NF2.

**R:** family history

**A:** NF1 — mutation on the gene encoding neurofibromin, located on chromosome 17. NF2 — mutation on chromosome 22.

**P:** decreased neurofibromin in Schwann cells, glia, melanocytes, mast cells, vascular endothelial and smooth cells → dysplasias and neoplasias.

**S/Smx of NF1:** café-au-lait spots (≥6 spots, flat light brown birthmarks); axillary/groin freckles; peripheral neurofibromas (tumours hanging off the skin); iris Lisch nodules; scoliosis; phaeochromocytomas.

**S/Smx of NF2:** bilateral vestibular schwannomas; multiple intracranial schwannomas, meningiomas, and ependymomas.

**Ix:** MRI/CT scans, genetic testing to confirm NF1.

**Mx:** symptomatic (as/when features cause problems); neurofibromas need surveillance and removal if assessed to be malignant.

### Tuberous Sclerosis

**D:** neurocutaneous and multi-systemic genetic disorder, autosomal dominantly inherited.

**R:** family history

**A/P:** mutations of TSC1 and TSC2 genes → cellular hyperplasia, tissue dysplasia, multi-organ hamartomas.

**S/Smx:** hypopigmented "ash-leaf" spots (fluoresce under UV light); roughened patches of skin over the lumbar spine (Shagreen patches); adenoma sebaceum (angiofibromas distributed over the nose); fibromata beneath nails; café-au-lait spots.

**Neuro problems:** developmental delay, epilepsy (infantile spasms), intellectual impairment, retinal hamartomas, rhabdomyomas of the heart, gliomatous changes of the brain.

**Other:** polycystic kidneys, renal angiomyolipomata, lymphangioleiomyomatosis (multiple lung cysts).

**Ix:** as per neurofibromatosis.

**Mx:** as per neurofibromatosis; mTOR inhibitors everolimus and sirolimus approved for some indications under tuberous sclerosis.

## Neuroinflammatory Disorders

### Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

**D:** demyelinating CNS condition clinically defined by ≥2 episodes of neurological dysfunction (brain, spinal cord, or optic nerves) separated in space and time.

**R:** F>M (3:1), family history, northern latitude, autoimmune disorders. "Typical patient": 20–40yo white female.

**A:** genetics + environment

**P:** activated T cells against an unknown antigen → cascade of inflammation, demyelination, and axonal loss. Multifocal areas of demyelination, decreased oligodendrocytes, loss of axons in the white matter of the CNS.
- If episode(s) not disseminated in time/space — clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)
- Relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) — 85%; acute attacks (1–2 months) followed by remission
- Secondary progressive — 65% of RRMS progress to this, generally within 15 years of diagnosis
- Primary progressive — no RRMS period, just deterioration

**S/Smx:** significant lethargy. Visual: optic neuritis, optic atrophy, Uhthoff's phenomenon (worsening vision with rise in body temperature), internuclear ophthalmoplegia. Sensory: pins and needles, numbness, trigeminal neuralgia, Lhermitte's phenomenon (paraesthesiae in limbs on neck flexion). Motor: spastic weakness, especially in legs. Cerebellar: ataxia, tremor. Others: urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, intellectual deterioration.

> [!info] Gap-filled — Uhthoff's and Lhermitte's phenomena were both named without explaining the mechanism, despite both stemming directly from the same demyelination process already established above (P:), just triggered by two different physical stimuli. **The shared foundation**: demyelinated axons lose their normal myelin insulation, which both slows/impairs their electrical conduction and makes them **abnormally sensitive to physical perturbation** — a normal, myelinated axon simply doesn't respond this way to temperature change or mechanical stretch. **Uhthoff's phenomenon (thermal sensitivity)**: nerve conduction velocity is temperature-dependent, and demyelinated fibres — already operating with a reduced safety margin for successful action potential propagation — have their conduction pushed below the threshold needed for reliable transmission by even a small rise in body temperature (from exercise, a hot shower, or fever), producing transient worsening of symptoms (classically visual, given optic nerve involvement) that resolves once temperature normalises. **Lhermitte's phenomenon (mechanical sensitivity)**: demyelinated axons in the cervical dorsal columns become mechanically irritable — neck flexion stretches the spinal cord, and this mechanical deformation triggers **ectopic (abnormal, spontaneous) action potentials** in the exposed, unstable axonal membrane, perceived as an electric-shock-like sensation radiating down the spine and limbs. Both are genuinely useful clinical signs precisely because they reveal the presence of demyelinated tissue through its abnormal responsiveness to an otherwise unremarkable stimulus (a hot shower, a neck movement) — not because the stimulus itself is doing anything unusual.

**Ix:** MRI (brain and spinal cord) (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the dissemination in space (lesions in ≥2 typical locations) and time (new/enhancing lesions on follow-up, or simultaneous enhancing and non-enhancing lesions) required for diagnosis; *what:* multifocal white matter lesions, classically periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, or spinal cord). LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis when MRI findings are equivocal, particularly by demonstrating intrathecal antibody production; *what:* oligoclonal bands present in CSF but not serum — indicating CNS-specific antibody synthesis). Visual evoked potentials (*why:* detects subclinical optic nerve demyelination even when the patient has no current visual symptoms, supporting dissemination in space; *what:* delayed conduction velocity, indicating prior/subclinical optic neuritis).

> **Spasticity:** baclofen and gabapentin first-line; others — diazepam, dantrolene. No AU-specific change identified.
>
> **Urinary incontinence:** bladder US to assess emptying; if significant residual volume, intermittent self-catheterisation; if not, anticholinergics may help. No AU-specific change identified.

**P:** variable prognosis. Negative prognostic factors: frequent relapses, motor or cerebellar onset, increased MRI lesion burden.

### Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

**D:** acute inflammatory polyneuropathy, divided into axonal and demyelinating forms.

**R:** preceding infection (viral, bacterial, mosquito-borne, hepatitis E) ~6 weeks before, especially associated with *Campylobacter jejuni* and CMV

**A/P:** immune-mediated attack on the myelin sheath or Schwann cells of sensory and motor nerves, via cellular and humoral immune mechanisms; cross-reaction of antibodies to gangliosides.

**S/Smx:** progressive symmetrical muscle weakness of all limbs — legs first then arms (ascending); flaccid paralysis; areflexia; paraesthesia in feet and hands. Acute development over 2–4 weeks, then plateau. Mild dysautonomia (2/3 of patients): urinary retention, ileus, respiratory failure (20–30% require mechanical ventilation). Cranial nerve involvement: diplopia, bilateral facial nerve palsy, oropharyngeal weakness. Rarely, papilloedema.

**Ix:** LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis via a characteristic pattern; *what:* ↑protein with normal WBC — "albuminocytological dissociation," a classic and specific finding distinguishing GBS from an infective process, which would typically show pleocytosis). Nerve conduction studies (*why:* confirms the demyelinating (or axonal) neuropathy and helps predict prognosis (electrically inexcitable nerves are a poor prognostic sign — see below); *what:* ↓motor conduction velocity, consistent with demyelination).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IVIG or plasma exchange first-line (equally effective, choice often based on availability/patient factors — no added benefit to combining them); supportive Mx is critical given the risk of respiratory failure — regular FVC monitoring, VTE prophylaxis (given the immobility/dysautonomia risk), mechanical ventilation PRN if respiratory muscles become involved.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation once the acute phase plateaus and recovery begins — physiotherapy/occupational therapy given the potential for prolonged recovery even in patients with a good ultimate prognosis.

> [!danger] **Why the monitoring is FVC and not oximetry — the single most important thing to understand about neuromuscular weakness, and the error is being reassured.** **FVC = forced vital capacity**, the volume a patient can breathe out in one maximal effort. Here it is measured serially at the bedside, which is a different use from the FEV1/FVC *ratio* used to classify obstructive vs restrictive lung disease (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Spirometry and Peak Flow).
>
> *The mechanism:* in GBS and myasthenia **the lungs are normal** — it is the respiratory *pump* that is failing. Gas exchange across a healthy alveolar membrane stays efficient right up until the bellows give out, so **oxygen saturation and CO₂ stay normal until immediately before the patient decompensates**, and then both change abruptly. A patient who is tiring, using accessory muscles and unable to complete a sentence, with saturations of 98%, is not reassuring — the saturation is measuring the wrong organ.
>
> **What to do instead:** serial FVC, at defined intervals, with the trend recorded. It falls progressively and gives hours of warning that the numbers everyone looks at do not. The commonly taught escalation trigger is the **"20/30/40 rule"** — VC below ~20 mL/kg, maximal inspiratory pressure below 30 cmH₂O, maximal expiratory pressure below 40 — prompting ICU review for likely intubation. Treat the figures as the trigger for a conversation with ICU, not a threshold to wait for: **a falling trend matters more than any single value**, and intubation in this setting is planned, not emergent, if the trend is being watched.
>
> Also watch for the bedside signs that precede any number: inability to count aloud to 20 in one breath, a weak cough (which predicts failure to clear secretions), and bulbar weakness — the last of which threatens the airway independently of the FVC.

> [!info] **IVIG — what it is, and why its use here is not the same as the immunoglobulin given after an exposure.** **IVIG = intravenous immunoglobulin**, pooled human antibody given by infusion. The corpus uses the abbreviation in eight files and had never expanded it.
>
> The distinction matters because the *reason* for giving it differs completely. In post-exposure use — VZIG, tetanus or rabies immunoglobulin, anti-D — the antibody is **targeted at one thing and does the work directly** (see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Passive Immunisation — Immunoglobulin After an Exposure). In GBS, CIDP, myasthenic crisis, ITP and Kawasaki disease, **high-dose IVIG is an immunomodulator**: the point is not what the antibodies bind, it is that a large load of pooled immunoglobulin dampens the patient's own autoantibody-driven attack. Hence the dose is far larger, the indication is autoimmune rather than infective, and there is no "window" to meet.
>
> *The practical consequence for an intern:* IVIG here is a **treatment**, not a prophylaxis, so it is not time-critical in the same way — but it is a blood product, so it carries the same consent, documentation and traceability requirements (see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]]), and supply is limited enough that it is usually authorised rather than simply ordered.

**P:** 85% good functional recovery. Negative prognostic factors: severe weakness, rapid onset, ↑age, muscle wasting, electrically inexcitable nerves, preceding diarrhoeal illness.

> [!note] Miller-Fisher syndrome — subset of GBS with ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and areflexia; usually descending paralysis.

### Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating (Poly)Neuropathy (CIDP)

**D:** acquired demyelinating peripheral neuropathy of presumed autoimmune aetiology.

**R:** M>F, autoimmune conditions, DM, infection, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS)

**A:** unclear; 20–30% preceded by infection, immunisation, or surgical procedure.

**P:** autoimmune, cell-mediated disease against myelin.

**S/Smx (typical CIDP):** chronically progressive, step-wise, or recurrent condition; symmetrical proximal and distal weakness and sensory dysfunction of all extremities; develops over ≥2 months; decreased or absent tendon reflexes in all extremities. Many variants exist.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* the key diagnostic test, demonstrating the demyelinating pattern and distinguishing CIDP from GBS mainly by the chronic (≥2 month) time course rather than the electrophysiological pattern itself, which can look similar; *what:* slow conduction, ↑latency, conduction block). LP (*why:* supports the diagnosis via a similar albuminocytological-dissociation-type pattern to GBS, reflecting the same underlying immune-mediated mechanism; *what:* WCC <10, protein >45 mg/dL).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute/chronic split in the GBS sense — CIDP is managed as a chronic relapsing/progressive condition from diagnosis, though flares may need escalation of the same therapies):** IVIG, corticosteroids, or plasma exchange; analgesia for neuropathic pain.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation; ongoing immunotherapy often needed long-term given the chronic/relapsing nature (unlike GBS, which is typically monophasic).

**P:** 80% respond to treatment within the first few months; 50% go into remission, 10% have poor outcomes (severe disability or death). Negative prognostic factors: ↑age, progressive course, CNS involvement, axonal loss on biopsy.

> [!note] No crossover between the demyelinating syndromes due to differing underlying pathology: MS affects oligodendrocytes in the CNS; GBS and CIDP affect Schwann cells in the PNS.

### Myasthenia Gravis (MG)

**D:** chronic autoimmune disorder of the post-synaptic membrane at the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) in skeletal muscle.

**A/P:** antibodies against nicotinic AChR on the post-synaptic membrane (90%) → complement-mediated destruction → decreased action potential in skeletal muscle → weakness. Also anti-muscle-specific tyrosine kinase (MuSK) antibodies (10–40%).

> [!info] Classification
> Class I: any eye weakness ± ptosis | Class II: mild weakness of other muscles | Class III: moderate weakness | Class IV: severe weakness | Class V: intubation required to maintain airway. Classes II–IV split into (a) predominantly limb/axial/both and (b) oropharyngeal/respiratory/both.

**S/Smx:** muscle fatiguability (patients usually complain of increasing weakness toward the end of the day); ptosis, diplopia; dysphagia; dysarthria; facial paresis; proximal limb weakness.

> [!info] Gap-filled — "fatiguability" was stated as MG's defining feature without explaining why weakness specifically *worsens with repeated use*, unlike most other causes of muscle weakness. **Mechanism:** normal neuromuscular transmission has a substantial **safety margin** — even in healthy people, each successive nerve impulse releases somewhat less acetylcholine than the one before (normal presynaptic vesicle depletion with repeated firing), but this is inconsequential because there are far more postsynaptic AChRs available than strictly needed to trigger a muscle action potential. In MG, **antibody-mediated destruction of postsynaptic AChR (per the A/P above) erodes this safety margin** — with fewer receptors remaining, the normal, otherwise-harmless decline in ACh release during repeated stimulation becomes clinically significant, since there are no longer enough receptors in reserve to compensate. Progressively fewer muscle fibres reach the threshold needed to fire with each successive contraction, producing genuine, measurable **fatiguable weakness** — worse with sustained or repeated activity, better after rest, and classically worse by the end of the day as the safety margin is exhausted by a day's cumulative use. This same principle underlies the "jitter" seen on single-fibre EMG above, and is also the physiological basis for testing fatiguability directly at the bedside (e.g. sustained upgaze provoking ptosis).

**Epidemiology:** females <40yo; males >60yo. Link with thymoma (20–40% of thymoma patients develop MG). Associated with other autoimmune disorders.

**Ix:** single fibre electromyography (*why:* the most sensitive test for MG, detecting the characteristic abnormal neuromuscular transmission even when other tests are negative; *what:* increased "jitter" between muscle fibre action potentials, high sensitivity >92%). CT thorax (*why:* screens for thymoma, a significant and treatable association requiring its own management (thymectomy); *what:* may show a thymic mass). CK (*why:* helps exclude a primary myopathy as an alternative cause of weakness; *what:* normal in MG, distinguishing it from myopathic causes of weakness where CK is typically elevated). Autoantibodies to AChR (*why:* the key diagnostic serological test; *what:* positive in ~90% — anti-MuSK antibodies tested if AChR-negative, positive in a further 10–40% of the remainder). In crises, forced vital capacity (*why:* the key bedside test for monitoring respiratory muscle involvement and predicting impending respiratory failure — trended serially rather than relying on a single measurement; *what:* declining FVC signals worsening respiratory muscle weakness and impending need for ventilatory support).

**Mx (Class I–III):** pyridostigmine first-line; corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (e.g. azathioprine, rituximab), thymectomy second-line.

**Mx (Class IV–V):** combination of both above.

**Mx of myasthenic crisis:** (1) serial FVC ± mechanical ventilation, (2) plasmapheresis or IVIG, (3) supportive care, remove trigger. **The FVC monitoring and the IVIG here are the same as in GBS** — see the two boxes under Guillain-Barré Syndrome above for why oximetry misleads in neuromuscular respiratory failure, and for what IVIG is doing, not repeated here.

> [!note] Myasthenic crisis = acute worsening of symptoms, often triggered by stressors — can lead to respiratory failure.

**Mx of MG in pregnancy:** PO pyridostigmine (not IV), prednisolone, azathioprine, tacrolimus, ciclosporin, IVIG, plasmapheresis.

> [!warning] Drugs that worsen MG: β-blockers, lithium, antibiotics (gentamicin, macrolides, quinolones, tetracyclines), phenytoin, quinidine, procainamide, penicillamine.

### Neuromyelitis Optica (Devic Disease)

**D:** chronic disorder of the brain and spinal cord dominated by optic neuritis and myelitis.

**R:** autoimmune disorders (PMH or FHx), non-white predominance relative to MS

**A/P:** similar to MS; antibody-mediated disease.

**S/Smx:** may be preceded by viral infection. Optic neuritis. Transverse myelitis: motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunction below a certain spinal level, may be unilateral.

**Ix:** as per MS + labs — test for anti-aquaporin 4 (AQP4-IgG) (*why:* the key distinguishing serological test from MS, since NMO and MS can look clinically similar but require different long-term Mx (some MS DMARDs can actually worsen NMO); *what:* highly specific, moderately sensitive — a positive result strongly supports NMO over MS) and anti-myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibodies (*why:* identifies a related but distinct antibody-mediated demyelinating spectrum (MOG-antibody disease) with its own prognostic and treatment implications; *what:* positive in a subset of AQP4-negative patients with a similar clinical picture).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** high-dose IV methylprednisolone; plasmapheresis for severe/steroid-refractory attacks.
- **Chronic/long-term:** long-term immunosuppression — critical to distinguish from MS DMARD choices, since using an MS-specific therapy in NMO can worsen outcomes; relapse prevention is the main long-term goal given the risk of cumulative disability with recurring episodes.

**P:** in most cases, partial recovery occurs; permanent loss of function can occur with recurring episodes.

## Cranial Nerve Disorders and Vertigo

### Bell's Palsy

**D:** acute, unilateral, idiopathic facial nerve (CN VII) palsy — a diagnosis of exclusion once other causes of facial weakness are ruled out.

**R:** pregnancy (especially third trimester/immediately postpartum), diabetes, upper respiratory tract infection, family history.

**A:** idiopathic, though HSV reactivation is suspected to play a role in many cases (analogous to the mechanism proposed for Ramsay Hunt syndrome, below).

**P:** inflammation and swelling of the facial nerve within the narrow bony facial canal → compression → conduction block/axonal injury.

**S/Smx:** unilateral facial weakness affecting **both the upper and lower face** (forehead involvement is the key distinguishing feature from a central/UMN cause — see below); onset over hours to 1–2 days; ± hyperacusis (stapedius muscle involvement), ± loss of taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (chorda tympani involvement), ± reduced tear production, ± post-auricular pain preceding the weakness.

> [!info] Forehead-sparing is the key localising sign
> **UMN facial weakness** (e.g. stroke): forehead is **spared** — the patient can still wrinkle their forehead on the affected side — because the forehead has bilateral cortical representation. **LMN facial weakness** (Bell's palsy and other CN VII lesions): forehead is **also affected** — the patient cannot wrinkle their forehead on the affected side, since the entire nerve from the nucleus outward is involved. This single bedside sign is one of the highest-yield distinctions in clinical neurology.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* Bell's palsy is diagnosed on the characteristic history and exam findings, and is a diagnosis of exclusion; *what:* the forehead-involving LMN pattern above, with no other neurological signs). Exclude alternative causes if atypical features present — bilateral involvement, other cranial neuropathies, or a slowly progressive (rather than acute) course should prompt imaging/further work-up (MRI to exclude a structural cause, e.g. tumour compressing CN VII; serology if Lyme disease is a geographic/exposure consideration; vesicles in the ear canal would suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome — VZV reactivation in the geniculate ganglion, causing a similar LMN facial palsy plus a vesicular rash and often more severe pain, requiring aciclovir + steroids rather than steroids alone).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** oral corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone) started within 72h of onset — the single most evidence-supported intervention, improving the chance of full recovery; specific regimens used include prednisolone 25mg twice daily for 10 days, or 60mg once daily for 5 days followed by a 5-day taper (reducing by 10mg/day) — either is reasonable, given equivalence hasn't been clearly established between the two; eye care is essential given the risk of exposure keratopathy from impaired eyelid closure — lubricating eye drops/ointment, and consider taping the eye closed at night, given the cornea is now unprotected on the affected side.
- **Definitive:** most patients recover spontaneously over weeks to months; antivirals (aciclovir) added to steroids remain of uncertain additional benefit for straightforward Bell's palsy specifically, though used for suspected Ramsay Hunt syndrome given the confirmed viral aetiology there. If no improvement within 3 weeks, refer urgently to ENT (± plastics ± ophthalmology if eye problems persist) rather than continuing to watch and wait, given this timeframe suggests the diagnosis or expected course should be reviewed.
- **Chronic/long-term:** if incomplete recovery, referral to specialist facial nerve services; physiotherapy for facial rehabilitation; watch for and manage synkinesis (aberrant nerve regeneration causing unwanted associated movements) as a longer-term complication.

**P:** the majority of patients recover fully within 3–4 months (roughly 80%); a minority have residual moderate-to-severe weakness if untreated (~15%), or synkinesis. Poorer prognosis with complete (rather than partial) paralysis at onset, older age, and delayed treatment initiation. Recurrence occurs in a small minority (~7%).

> [!warning] Bell's palsy is a diagnosis of exclusion — refer to ENT or neurology (rather than continuing to treat as straightforward Bell's palsy) if: **bilateral** facial palsy (raises a broader differential — Lyme disease, Guillain-Barré syndrome, leukaemia, sarcoidosis, EBV, trauma, myasthenia gravis — none of which is "bilateral Bell's palsy," given Bell's palsy is by definition unilateral), or **recurrent** episodes (~7% of cases, itself atypical enough to warrant re-evaluation of the underlying cause).

> [!info] Recognised risk factors also include intranasal influenza vaccination and pregnancy (particularly the third trimester and immediate postpartum period, ~3× risk) and diabetes (~5× risk) — an association with COVID-19 has also been noted (see [[02_Respiratory]] Viral pneumonia/pneumonitis (including COVID-19) for the general COVID-19 entry, not repeated here), though the mechanism (as with the broader HSV-reactivation theory) remains incompletely understood.

### Vertigo (Peripheral vs Central, BPPV, Vestibular Neuritis)

`CF-PAIR` **Three accounts of vertigo sit in this file and none declared the others before 2026-09-02:** `D5 §0.1 The "Dizzy" Patient` / `D5 §0.3 Episodic Vertigo` / `D5 §0.4 Disequilibrium and Chronic Dizziness` (which carry the Dix-Hallpike discriminators) · `04_Neurology ### Vertigo (Peripheral vs Central, BPPV, Vestibular Neuritis)` · `NEW_Neurology ## Acute Vertigo` (the only one with otoscopy and Rinne/Weber). **All three kept in full, none reconciled.** The HINTS examination itself is `[[Exam_02_Neurological_Skin_and_Head_and_Neck]] §1.21 Vertigo Examination (Dix-Hallpike and the HINTS Exam)`; the ear causes — Ménière's disease in particular — are `[[ENT_01_Ear_Hearing_Vertigo_and_Facial_Palsy]] 13_03 ## Ménière's disease`.

**D:** an illusory sensation of movement (typically spinning) of oneself or the environment, reflecting a mismatch between vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive input.

> [!info] Peripheral vs central vertigo — the key bedside distinction
> **Peripheral** (inner ear/vestibular nerve): sudden onset, severe/intense, often with hearing loss/tinnitus (if the cochlea is involved), horizontal or horizontal-torsional nystagmus that **suppresses with visual fixation**, no other neurological signs, worsened by head movement. Much more common overall.
> **Central** (brainstem/cerebellum): can be more insidious, often less severe intensity but more constant, vertical or direction-changing nystagmus that does **not** suppress with fixation, associated neurological signs (diplopia, dysarthria, ataxia, limb weakness — see the DANISH cerebellar signs above), less affected by head movement position. Red flags for a central cause include any of these associated neurological signs, and warrant urgent imaging.

**Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV):**
- **D:** the most common cause of peripheral vertigo — dislodged otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) within the semicircular canals (most often posterior canal) triggering false movement signals with head position change.
- **S/Smx:** brief (seconds, typically <1 minute) episodes of vertigo triggered specifically by head position change (rolling over in bed, looking up, bending down); no hearing loss or tinnitus.
- **Ix:** Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre (*why:* the diagnostic test — reproduces the vertigo and characteristic nystagmus by provoking the affected canal; *what:* a positive test shows torsional/upbeating nystagmus with a brief latency, fatiguing with repetition).
- **Mx:** Epley manoeuvre (*why/what:* a repositioning technique that moves the dislodged otoconia out of the semicircular canal — highly effective and typically curative in one or a few sessions, more effective than medication for this specific cause); vestibular sedatives (e.g. prochlorperazine) have a limited role, mainly for short-term symptomatic relief rather than definitive treatment, since they don't address the mechanical cause.

**Vestibular Neuritis (and Labyrinthitis):**
- **D:** presumed viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve (vestibular neuritis) or the labyrinth itself, the latter also affecting hearing (labyrinthitis = vestibular neuritis + hearing loss/tinnitus).
- **S/Smx:** acute, severe, continuous vertigo (unlike BPPV's brief positional episodes) lasting days, often with nausea/vomiting; no other neurological signs; hearing loss/tinnitus present in labyrinthitis but not vestibular neuritis specifically.
- **Ix:** clinical diagnosis; HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) used by specialists to help distinguish from a central (posterior circulation stroke) cause in acute vestibular syndrome, given the two can otherwise look similar and the consequences of missing a stroke are severe.
- **Mx:** vestibular sedatives (e.g. prochlorperazine, betahistine) for short-term symptomatic relief in the acute phase only (prolonged use can impair central vestibular compensation and delay recovery); vestibular rehabilitation exercises to promote compensation once the acute phase settles; corticosteroids sometimes used though evidence is mixed.

**P (vertigo generally):** BPPV frequently recurs but responds well to repeat Epley manoeuvres; vestibular neuritis typically resolves over days to weeks as central compensation develops, though some patients have prolonged symptoms.
