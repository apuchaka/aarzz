## CNS Infections

### Bacterial Meningitis

**D:** inflammation of the meninges by various microbes.

**R:** ↑age, crowding, exposure history, ↓immunity, brain defects (e.g. AV defects), cochlear implants, sickle cell disease

> [!info] Causative organisms by age
> 0–3mo: **Group B Streptococcus** (*S. agalactiae* — note this file also uses **GBS** for **Guillain-Barré syndrome**, so the organism is spelled out here), *E. coli*, *L. monocytogenes* | 3mo–6y: *N. meningitidis*, *S. pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae* | 6–60y: *N. meningitidis*, *S. pneumoniae* | >60y: *S. pneumoniae*, *N. meningitidis*, *L. monocytogenes* | Immunosuppressed: *L. monocytogenes*

**P:** haematogenous or direct contiguous spread → rapid multiplication in the subarachnoid space → bacterial components in CSF trigger inflammation → influx of WCC → cerebral oedema, ↑ICP → damage, death.

**S/Smx:**
- Early: headache, neck stiffness, fever, altered consciousness
- Late (red flags): shock, sepsis/rash, respiratory or cardiac compromise, bleeding, raised ICP (focal neuro signs, papilloedema, seizures, ↓GCS)

> [!tip] Kernig's sign (11%): pain in lower back/thigh on knee extension when hip is flexed to 90°. Brudzinski's sign (9%): forced neck flexion elicits reflex hip flexion.

> [!info] Verified against an Australian-authored review (Young et al., Internal Medicine Journal — "Meningitis in adults: diagnosis and management"), Aug 2026 — the overall pathway and drug classes below are essentially consistent with Australian practice (Australian, UK, and European guidelines share the same broad approach, in contrast to North American guidelines which add vancomycin routinely for all patients). Adding specific AU-sourced doses not in the original UK-style note.
> **Ix + Mx pathway:**
> - If patient presents at GP, send to hospital ASAP; give IM benzylpenicillin if this does not delay transfer — **1200mg for anyone ≥10 years**, per the age-banded table in [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Giving IM benzylpenicillin before hospital admission, which is the corpus's owner for this dose and includes the adult band. **This entry named the drug with no dose** until the G22 round (2026-08-29).
> - ABCDE + senior review if red flags (e.g. GCS <12, RR <8 or >30)
> - Decide on LP: delay if rapidly deteriorating, very unstable (e.g. severe sepsis), or signs of raised ICP (papilloedema, GCS ≤12, focal neuro signs, seizures); if LP cannot be done within 1 hour, give IV antibiotics after blood cultures
> - **Antibiotics + IV dexamethasone + fluids** (for the criteria that justify dexamethasone — frankly purulent CSF, CSF WBC >1000, protein >1g/L, or bacteria on Gram stain — see [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Meningitis, which states them and this entry did not)**:** empirical ceftriaxone 2g IV q12h (base regimen — consistent with the age range already in the UK-sourced note); **vancomycin is only added if there's a higher suspicion of pneumococcal meningitis or reduced ceftriaxone susceptibility** (known/suspected otitis media or sinusitis, Gram-positive cocci on CSF Gram stain, positive pneumococcal antigen test) — not routinely added for every patient; **benzylpenicillin 2.4g IV q4h is added for Listeria coverage** in patients with Listeria risk factors (age <3mo or >50yo, immunocompromise) — consistent with the UK-sourced age thresholds already here. Dexamethasone 10mg IV q6h is a routine, guideline-supported component given its proven mortality/morbidity benefit specifically in pneumococcal meningitis.
> - Contacts exposed within 7 days before symptom onset: PO ciprofloxacin (alternative: rifampicin); offer meningococcal vaccination if confirmed; no prophylaxis for pneumococcal meningitis unless a cluster of cases — not individually re-verified against an Australian public health unit source this session; the general principle is standard, but check current state/territory public health guidance for the specific first-line prophylactic agent.

**Ix (why + what, expanding on the pathway above):** LP with CSF analysis (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test, identifying the causative organism and guiding antibiotic rationalisation once results return — see the CSF Interpretation table further down this file for the bacterial pattern; *what:* neutrophilic pleocytosis, raised protein, reduced glucose/CSF:plasma glucose ratio, positive Gram stain/culture). Blood cultures (*why:* identifies the organism when LP is delayed or contraindicated, and supports diagnosis even without CSF culture; *what:* may be positive, particularly for meningococcal/pneumococcal disease). FBC, CRP (*why:* supports the inflammatory/infective picture and can be trended; *what:* leucocytosis, elevated CRP). Coagulation profile (*why:* screens for DIC, a recognised complication especially of meningococcal disease; *what:* may show coagulopathy in severe/fulminant disease).

**Complications:** sensorineural hearing loss (25–35% after pneumococcal meningitis, 5–10% after Hib), seizures, focal neurological deficit, cognitive/behavioural problems, sepsis/abscess, raised pressure (herniation, hydrocephalus), Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome (adrenal insufficiency secondary to adrenal haemorrhage, especially with meningococcal meningitis).

> [!note] *Cryptococcus neoformans* stains with India ink.

### Viral Meningitis

**D:** inflammation of the meninges by a virus.

**R:** very young or old, immunocompromised, summer/autumn, unvaccinated for mumps, IVDU

**A:** non-polio enteroviruses (e.g. coxsackievirus, echovirus), mumps, HSV, CMV, HZV/VZV, HIV, measles

**S/Smx:** presents similarly to bacterial meningitis, possibly less severe.

**Ix:** exclude bacterial meningitis — LP (*why:* distinguishes viral from bacterial meningitis, since the two are clinically similar but require very different Mx; *what:* lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal/mildly elevated protein, normal glucose — see CSF Interpretation table further down this file for the full comparison), PCR (*why:* identifies the specific viral cause, particularly important to identify/exclude HSV given the specific antiviral implications; *what:* may identify enterovirus, HSV, VZV, or other causative virus).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** supportive generally; if HSV meningitis suspected, consider aciclovir; if bacterial meningitis or encephalitis suspected clinically, treat until proven otherwise (IV ceftriaxone and aciclovir) — don't wait for CSF results to start empirical treatment when the clinical picture doesn't clearly distinguish viral from bacterial disease.
- **Chronic/long-term:** none typically required — most cases are self-limiting.

**P:** generally good, but post-viral syndrome (headache, malaise) may persist for weeks. May recur.

### Encephalitis

**D:** inflammation of the brain parenchyma associated with neurological dysfunction.

**R:** <1yo or >65yo, immunocompromised, post-infection, blood/fluid exposure, organ transplant, animal/insect bites, geographic location, season, swimming in certain waters

**A:** causative agent found in only 50%; viruses, bacteria, immune-mediated causes.

**P:** similar to meningitis but involving the brain parenchyma.

> [!info] Diagnostic criteria (1 major + ≥3 minor for probable encephalitis)
> Major: altered mental status lasting ≥24h with no alternative cause identified. Minor: fever ≥38°C within 72h before/after presentation; partial/generalised seizures; new-onset focal neurological findings; CSF WCC ≥5; positive neuroimaging findings; positive EEG findings

**Ix:** LP (*why:* essential for diagnosis and to identify the causative organism via PCR, which directly guides antiviral therapy; *what:* CSF — ↑WCC, ↑protein + PCR for HSV, VZV, enteroviruses). Neuroimaging — MRI better than CT (*why:* identifies the pattern of parenchymal involvement, supporting the diagnosis and sometimes suggesting a specific cause (e.g. temporal lobe changes in HSV encephalitis — see the note below); *what:* normal in 1/3 of patients; may show medial temporal and inferior frontal changes). EEG (*why:* supports the diagnosis (one of the minor criteria above) and screens for subclinical seizure activity; *what:* lateralised periodic discharges at 2Hz — a pattern particularly associated with HSV encephalitis). Bloods + blood cultures (*why:* screens for a systemic infective source and baseline; *what:* may be unremarkable or show evidence of the underlying infective/systemic process).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** hospitalisation + isolation; **IV aciclovir 10mg/kg every 8 hours if suspected encephalitis — start empirically without waiting for confirmatory PCR**, given HSV encephalitis causes irreversible damage rapidly and aciclovir is safe to start presumptively; supportive care (airway, fluids, analgesia). **Continue for 14–21 days if HSV is confirmed**, then repeat the LP to confirm the CSF is HSV-negative before stopping.

> [!note] **The dose and duration were absent from both encephalitis entries** — this one gave neither, and [[15_02_Paeds_-_Ill_and_Feverish_Child__Meningitis__Encephalitis]] Encephalitis gave the duration but no dose. **No IV aciclovir dose existed anywhere in the corpus**, which carried only oral regimens for cold sores, shingles and suppression. Added by the pairs audit (2026-08-29); see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B56**. That paediatric entry also owns the **encephalitis-vs-meningitis distinction** (meningeal irritation usually absent; seizures and focal signs more common) and the empirical **triple therapy** — ceftriaxone, clarithromycin and aciclovir — given while the cause is unknown.
- **Chronic/long-term:** rehabilitation for any residual neurological deficit; ongoing seizure management if seizures occurred during the acute illness.

**P:** depends on aetiology; 12% mortality for infectious aetiology in England.

> [!note] Herpes simplex encephalitis — HSV-1 causes 95% of cases; characteristically affects temporal and inferior frontal lobes. Focal features of temporal lobe infection, e.g. aphasia. CT may show bilateral temporal lobe changes.

### Brain Abscess

**D:** suppurative collection of microbes within a gliotic capsule occurring within brain parenchyma — single or multiple.

**R:** infection elsewhere (especially sinusitis, otitis, dental procedure/infection, endocarditis), brain surgery, AVM, immunocompromise, DM

**A:** bacterial, fungal, or parasitic organisms (possibly polymicrobial)

**P:** organisms enter the brain via haematogenous spread or direct inoculation; most often originates in ischaemic white matter adjacent to the cortex.
1. Early cerebritis (~3 days): local inflammation, necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, microglial/astrocyte activation
2. Late cerebritis (days 4–9): lymphocytic and microglial infiltration
3. Frank abscess formation (after day 10): encapsulation and suppuration

**S/Smx:** headache, signs of raised ICP, cranial nerve palsy, fever, focal neurological deficit.

**Ix:** bloods (FBC, CRP/ESR) (*why:* supports the inflammatory diagnosis, and the degree of elevation can help distinguish abscess from tumour, which can otherwise look similar on imaging; *what:* elevated inflammatory markers — ↑CRP/ESR favours abscess over tumour). Blood cultures (*why:* may identify the causative organism, particularly if haematogenous spread is the source; *what:* may be positive, especially with endocarditis-related seeding). Neuroimaging — MRI better than CT (*why:* the key diagnostic test, characterises the lesion and its stage; *what:* ring-enhancing lesion with surrounding oedema, evolving through the cerebritis-to-abscess stages described above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole) ASAP — empirical broad cover pending culture/drainage results; anticonvulsants if seizures occur; steroids only if patient unstable (raised ICP causing mass effect) — not routine, since steroids can theoretically reduce antibiotic penetration and are reserved for genuine clinical need.
- **Definitive:** surgical drainage once confirmed and the collection has organised enough to be amenable to drainage (early cerebritis is typically treated medically, since there's no discrete collection yet to drain).
- **Chronic/long-term:** prolonged antibiotic course (often several weeks) with imaging follow-up to confirm resolution; address the underlying source (e.g. dental/sinus/ear infection) to prevent recurrence.

**P:** permanent hemiparesis and long-term seizures in <50%; mortality <13%. Main prognostic factor is the patient's neurological status at presentation.

### Spinal Epidural Abscess

**D:** inflammation with pus within the epidural space.

**R:** IVDU, recent surgery/trauma, indwelling catheter, immunocompromise, DM, concomitant bacteraemia or endocarditis

**A:** haematogenous spread (up to 50% of cases), contiguous spread (30%), direct inoculation (surgery, trauma)

**P:** the epidural venous plexus (valveless veins running from foramen magnum to sacrum) and lymphatics allow bacterial spread. Neurological impairment secondary to vascular compromise of the spinal cord/roots and direct pressure on tissue.

> [!info] Clinical stages
> (1) Localised muscle/bone pain (2) Radicular pain and paraesthesias (3) Muscle weakness, sensory loss, sphincter dysfunction (4) Paralysis

**S/Smx:** classic triad of focal back pain, fever, and neurological deficit present in only 10%. **The usual organism is *S. aureus*** — named in [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Spinal epidural abscess and absent here until the pairs audit (2026-08-29); that entry owns the antimicrobial approach.

**Ix:** bloods (FBC, CRP/ESR) (*why:* supports the inflammatory diagnosis and can be trended; *what:* elevated inflammatory markers). Blood cultures (*why:* identifies the causative organism, especially relevant given the frequent haematogenous source; *what:* often positive, most commonly *S. aureus*). MRI spine (*why:* the definitive diagnostic and localising test — essential for surgical planning and to assess the degree of cord/root compression; *what:* epidural collection with cord/root compression, extent defining the urgency of surgical intervention).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics started promptly (don't delay for culture results given the risk of rapidly progressive paralysis) → culture-directed once results available; supportive therapy (manage hypotension, DVT prevention given reduced mobility).
- **Definitive:** surgical decompression for patients with neurological deficit or failure to improve on antibiotics alone — timing is critical, since paralysis lasting ≥24–36h is likely to persist (see Prognosis below), making this a genuine surgical emergency once deficit appears.
- **Chronic/long-term:** prolonged antibiotic course (typically several weeks) with clinical/imaging follow-up.

**P:** ~5% mortality; most important prognostic factor is the patient's neurological status at presentation. Paralysis lasting ≥24–36h is likely to persist.

## CNS Infections Associated with Immunosuppression

### Toxoplasmosis

**D:** infection by the protozoan parasite *Toxoplasma gondii*, usually symptomatic only in immunocompromised patients.

**R:** immunocompromise (HIV etc.), exposure during pregnancy (risk to child), ingestion of raw/undercooked meat, exposure to cat faeces

**A:** *Toxoplasma gondii* found in cat faeces → ingested → spreads haematogenously throughout the body.

**P:** 90% asymptomatic if immunocompetent. In immunocompromised patients, increased dissemination (especially neural and muscular tissues) → inflammation → symptoms.

**S/Smx:**
- Immunocompetent: resembles infectious mononucleosis — fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy; rarely meningoencephalitis and myocarditis
- Immunocompromised: headache, confusion, drowsiness, vision issues
- Toxoplasmic chorioretinitis: blurry vision, fundoscopic lesions
- Congenital: cerebral calcification, hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, retinopathy, cataracts

**Ix:** serology (*why:* screens for exposure/infection, though a positive result mainly indicates past exposure and doesn't confirm active CNS disease in an immunocompromised patient — often used alongside imaging rather than alone; *what:* positive IgG indicates prior exposure, with reactivation being the mechanism of disease in immunocompromise rather than new infection). CT (*why:* the key diagnostic imaging test in a patient with new neurological symptoms and known immunocompromise/HIV risk; *what:* single or multiple ring-enhancing brain lesions, often involving basal ganglia ± mass effect — a pattern overlapping with primary CNS lymphoma below, sometimes distinguished by a therapeutic trial of anti-toxoplasma treatment with repeat imaging).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (immunosuppressed/congenital cases):** pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine + calcium folinate (≥6-week course) — folinic acid co-administered to reduce pyrimethamine's marrow toxicity. Eye disease — steroids to reduce inflammation.
- **Definitive:** not needed in immunocompetent patients (self-limiting).
- **Chronic/long-term:** treat until CD4 >200 for ≥6 months (immune reconstitution allows safe cessation); prophylaxis for HIV patients with CD4 <100: cotrimoxazole (primary prevention before infection occurs, distinct from the treatment regimen above).

**P:** in immunocompromised patients, infection is lifelong; chronic suppressive therapy needed unless CD4 >200.

### Primary CNS Lymphoma

**D:** extranodal non-Hodgkin lymphoma confined to the brain, eyes, and CSF without evidence of systemic spread.

**R:** HIV + EBV, usually >60yo

**A/P:** various mutations; usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma histology.

**S/Smx:** focal neurological deficits, altered mental status, behavioural changes, raised ICP (headache, nausea/vomiting, papilloedema), seizures.

**Ix:** CT/MRI (*why:* the initial imaging test, though the appearance can overlap with toxoplasmosis (see above) in an HIV-positive patient — this overlap is a genuine diagnostic challenge worth knowing; *what:* homogeneously enhancing mass lesion, most commonly brain, less frequently eyes/spine). PET scan (*why:* screens for systemic (non-CNS) disease, which would reclassify this as secondary rather than primary CNS lymphoma and change Mx; *what:* absence of systemic disease supports the primary CNS diagnosis). Diagnostic procedure — stereotactic biopsy, vitrectomy, or CSF cytology (*why:* tissue/cytological diagnosis is required for definitive confirmation, since imaging alone can't reliably distinguish lymphoma from toxoplasmosis or other mass lesions; *what:* confirms diffuse large B-cell lymphoma histology).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — this is managed as an oncological diagnosis via a treatment protocol, not an acute emergency Mx pathway):** high-dose methotrexate + rituximab ± other chemotherapy ± radiation. Surgery of limited use (unlike many other brain tumours, since lymphoma is chemosensitive and surgical resection doesn't improve outcomes the way it might for other lesions).
- **Chronic/long-term:** close monitoring for relapse given the significant relapse rate (see Prognosis).

**P:** 10–15% primary refractory, 50% of patients relapse → poor prognosis, median survival of 2 months without added treatment. Median time to relapse is 10–18 months; most relapses occur within 2 years of initial diagnosis.

### Cryptococcosis

**D:** opportunistic fungal infection caused by *Cryptococcus* species.

**R:** HIV (80% of cases; CD4 <100), immunosuppression, comorbidities, smoking

**A/P:** in immunosuppressed patients, majority caused by *Cryptococcus neoformans*, usually starting in the lungs.
1. Dry yeast cells inhaled and deposited in lungs
2. Alveolar macrophages release cytokines/chemokines to recruit inflammatory cells
3. Th1 response → granulomatous inflammation; haematogenous dissemination to multiple organs

**S/Smx:** symptom onset 1–2 weeks in HIV. Pulmonary infection: fever, productive cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, weight loss. CNS involvement: headache, fever, cranial nerve palsies, change in mental status over days.

**Ix:** cryptococcal polysaccharide antigen in serum/CSF/pleural fluid (*why:* a highly sensitive and specific rapid test, often the first confirmatory test used given its speed compared to culture; *what:* positive antigen confirms the diagnosis). Cultures (*why:* confirms the diagnosis and allows susceptibility testing; *what:* may take longer to grow than the antigen test takes to return, hence the antigen test's practical importance). HIV antibodies (*why:* cryptococcal CNS disease is strongly associated with HIV, so testing is essential both diagnostically and for planning concurrent ART; *what:* screens for the underlying immunosuppressive cause). CXR (*why:* screens for the pulmonary source/co-existing pulmonary disease; *what:* may show pulmonary infiltrates/nodules). LP (*why:* essential to assess for CNS involvement and, critically, to measure opening pressure — raised ICP is a major driver of morbidity/mortality in cryptococcal meningitis specifically and needs active management; *what:* often markedly raised opening pressure, positive India ink stain/antigen — see the note on this above).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** antifungal therapy (fluconazole for milder disease; amphotericin B for CNS/severe disease, often combined with flucytosine per current international practice); if relapse/refractory, amphotericin B. Manage raised ICP actively (repeated therapeutic LPs to reduce opening pressure — a distinct and important part of Mx given how much raised ICP contributes to mortality in this specific infection).
- **Definitive/chronic:** if HIV-positive, start ART — but timing matters, since starting ART too early relative to antifungal treatment risks immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS), so this is usually deferred by some weeks after antifungal treatment begins.

**P:** 70% fatal if untreated; 20–30% fatal in high-income countries.

### Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)

**D:** devastating CNS infection caused by the JC virus (JCV).

**R:** immunocompromise (especially HIV, but also drug-induced)

**A/P:** JCV establishes an asymptomatic, lifelong persistent/latent infection in the general population. In immunocompromise, JCV can reactivate and cause lytic infection of CNS glial cells (demyelination) → PML.

> [!note] "Classic PML" triad: multifocal demyelination, oligodendroglia with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei, and enlarged bizarre astrocytes with irregularly lobulated nuclei.

**S/Smx:** multifocal neurological symptoms, altered mental status, blindness, aphasia, progressive cranial nerve dysfunction, motor or sensory decline, ultimately coma. Rarely, seizures.

**Ix:** brain biopsy is definitive (*why:* the gold-standard diagnostic test, though invasive and not always performed if the clinical/imaging picture plus a positive CSF PCR is sufficiently convincing; *what:* the classic triad described above). PCR from CSF (*why:* a less invasive alternative first-line test, though sensitivity is imperfect so a negative result doesn't fully exclude PML; *what:* detects JCV DNA in CSF, supporting the diagnosis when positive).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no specific antiviral cure exists — Mx centres on restoring immune function):** direct antiviral therapies or boosting antiviral immune response — the mainstay is actually reversing the underlying immunosuppression where possible (e.g. ART in HIV, or stopping/reducing an implicated immunosuppressive drug) since there's no reliably effective direct anti-JCV therapy; watch for immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) as immune function recovers, which can paradoxically worsen symptoms initially.

**P:** if ART started early, better chance of improvement, but 70% have residual neurological disability. JCV persists in the host.

## Brain Tumours

**Metastases:** most common type of brain tumour, most commonly from lung, breast, bowel, skin (melanoma), and renal cancers. Mx: radiotherapy, chemotherapy, palliation; surgery rarely an option.

**Glioblastoma multiforme:** 45% of primary brain/CNS tumours; 5-year survival only 5.5%. CT/MRI: lesions with central necrosis + vasogenic oedema. Histology: pleomorphic tumour cells around a necrotic centre. Mx: surgery + chemo ± RT (dexamethasone for swelling).

**Meningioma:** tumour originating from the meningeal layer of the brain/spinal cord. Majority benign, but can undergo malignant transformation (32–64% 5-year survival). 37% of primary brain/CNS tumours. R: neurofibromatosis type 2, radiation, hormonal therapy, family history. CT/MRI — 65% show a dural tail. Histology: spindle cells in concentric whorls ("onions") and calcified psammoma bodies. Mx: surgery + chemo ± RT; if asymptomatic, ?watch and wait.

**Vestibular schwannoma:** see entry under ENT.

> [!note] This entry covers **adult** brain tumours. The paediatric tumours are a different set — [[15_12b_Paeds_-_Brain_Tumours]] Brain tumours owns medulloblastoma, brainstem astrocytoma and the rest, organised by location. Added by the pairs audit (2026-08-29).

**Pilocytic astrocytoma:** malignancy of the astrocytes ("pilocytic" = hair-like). Most common brain cancer in children, usually slow-growing, good prognosis. Histology: Rosenthal fibres (corkscrew eosinophilic bundle). Mx: resection is usually curative.

**Medulloblastoma:** most common malignant brain tumour in children, very aggressive. Arises and spreads from the fourth ventricle; can spread to the whole CNS. Histology: small round cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratio, very invasive.

**Ependymoma:** malignancy of cells lining the ventricular system, commonly the 4th ventricle. Rare (1.7%). Histology: perivascular pseudorosettes.

**Oligodendroglioma:** malignancy of grey matter, commonly frontal lobes. Usually benign and slow-growing. Histology: "fried egg"-like microscopic calcifications.

**Haemangioblastoma:** vascular tumour occurring in brain, spinal cord, and retina. Associated with Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (multiple tumours over lifetime). Histology: foam cells, high vascularity.

**Craniopharyngioma:** most common supratentorial brain tumour in children. Arises from remnants of Rathke's pouch (near the pituitary gland). Can cause bitemporal hemianopia due to location.

**Pituitary adenoma:** benign tumours of the pituitary gland; divided into secretory or non-secretory, and micro (<1cm) or macro (>1cm) adenomas. Secretory tumours cause symptoms of hormone excess (e.g. Cushing's from ↑ACTH, acromegaly from ↑GH). Classically causes bitemporal hemianopia via optic chiasm compression. Mx: hormonal ± surgical (transsphenoidal approach).

> [!info] Bitemporal hemianopia: lower quadrant loss = craniopharyngioma (tumour compresses from below/behind); upper quadrant loss = pituitary adenoma (tumour compresses from below).

> [!note] Unless otherwise noted, most brain tumours are treated with a combination of surgery, radiotherapy ± chemotherapy.

## CT Head, Head Injury, and Intracranial Pressure

### Who gets a CT head for head injury?

> [!info] Verified — this UK NICE-derived algorithm (ABCDE/LATE mnemonics) is a reasonable reflection of the evidence base, but **at least some Australian diagnostic-imaging pathway guidance is explicitly built on the Canadian CT Head Rule (CCHR) instead of NICE**, so don't assume this exact mnemonic-based version is "the" Australian standard — practice varies by state/hospital, and the underlying high-risk-factor logic is broadly similar between the two rules but not identical in detail. The Canadian CT Head Rule's own high-risk factors (CT mandatory): GCS <15 at 2h post-injury, suspected open/depressed skull fracture, any sign of basal skull fracture, ≥2 episodes of vomiting, age ≥65 — closely overlapping with, but not worded identically to, the ABCDE list below. Its medium-risk factors (CT recommended, since a clinically important brain injury could still be missed): amnesia before impact ≥30min, dangerous mechanism (similar to the "L" in LATE below). Note the CCHR only applies to patients who had loss of consciousness or amnesia — patients without either aren't covered by it, though evidence suggests some residual risk can still exist if other risk factors are present. Given this genuine local variation, check the current protocol at the specific institution/state rather than treating either the NICE-style ABCDE/LATE mnemonic below or the CCHR as the single definitive Australian answer.

**CT within 1 hour if any of:**
> [!danger] Mnemonic "ABCDE"
> **A**fter-hit seizures, **B**one fractures (suspected open/depressed skull fracture, or signs of basal skull fracture — racoon eyes, mastoid ecchymosis, haemotympanum, CSF otorrhoea/rhinorrhoea), **C**oma (GCS <13 initially, or GCS <15 at 2h post-injury), **D**eficit (focal neurological), **E**mesis (>1 episode of vomiting since injury)

If none of the above, but loss of consciousness or amnesia after injury occurred, check for:

> [!warning] Mnemonic "LATE"
> **L**egendary tale (dangerous mechanism — pedestrian/cyclist struck by vehicle, occupant ejected from vehicle, fall from >1m or >5 stairs), **A**mnesia (>30 min retrograde amnesia of events before injury), **T**hrombin disorder (Hx of bleeding/clotting disorder), **E**lderly (≥65yo)

If any present → CT within 8h of injury (and check if the patient is on warfarin → if so, CT <8h regardless). If none present → no CT head required.

### Intracranial Pressure

Normal ICP: 7–15 mmHg (higher in adults than children). Cerebral perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure − intracranial pressure.

### Low ICP
**Causes:** iatrogenic (e.g. lumbar puncture headache, post-epidural), spontaneous.

**Ix:** MRI may show thickened meninges. LP opening pressure <4–5 mmHg suggestive.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** bed rest, fluids, caffeine — after LP, patients should lie flat for ≥1h. Consenting patients for spinal/epidural should be warned about the risk of low-pressure headache.
- **Definitive (if conservative measures fail):** epidural blood patching — patient's own blood injected into the epidural space; blood acts as a tamponade and clots over the epidural leak; usually works very well.

### Raised ICP
**Causes:** traumatic head injuries (secondary to bleeding, haematomas), infections (e.g. meningitis, TB), tumours, hydrocephalus, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (a diagnosis of exclusion).

**S/Smx:** headache — typically worse in the morning or on lying down; vomiting; decreased consciousness; papilloedema.

> [!danger] Cushing's triad (late stage): widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular breathing. **The mechanism resolves an apparent paradox** — rising ICP reduces cerebral perfusion, triggering a **two-stage** autonomic response, not a single simultaneous reflex: (1) **sympathetic activation first** — raised ICP → cerebral ischaemia → sympathetic surge → peripheral vasoconstriction → hypertension (the widening pulse pressure, systolic rising more than diastolic), an attempt to restore cerebral perfusion pressure against the raised ICP; (2) **baroreceptor-mediated vagal bradycardia second** — the resulting hypertension is detected by carotid/aortic baroreceptors, triggering a **parasympathetic (vagal) reflex bradycardia** — genuinely the *opposite* of the reflex tachycardia hypertension would normally cause, which is exactly why the combination is a specific, recognisable sign rather than two unrelated findings. Irregular breathing reflects brainstem compression/ischaemia directly. **Genuinely a preterminal, late sign** — ED patients with 2 of 3 signs present have roughly double the mortality of those with normal vitals — so relying on the full triad to recognise raised ICP means acting too late; earlier signs (headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, per the S/Smx above) should prompt action well before this triad appears.

**Ix:** CT/MRI first-line (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] CT Head — Systematic Approach for the general reading framework, including the cistern-effacement sign of raised ICP specifically, not repeated here). LP opening pressure >20 suggestive of raised ICP.

> [!danger] If suspecting raised ICP due to mass effect (e.g. haematoma, tumour), DO NOT perform LP — risk of herniation. The mass must be dealt with first to relieve pressure.

Invasive ICP monitoring: catheter placed into the lateral ventricles to monitor ICP ± collect samples/drain small amounts.

**Mx:**
- Treat underlying cause if possible (dexamethasone can help reduce swelling in brain tumours → decreased ICP)
- Head elevation to 30°
- IV mannitol (osmotic diuretic)
- Controlled hyperventilation — ↓pCO2 → cerebral artery vasoconstriction → ↓ICP (temporary solution)
- Removal of CSF — ventricular drain, repeated LPs (e.g. in IIH), ventriculoperitoneal shunt (for hydrocephalus)

### Hydrocephalus

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Hydrocephalus," High yield) — Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus is covered as its own subtype under Dementias above, but general hydrocephalus (classification and the acute/obstructive presentation, a genuine neurosurgical emergency) was previously only mentioned in passing as a complication of other conditions throughout this file. This entry covers the general framework; see Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus under Dementias above for that specific chronic subtype, not repeated here.

**D:** an abnormal accumulation of CSF within the ventricular system, typically causing ventricular dilatation ± raised intracranial pressure.

**Classification (by mechanism):**
- **Non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus** — a physical blockage within the ventricular system itself prevents CSF flow (e.g. at the cerebral aqueduct, or the fourth ventricle outlets) — causes include tumours, congenital aqueductal stenosis, and posterior fossa masses/malformations (including Chiari malformation). This is the pattern most likely to present acutely and dangerously, since CSF continues to be produced proximal to the blockage with nowhere to go.
- **Communicating hydrocephalus** — CSF flows freely between the ventricles but is inadequately reabsorbed at the arachnoid granulations — causes include meningitis (impairing reabsorption via inflammatory exudate), subarachnoid haemorrhage (blood similarly impairing reabsorption — see Strokes/Brain Bleeds above for the disease-level detail on SAH, not repeated here), and normal pressure hydrocephalus (see Dementias above).

**A:** congenital (aqueductal stenosis, Chiari malformation, Dandy-Walker malformation, spina bifida with associated hydrocephalus), acquired (tumours, meningitis, subarachnoid or intraventricular haemorrhage, post-traumatic).

**S/Smx:** depends heavily on the rate of onset and, in children, whether the cranial sutures have fused —
- **Acute (any age):** headache (classically worse in the morning or with lying flat/Valsalva, given CSF pressure dynamics), nausea/vomiting, reduced conscious level, papilloedema (see raised ICP above for the fuller general S/Smx this overlaps with, not repeated here); untreated acute obstructive hydrocephalus can progress rapidly to brainstem compression/herniation and death, making this a genuine time-critical neurosurgical emergency.
- **Infants (before suture fusion):** the skull can expand to partially accommodate raised pressure, giving a somewhat different presentation — macrocephaly/rapidly increasing head circumference, a bulging anterior fontanelle, "sunset sign" (downward-deviated eyes from pressure on the tectal plate), poor feeding, irritability, developmental delay if untreated.
- **Chronic/gradual (older children/adults):** may present more insidiously with headache, cognitive change, or gait disturbance — overlapping with the Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus triad above in the communicating, normal-pressure subtype specifically.

**Ix:** CT or MRI head (*why:* the key diagnostic and localising test, confirming ventricular dilatation and — critically — identifying the level and likely cause of any obstruction, which directly determines whether urgent surgical decompression is needed; *what:* ventriculomegaly, with the pattern of which ventricles are enlarged helping localise an obstructive lesion — e.g. dilated lateral and third ventricles with a normal-sized fourth ventricle points to aqueductal obstruction). Further Ix to identify the underlying cause per the causes listed above (e.g. LP only once mass lesion/obstruction has been excluded on imaging, given the coning risk — see Raised ICP above for this explicit contraindication, not repeated here).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute (obstructive hydrocephalus, or any acutely decompensating patient):** urgent neurosurgical referral — external ventricular drain (EVD) provides immediate CSF diversion and ICP control while the underlying cause is addressed/definitive treatment is arranged; this is genuinely time-critical given the herniation risk described above.
- **Definitive:** treat the underlying cause where possible (e.g. resection of an obstructing tumour); **ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt** is the mainstay definitive treatment for hydrocephalus that isn't resolved by treating the underlying cause alone — a permanent implanted device diverting CSF from the ventricles to the peritoneal cavity for reabsorption; **endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV)** is an alternative to shunting in selected obstructive cases (particularly aqueductal stenosis), creating an internal bypass route for CSF flow and avoiding a permanent implanted shunt system.
- **Chronic/long-term:** patients with a VP shunt require lifelong awareness of shunt complications — blockage (presenting with recurrence of the original hydrocephalus symptoms) and infection (shunt infection can present with fever plus the same symptoms, and is a genuine diagnostic trap if not specifically considered in a shunted patient presenting unwell) — a shunted patient presenting with headache/vomiting/reduced consciousness should always prompt consideration of shunt malfunction as a differential, given how easily this can be missed if not specifically asked about.

### Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumour Cerebri)

**R:** F>M (~6:1), obesity and weight gain, some drugs (COCP, steroids, lithium, tetracyclines, isotretinoin)

**S/Smx:** headache (typically worse on waking or when lying down); papilloedema; blurred vision, enlarged blind spot; ± CN VI palsy (cannot look laterally).

**Ix:** as per raised ICP; IIH is a diagnosis of exclusion (*why:* other causes of raised ICP (mass lesion, hydrocephalus, venous sinus thrombosis) must be actively excluded via imaging before accepting an IIH diagnosis, since the label itself means "no other cause found"; *what:* imaging is essentially normal aside from signs of raised pressure — no mass lesion, ventricles typically normal or small).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** repeated LP to temporarily reduce ICP — provides rapid symptomatic relief while definitive Mx takes effect.
- **Definitive/chronic:** carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g. acetazolamide, topiramate — not in pregnancy, teratogenic) — MOA: decreased CSF production; long-term weight loss (a genuinely disease-modifying intervention given the strong obesity association, not just symptomatic); surgery (e.g. optic nerve sheath fenestration or CSF shunting) for vision-threatening or refractory disease.

### Head Injury

Primary injuries divided into focal or diffuse.
- **Focal:** contusion (bruising), haematoma (collection of blood — see brain bleeds section)
- **Diffuse axonal injury:** occurs from mechanical shearing after deceleration, causing disruption and tearing of axons; 90% end up in coma, very poor prognosis

**Secondary brain injuries** occur due to (1) the body's response to the primary injury, and (2) loss of autoregulatory mechanisms from the primary injury — e.g. cerebral oedema, ischaemia, infection, tonsillar or tentorial herniation, Cushing's reflex (preterminal event).

**Ix:** CT head (per algorithm above); full body survey; ICP monitoring (mandatory if GCS 3–8 + abnormal CT head; consider if GCS 3–8 + normal CT head); U&Es (may reveal hyponatraemia if SIADH develops secondary to brain injury).

**Mx:**
- ABCDE + resuscitate — C-spine collar may be needed; intubate & ventilate if GCS <8
- ICP monitoring as above
- If markedly raised ICP, decompressive craniotomy may be needed
- Depressed skull fractures: surgical reduction and debridement
- Maintain cerebral perfusion pressure ≥70 mmHg in adults, 40–70 mmHg in children

### Restless Legs Syndrome

**D:** compulsive urge to move the legs.

**R:** conditions associated with iron deficiency (e.g. ESRD), pregnancy, family history (50%), use of antidepressants/antihistamines/metoclopramide, uraemia, DM

**A/P:** not fully understood; associated with iron and dopamine dysfunction.

**S/Smx:** akathisia — uncontrollable urge to move the legs; usually occurs at night, but progresses to daytime as the condition worsens; may be associated with paraesthesias (crawling/throbbing sensations); movement during sleep may be noted by a partner (periodic limb movements of sleep).

**Ix:** FBC and iron studies (especially ferritin) to exclude iron deficiency.

**Mx:** treat any iron deficiency; remove potentially offending drugs; lifestyle modifications (walking, stretching, massaging affected limbs; stop smoking; trial cutting caffeine and alcohol).

> [!info] Verified — dopamine agonists (ropinirole or pramipexole) as first-line pharmacological Mx for restless legs syndrome, with benzodiazepines/gabapentin as options, reflects standard international practice and is consistent with Australian eTG/AMH prescribing — no change needed to the drug classes below.

### Abnormal Involuntary Movements

**Chorea:** sudden, rapid, involuntary, purposeless movements (Greek for dance/choreography). Results from basal ganglia damage.
> [!info] Important causes — Neuro: Huntington's disease, Wilson's disease, ataxic telangiectasia, stroke/TIA (basal ganglia); Rheum/autoimmune: SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, rheumatic fever (Sydenham's chorea); Drugs: COCP, L-dopa, antipsychotics; Haem: polycythaemia rubra vera, neuroacanthocytosis; Pregnancy (chorea gravidarum); thyrotoxicosis; carbon monoxide poisoning.

**Athetosis:** slower form of chorea with a writhing, snake-like quality; often accompanies chorea.

**Dystonia:** slow, prolonged contraction of trunk and limb muscles.

**Ballism & hemiballism:** severe form of chorea involving proximal muscles, characterised by violent flinging movements. Hemiballism affects only one side of the body. Results from subthalamic nucleus damage. Mx: antidopaminergics (e.g. haloperidol).

**Tics:** intermittent, stereotypical, repetitive, involuntary movements (e.g. blinking, shrugging). Tourette syndrome: multiple motor and vocal tics beginning in childhood ± comorbid neuropsychiatric disorders. Mx: clonidine, atypical antipsychotics.

**Oculogyric crisis:** subtype of dystonia characterised by restlessness, agitation, and involuntary upward eye deviation. Causes: antipsychotics (especially first-gen), metoclopramide, post-encephalitic PD. Mx: stop offending drug, IV antimuscarinics (e.g. benztropine, procyclidine).

**Essential tremor (benign essential tremor):** progressive, mainly symmetrical, rhythmic, involuntary oscillatory movement disorder of the hands and forearms. Autosomal dominant. Usually absent at rest, present during intentional movements (e.g. finger-to-nose test). Improves with alcohol and rest (opposite of Parkinson's). Associated with titubation (head tremor). Mx: propranolol first-line; ?primidone.

> [!info] Verified against Austroads *Assessing Fitness to Drive* 2022 edition (current at Aug 2026), private-vehicle standards, Aug 2026 — Australian standards are structured quite differently from the UK DVLA rules, generally built around a single "default standard" seizure-free/event-free period rather than the UK's variable tiered system. Commercial-vehicle standards are considerably stricter (e.g. 10-year seizure-free default for some categories) — check the source document directly if advising a commercial driver.

**Epilepsy/seizures:**
- General principle: a person with epilepsy must be seizure-free for **12 months** to hold an unconditional private licence (Austroads' "default standard") — this replaces the UK's variable 6-month/12-month split by structural cause/EEG findings with a single, simpler 12-month threshold for private drivers.
- Sleep deprivation is explicitly **not** considered an acceptable "provoking factor" exemption (since it can't be reliably avoided) — a seizure attributed to sleep deprivation is still assessed under the standard epilepsy criteria, not treated as a one-off provoked event.
- Switching from one antiepileptic to another, or planned withdrawal of medication in a driver who otherwise meets the conditional-licence standard: **3-month non-driving period** applies.
- General safety principles for all licence-holders with epilepsy: take antiseizure medication regularly as prescribed, ensure adequate sleep, avoid substances (e.g. alcohol) known to increase seizure risk; periodic review recommended (at least annually for conditional-licence holders).

**Syncope:** (see also [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.35.5 for the fuller Austroads cardiovascular-syncope driving table) — a non-driving period of at least 4 weeks applies generally for cardiovascular-cause syncope in private drivers, with the specific period depending on whether a treatable cause was identified and addressed.

**Stroke/TIA:**
- **Stroke:** minimum **4 weeks** non-driving for private drivers (this applies as a blanket minimum regardless of severity, reflecting the non-progressive nature of stroke once stabilised — a notably simpler rule than the UK's deficit-dependent approach). Private drivers discharged from specialist care within 4 weeks of a stroke, assessed as fit to drive at discharge, can continue driving on their current licence without needing formal reassessment (a 2022-edition streamlining).
- **TIA:** minimum **2 weeks** non-driving for private drivers (materially shorter than the UK's 4-week/6-month tiered approach) — reflecting the genuinely low risk of a TIA itself causing a crash (a TIA essentially never causes loss of consciousness). A conditional licence is not required, since there's no expected long-term impairment.
- **Subarachnoid haemorrhage:** minimum **3 months** non-driving for private drivers, given the higher rate of associated neurological injury and post-SAH seizures compared to other stroke types.

**Narcolepsy/cataplexy:** case-by-case, depending on symptom control — consistent with the general Austroads approach for sleep disorders.

**Chronic neurological disorders (e.g. MS, MND):** Australia doesn't use a UK-style "PK1 form" — instead, the treating doctor assesses and reports to the state/territory driver licensing authority as required, with licensing decided case-by-case based on functional impairment rather than diagnosis alone. Patients have a legal obligation to notify the licensing authority themselves if a condition is likely to affect driving safety.

**Neurosurgery:** not separately verified this session — the source's UK-specific craniotomy/meningioma/pituitary-tumour timeframes below have not been individually checked against Austroads; treat as illustrative only and confirm against the current Austroads "Other neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions" chapter before relying on a specific figure:
- Craniotomy: UK figure (unverified for AU use) — no driving for 12 months
- Benign meningioma + no seizure history: UK figure (unverified for AU use) — licence reconsidered 6 months after surgery if no seizures
- Pituitary tumour: UK figures (unverified for AU use) — craniotomy: no driving for 6 months; transsphenoidal surgery: if safe to drive, OK

### Wernicke's Encephalopathy

> [!warning] FLAG 2026-09-01 — duplicate pair with GI
> `GI_merged C2 §0.7 Complications of Vomiting` opens with
> *"Wernicke's encephalopathy — give thiamine before glucose"* and covers the same emergency.
> **Both retained; pair marked, not merged.**

**D:** neuropsychiatric disorder caused by thiamine (B1) deficiency.

**R:** alcoholism, AIDS, chemotherapy treatment (due to increased vomiting), GI surgery, malnutrition

**P:** decreased activity of thiamine-dependent enzymes → neuronal death.

**S/Smx:** oculomotor dysfunction (nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia — lateral rectus palsy); gait ataxia; encephalopathy (confusion, disorientation, indifference, inattentiveness); peripheral sensory neuropathy.

**Ix:** MRI (*why:* supports the diagnosis, though Wernicke's is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis and treatment should never be delayed pending imaging; *what:* may show characteristic symmetrical signal changes around the third ventricle, periaqueductal grey, and mammillary bodies — though a normal MRI does not exclude the diagnosis).

**Mx:** urgent thiamine replacement — **give thiamine before or with any glucose administration**, since glucose given first can precipitate or worsen Wernicke's in a thiamine-deficient patient by consuming remaining thiamine stores in glucose metabolism; this is a genuine and easily-missed medical emergency where treatment should start on clinical suspicion alone, never delayed for confirmatory Ix.

**P:** if untreated, Korsakoff's syndrome can develop (antero/retrograde amnesia and confabulation — confabulation = making up memories).

### Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease

**D:** hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy.

Different inheritance patterns; can affect Schwann cells and myelin (CMT1) or axons (CMT2). Look for family history.

**S/Smx:** distal muscle weakness and atrophy; hyporeflexia; high-stepping gait/foot drop (history of frequently sprained ankles); high-arched feet (pes cavus); hammer toes; "stork leg" deformity.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* distinguishes the demyelinating (CMT1, slowed conduction) from axonal (CMT2, reduced amplitude with relatively preserved velocity) subtypes, relevant to genetic counselling and prognosis; *what:* pattern depends on subtype as above). Genetic testing (*why:* confirms the specific genetic subtype, informs inheritance pattern counselling for family members, and can guide prognosis; *what:* identifies the specific causative mutation among the many genes associated with CMT).

**Mx:** supportive — physical and occupational therapy (gait aids, ankle-foot orthoses for foot drop); podiatry for foot deformities; genetic counselling for at-risk family members given the hereditary nature.

> [!note] Other random notes from this source: local anaesthetic toxicity — Mx with 20% IV lipid emulsion. Brain pathology causing nausea/vomiting — Mx with cyclizine.
