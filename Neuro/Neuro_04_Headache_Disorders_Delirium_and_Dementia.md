## Tension Headache

**D:** headache characterised by generalised distribution, like a "tight band around the head". Also known as tension-type headache.

**R:** mental tension/stress, fatigue, missing meals, dehydration

**A:** unknown; ?muscle tension

**P:** release and activation of inflammatory agents → pain

**S/Smx:** "tight band around head"; can coexist with migraine but lacks migraine features.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* tension headache is diagnosed on the characteristic history alone; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above, with exclusion of red flags as for other primary headaches).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** paracetamol, NSAIDs, aspirin.
- **Chronic/long-term:** avoid known triggers (stress, missed meals, dehydration, fatigue) — lifestyle-focused prevention rather than pharmacological prophylaxis for most patients, given tension headache doesn't typically require the disease-modifying prophylaxis used in migraine/cluster headache.

## Migraine

**D:** chronic, episodic neurological disorder characterised chiefly by severe headaches ± aura.

**R:** family history, F>M (~3:1), young to middle age, obesity (BMI >30), stress, sleep disorder

**A:** uncertain; ?hyperexcitable brain, strong genetic component

**P:** neurogenic inflammation of trigeminal sensory neurons → vasodilation of blood vessels → headache. Aura relates to neuronal dysfunction.

**S/Smx:** headache lasting 4–72h, unilateral location, pulsatile quality, moderate-severe pain, aggravated by (or causes avoidance of) usual activity, ± nausea/vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia, ± association with menses in women, ± aura (25% — visual symptoms e.g. scotoma).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* migraine is diagnosed on history alone using established criteria — Ix is reserved for excluding a secondary cause when red flags are present, not for confirming migraine itself; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above meeting **ICHD (International Classification of Headache Disorders)** criteria — the formal diagnostic classification, applied to the symptom pattern rather than requiring any test). Exclude red flag symptoms (see "Other Headache Causes" section below for the full SNOOP4 red-flag list) — imaging (CT/MRI) only if red flags present, not for typical migraine.

**Mx:**

> [!info] Verified against Australian migraine management literature (Pain Management Today, an Australian GP-focused source), Aug 2026 — acute Mx is essentially internationally standard and matches the content below; the main AU-specific point is a difference in prophylaxis prescribing pattern.
> **Immediate/acute:** sumatriptan + NSAID/paracetamol (in 12–17yo, consider nasal triptan instead of PO); non-PO metoclopramide or prochlorperazine, consider non-PO NSAID or triptan if vomiting limits oral absorption.
>
> Chronic migraine = ≥8 days/month for ≥3 months.
>
> **Chronic/long-term (prophylaxis):** propranolol; topiramate (NOT in women of childbearing age — teratogenic); amitriptyline. **In Australian general practice specifically, there has been a strong historical bias toward pizotifen or propranolol as first-choice prophylaxis** — pizotifen does not appear in the standard UK-sourced prophylaxis list this entry began from (propranolol/topiramate/amitriptyline, named at the start of this line) but is a genuinely common Australian first-line choice; note, however, that current specialist guidance cautions this bias doesn't always reflect the most appropriate individual choice, and agent selection should be guided by comorbidities and side-effect profile rather than habit. For women with predictable menses-related migraines, frovatriptan or zolmitriptan may be given ± mefenamic acid, caffeine. In pregnancy, paracetamol + NSAIDs (2nd line); avoid aspirin and opioids.

> [!danger] Migraine with aura is an absolute contraindication to the COCP due to markedly increased stroke risk. HRT might worsen migraines.

## Medication Overuse Headache

**D:** chronic headache secondary to overuse of analgesics, ≥15 days/month. Common.

**A/P:** not fully understood — ?overuse of analgesics leads to decreased receptor sensitivity to analgesia → paradoxical pain ("rebound effect") when the analgesic is withdrawn.

**Dx:** pre-existing headache disorder requiring analgesia; headache ≥15 days/month; regular analgesic overuse for >3 months (people taking triptans or opioids at higher risk); not better accounted for by another headache syndrome.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** if on paracetamol, NSAID, or triptan — withdraw abruptly (warn patient headache will get worse before it improves; persist). If on opioids — gradual reduction. Withdrawal symptoms: nausea/vomiting, hypotension, ↑HR, restlessness, anxiety, sleep disturbance — persist through these.
- **Chronic/long-term:** address the underlying primary headache disorder driving the analgesic overuse (e.g. appropriate migraine prophylaxis) once withdrawal is complete, to prevent relapse into overuse.

## Trigeminal Neuralgia

**D:** facial pain syndrome in the distribution of ≥1 divisions of the trigeminal nerve (CN V).

**R:** ↑age, multiple sclerosis (20x risk), ?F>M (3:2), ?HTN

**A:** focal compression of the CN V root by an aberrant vascular loop; idiopathic/other causes

**P:** focal demyelination → conduction dysfunction → pain

**S/Smx:** brief (seconds), sudden onset and cessation; electric shock-like pain limited to the CN V distribution; triggered by any movement/sensation in that distribution (touching the face, shaving, brushing teeth).

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* trigeminal neuralgia is a clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic pain pattern; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above). Exclude red flags — MRI if atypical features (younger age, bilateral, sensory deficit, other neurological signs) since these raise suspicion for an underlying cause such as MS or a compressive lesion rather than idiopathic/vascular-loop compression (*why:* MRI screens for a structural cause needing separate management, particularly relevant given the MS association noted above; *what:* may show an aberrant vascular loop, or an alternative structural cause such as a demyelinating plaque or tumour).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute/emergency tier — this is a chronic pain condition managed pharmacologically first):** carbamazepine first-line; if no response, refer to neurology.
- **Chronic/long-term:** neurosurgical options (e.g. microvascular decompression, ablative procedures) considered for medically refractory pain once specialist-assessed.

## Trigeminal Autonomic Cephalalgias

**D:** group of primary headache disorders characterised by unilateral head pain associated with generally prominent ipsilateral cranial autonomic features. Includes: cluster headache, paroxysmal hemicrania, SUNCT (short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks with conjunctival injection and tearing), SUNA (with cranial autonomic symptoms).

### Cluster Headache

**D:** a trigeminal autonomic cephalalgia causing headaches occurring in clusters of weeks, around once a year.

**R:** M>F (3:1), family history, head injury, smoking, alcohol use (can trigger)

**A:** unknown

**P:** trigeminal distribution, ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms, occurring a few weeks per year.

**S/Smx:** headache lasting 15 min–2h; clusters typically ~4–12 weeks; intense, sharp, stabbing pain around one eye (described as one of the worst kinds of pain); associated with lacrimation, redness, lid swelling; ± miosis, ptosis. Patient is often restless during attacks (as opposed to migraine, where patients often want to lie down).

**Ix:** MRI head with gadolinium (*why:* excludes a secondary structural cause — cluster headache is a clinical diagnosis, but first presentations warrant imaging to exclude a pituitary or other structural lesion given some overlap in presentation; *what:* usually normal in primary cluster headache).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** 100% oxygen and subcutaneous triptan (80% and 75% respond within 15 min for each treatment respectively) — refer to neurology for diagnosis confirmation and ongoing management.
- **Chronic/long-term (prophylaxis, started early in a cluster to shorten its course):** verapamil ± tapering dose of prednisolone.

## Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis)

> [!note] For further detail, see [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Giant cell arteritis (GCA) — this section covers only the headache aspect.

**R:** >50–60yo, F>M, ?family history

**S/Smx:** headache occurs in 85% of GCA patients, with a tender, palpable temporal artery. Rapid onset, history <1 month, unilateral. Jaw claudication in 65%. ± ocular complications.

**Ix:** ↑ESR (*why:* a sensitive but non-specific inflammatory marker, and the key first-line screening test given how time-critical this diagnosis is — visual loss can be sudden and irreversible; *what:* typically markedly elevated). Temporal artery biopsy to confirm (*why:* the definitive diagnostic test; *what:* may require >1 biopsy due to skip lesions, so a single negative biopsy doesn't fully exclude the diagnosis if clinical suspicion remains high).

> [!warning] **[[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Giant cell arteritis (GCA) owns the full Mx and the steroid doses** — urgent high-dose steroids should not be delayed for biopsy confirmation, given the risk of irreversible visual loss, and biopsy stays positive for 1–2 weeks after steroids are started. Not repeated here.

## Other Headache Causes

> [!danger] Headache red flags — mnemonic "SNOOP4"
> **S**ystemic symptoms (incl. fever), **N**eoplasm in PMH/FHx, **N**eurologic deficit or change, **O**nset — sudden/abrupt, **O**lder age >50yo, **P**attern change or recent onset, **P**ositional headache, **P**recipitated by sneezing/coughing/exercise, **P**apilloedema, **P**rogressive headache + atypical symptoms, **P**regnancy or puerperium, **P**ainful eye with autonomic features, **P**ost-traumatic onset, **P**athology of immune system (e.g. HIV), **P**ainkiller overuse or new drug at headache onset

**Acute, single episodes:**
- Subarachnoid haemorrhage — occipital location, "thunderclap" sudden onset, "worst headache of my life"
- Acute closed-angle glaucoma — severe pain (ocular or generalised), ↓visual acuity, hard red eye, semi-dilated non-reacting pupil
- Meningitis/encephalitis — non-specific symptoms
- Head injury
- Sinusitis — associated with rhinorrhoea, allergic-type symptoms
- Tropical illness (e.g. malaria)

**Chronic:**
- Idiopathic intracranial hypertension — risk factors: obesity, F>M, pregnancy, drugs (COCP, steroids, tetracyclines, retinoids, lithium); symptoms include blurred vision, papilloedema, enlarged blind spot ± CN VI palsy (see ICP section)
- Chronically raised ICP
- Paget's disease of bone — secondary to mechanical compression or reduced blood flow to neural tissue; occipital headache, worse on coughing
- Psychological

## Delirium

**D:** acute, fluctuating changes in mental status — inattention, disorganised thinking, altered level of consciousness. Divided into hyperactive, hypoactive, mixed.

**R:** >65yo, past/present cognitive impairment or dementia, current hip fracture, severe illness

**A/P:** reduced functional reserve + precipitating insult = delirium. Insults include: drugs and withdrawal, primary neurological injury, acute illness (infection, hypoxia, shock, dehydration, fever, constipation), metabolic abnormalities, surgery, pain, sleep deprivation, environmental factors (physical restraints, catheters, ICU stay).

**S/Smx:** acute onset (hours to days); decreased cognition (often fluctuating); impaired consciousness (more drowsy than normal); agitation, fear, delusions; abnormal perceptions (e.g. hallucinating); symptoms usually worse at night, interspersed with periods of normality.

**Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* establishes a baseline and severity, though delirium's fluctuating course means a single assessment can under- or over-estimate impairment; *what:* fluctuating deficits, often with impaired attention as the most sensitive finding). Look for the source of delirium (*why:* delirium is always a symptom of an underlying precipitant — Ix should be directed at finding the reversible cause(s) rather than confirming delirium itself, which is diagnosed clinically; *what:* U&Es for AKI/electrolyte disturbance, FBC for infection (↑WCC), plus other targeted Ix per the precipitants listed above — e.g. blood glucose, urinalysis/culture, CXR, medication review).

> [!tip] Organic vs psychiatric causes of memory loss can be assessed with serial 7s — usually intact in psychiatric causes.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** remove precipitating insult, manage risk factors (e.g. medication review), give clear instructions, signage, visits — non-pharmacological measures remain first-line even in acute agitation, with medication reserved for when these are insufficient (see the box below for AU-specific antipsychotic dosing considerations, flagged as needing direct eTG/AMH confirmation).
- **Chronic/long-term (prevention):** assess for risk factors, ensure familiarity of environment — proactive prevention in at-risk patients (elderly, known cognitive impairment, hip fracture, severe illness) reduces incidence, given how much easier delirium is to prevent than to treat once established.

> [!info] Resolved — non-pharmacological management remains first-line in Australia as elsewhere (per eTG/AMH), and haloperidol is confirmed as the standard first-choice antipsychotic when medication is needed. CareSearch (an Australian palliative care clinical resource) gives the directly-matching, PBS-referenced Australian starting dose: **haloperidol 0.5–1mg (SC in the palliative/end-of-life setting specifically, though PO is also used more generally) twice daily PRN**, confirming the file's existing starting figure is essentially correct — the "0.5mg BD" starting point matches, though route (SC vs PO) depends on clinical context and swallowing ability rather than being fixed. For ongoing/persistent delirium, dosing is typically given regularly rather than PRN-only (either as a standing BD dose or via continuous subcutaneous infusion in the palliative setting), with additional PRN doses as needed on top. As before: QTc prolongation risk means baseline/repeat ECG monitoring is prudent, particularly in frail/elderly patients or with escalating doses, and doses should be titrated cautiously with more frequent review in this population.
> If patient becomes aggressive: haloperidol 0.5 mg PO bd → increase to 2.5 mg PO if no effect; IM haloperidol if no other choice; if secondary to alcohol withdrawal — diazepam is the Australian first-line choice, not chlordiazepoxide (which isn't registered in Australia — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) → Alcohol withdrawal for the fuller AU-verified regimen).

## Dementias

### Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Cognitive screening tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS) & MCI progression to dementia," High yield). The tools half of that row is fully built in [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS), but **MCI itself appeared exactly once in the entire corpus** — as an acronym inside a sentence about MoCA's sensitivity — with no definition, no boundary against normal ageing or dementia, and no management. Placed here at the head of the Dementias section rather than in the Geriatrics file because MCI is the prodromal state of the conditions below, and belongs with them. Verified against Dementia Australia's MCI guidance and healthdirect (Australian Government), Aug 2026.

**D:** objective cognitive impairment in one or more domains that is **greater than expected for age but does not impair independent function in daily life** — the functional criterion is the whole distinction. Once cognitive impairment causes loss of independence in everyday activities, the diagnosis is dementia, not MCI.

**The three-way boundary an intern is expected to hold:**
- **Subjective cognitive decline** — the person notices a change; formal testing is normal.
- **MCI** — testing is objectively abnormal; **function is preserved**.
- **Dementia** — testing is abnormal **and** function is impaired.

**Subtypes:** *amnestic* MCI (memory predominantly affected — the pattern most associated with progression to Alzheimer's disease) and *non-amnestic* MCI (executive, language or visuospatial domains predominantly).

> [!info] **Progression to dementia — represent this honestly, because the commonly-quoted single figure is misleading.** Annual conversion rates vary substantially with the population studied: around **8% per year in clinical trial cohorts and roughly 13% in large registries**, with higher rates in specialist memory-clinic populations, which are enriched for people already more likely to progress. Amnestic MCI converts faster than non-amnestic.
>
> The genuinely important counterpoint, and the one patients most need to hear: **MCI is not a guaranteed pre-dementia state.** It may remain stable for years, and a meaningful proportion of people **revert to normal cognition** — often those whose impairment had a reversible contributor in the first place. Presenting MCI as "early dementia" is both inaccurate and needlessly distressing.

**Ix — the point of investigating MCI is to find the reversible contributors**, because that is where the clinical value lies:
- Cognitive assessment with a validated tool (see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS)) — MoCA is preferred over MMSE here specifically because of its greater sensitivity at the mild end.
- **Collateral history**, which does more work than any test in establishing whether function is genuinely preserved.
- Bloods: **B12 and folate** (*why:* deficiency is a treatable cause of cognitive impairment; *what:* low — see Subacute Combined Degeneration of the Spinal Cord for the fuller mechanism, not repeated here), **TFTs** (*why:* hypothyroidism is treatable and commonly presents with cognitive slowing in older people; *what:* raised TSH), U&Es, calcium, LFTs, glucose/HbA1c.
- **Screen for depression** — depressive pseudodementia is the single most important reversible mimic (see Delirium vs Dementia vs Depression — the "3 Ds" in Older People below).
- **Medication review** — anticholinergic and sedative burden is a genuinely common and fully reversible contributor (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Polypharmacy and Deprescribing, not repeated here).
- Neuroimaging (CT or MRI brain) where there are atypical features, focal signs, rapid progression, or a young age at onset — not routinely in every case.

> [!danger] **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people develop dementia earlier and more often, and the age at which you should be thinking about it is correspondingly younger.** Age-specific prevalence ratios are most pronounced at the *younger* age bands — of the order of 3.5-fold at 45–49 and 3.8-fold at 50–54 — so cognitive symptoms in a 50-year-old Aboriginal patient warrant assessment rather than reassurance that they are too young. This is also why aged care eligibility begins at 50 rather than 65 — a threshold that applies to people who are homeless or at risk of homelessness as well (see [[18_Geriatrics_and_Older_Persons_Health]] Falls in Older People, not repeated here). **A further practical caution: the standard screening tools are not culturally neutral** — MMSE and MoCA depend on formal schooling, literacy and Western cultural content, and applying them unadjusted will over-diagnose impairment. Validated alternatives designed for this population exist (the KICA — Kimberley Indigenous Cognitive Assessment — being the best known) and should be used instead. Verified against AIHW dementia reporting for First Nations Australians and Australian rurality/dementia prevalence research, Aug 2026.

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** identify and treat every reversible contributor found above — this is the highest-value intervention and the reason the work-up matters.
- **Definitive:** **there is no approved drug treatment for MCI**, and cholinesterase inhibitors are **not** indicated at this stage — a common error, given they are used in Alzheimer's disease below. What has evidence is: **regular physical exercise** (any level better than none), **treating cardiovascular risk factors** — hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia — as modifiable dementia risk factors, cognitive and social engagement, addressing hearing and vision impairment, and treating depression where present.
- **Chronic/long-term:** **planned review over time** rather than a single assessment, since the trajectory is the diagnostic information and a one-off score cannot distinguish stable MCI from early dementia. Refer to a geriatrician, memory clinic or neurologist where the presentation is atypical, progression is rapid, or onset is young. Use the period of preserved capacity to raise **advance care planning and enduring power of attorney** while the person can genuinely participate (see [[A10_Ethics__Capacity__Consent_and_Certification]] Capacity assessment — the general framework) — a conversation that is far easier now than after progression, and a specific reason not to defer the discussion simply because the diagnosis is "only" MCI.

### Vascular Dementia

**D:** chronic progressive cerebrovascular disease of the brain causing cognitive impairment. 2nd most common dementia after Alzheimer's.

**R:** >60yo, obesity, HTN, smoking, stroke risk factors (e.g. AF)

**A/P:** infarction (secondary to emboli, thrombi, lacunar infarction, hypoxia), leukoaraiosis (subcortical leukoencephalopathy), haemorrhage. Vascular changes exhaust the brain's compensatory mechanisms and lead to dementia.

**S/Smx:** history of stroke strongly suggestive. Frontal cognitive syndrome — difficulty solving problems, apathy, disinhibition, slowed processing, poor attention, retrieval memory deficit, frontal release reflexes (grasp, glabella tap, jaw jerk). ± focal neurological signs, impaired gait and balance.

**Dx/Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* quantifies the cognitive impairment and its pattern, feeding into the NINDS-AIREN criteria below; *what:* impairment across multiple domains, often with a frontal-subcortical pattern in VD specifically — see the frontal cognitive syndrome features above). Imaging — MRI (best) or CT (*why:* directly demonstrates the cerebrovascular disease burden required for diagnosis (criterion 2 below) and its temporal relationship to symptom onset; *what:* infarcts, leukoaraiosis, or haemorrhage — the specific findings needed to fulfil the NINDS-AIREN criteria). Baseline bloods — FBC, vitamin B12 (*why:* screens for reversible/contributing causes of cognitive impairment that could mimic or compound VD; *what:* anaemia or B12 deficiency would be alternative/additional explanations), consider syphilis serology (*why:* neurosyphilis is a rare but treatable mimic of dementia, worth excluding especially with an atypical picture; *what:* positive serology would redirect Mx entirely).

> [!info] NINDS-AIREN criteria for diagnosis of VD
> (1) Dementia — impairment in memory + ≥2 other domains (orientation, attention, language, executive function)
> (2) Cerebrovascular disease — focal signs on neuro exam (e.g. hemiparesis)
> (3) Time relationship — step-wise decline or dementia within 3 months after stroke
> (4) Clinical symptoms consistent, e.g. gait disturbance, urinary incontinence

**Mx:**
- **Definitive (no acute tier — this is a chronic, progressive condition, though acute strokes contributing to it are managed per `D3 §0.1 Acute Stroke — Recognition and Hyperacute Management` above):** consider referral to specialist dementia clinic/neuro services; secondary prevention of stroke (antiplatelets, antihypertensives — the mainstay of Mx, since preventing further vascular injury is the main lever available, unlike AD where disease-modifying drugs exist); no specific drugs for VD itself.
- **Chronic/long-term:** non-pharmacological — cognitive stimulation programmes, music/art therapy; ensure social support for family/carers; ongoing cardiovascular risk factor management (the same risk factors driving the vascular disease itself).

### Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

**D:** chronic neurodegenerative disease with insidious onset and progressive (generally slow) decline.

**R:** ↑age, family history, genetics, Down's syndrome (early onset), CVD, smoking, midlife obesity, ↑saturated fats (linked to apolipoprotein E allele E4), alcohol >14 units/week or 0 units/week

**A/P:** increased interneuronal amyloid peptides due to decreased clearance of β-amyloid → amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles → microglial activation, cytokine and complement activation → neuritic plaques → synaptic/neuritic injury and cell death.
- Presenilin 1 and 2 gene missense mutations → early-onset AD
- Cerebral cortical and hippocampal atrophy

**S/Smx:** cognitive deficits (loss of recent memory, then decreased executive function, nominal dysphasia). Insidious onset, gradual progression.

> [!info] Early warning signs (10)
> Memory loss disrupting daily life; challenges in planning/problem-solving; difficulty completing familiar tasks; confusion with time/place; trouble with visual images and spatial relationships; new problems with words in speaking/writing; misplacing things and losing ability to retrace steps; decreased judgment; withdrawal from work/social activities; change in mood and personality

**Ix:** cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE) (*why:* establishes the pattern and severity of cognitive impairment, and provides a baseline to track progression; *what:* typically shows a memory-predominant deficit pattern early on, distinguishing it from the frontal-subcortical pattern of VD or the executive/visuospatial pattern of LBD). Baseline bloods (*why:* excludes reversible/contributing causes as for VD above — B12/folate deficiency, hypothyroidism, and other metabolic causes of cognitive impairment should always be screened before attributing decline to AD; *what:* identifies any reversible contributor).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — chronic progressive neurodegenerative disease):** non-pharmacological (reminiscence therapy, cognitive rehab); acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g. donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) for mild-moderate AD; memantine (NMDA receptor antagonist) as add-on or monotherapy for more advanced disease.
- **Behavioural symptoms:** antipsychotics ONLY for patients at risk of self-harm or severe distress (associated with increased mortality in dementia — a genuine risk-benefit trade-off, not a routine option).

**P:** 8–10 years of deterioration typically.

### Lewy Body Dementia (LBD)

**D:** neurodegenerative disorder with parkinsonism, progressive cognitive decline, prominent executive dysfunction, and visuospatial impairment.

**R:** ↑age

**A:** unknown

**P:** accumulation of Lewy bodies in vulnerable sites; course mimics idiopathic Parkinson's disease. Lewy bodies contain α-synuclein (a cytoplasmic protein associated with synaptic vesicles), neurofilament, ubiquitin. Marked ↓ ACh and dopamine.

**S/Smx:** cognitive fluctuations, visual hallucinations (80%), parkinsonian features, REM sleep disturbance; autonomic dysfunction (falls, syncope, urinary incontinence, constipation), delusions, altered sleep.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* LBD is primarily diagnosed on the characteristic clinical triad of fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, and parkinsonism; *what:* diagnosis rests on the S/Smx pattern above). Imaging — SPECT (*why:* dopamine transporter (DaT) SPECT imaging supports the diagnosis when the clinical picture is ambiguous, by demonstrating reduced striatal dopaminergic activity similar to idiopathic Parkinson's; *what:* reduced striatal uptake supports LBD/PD-spectrum pathology over AD).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic:** as per AD — cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine (often particularly effective for the cognitive/hallucination symptoms in LBD specifically).

> [!danger] Avoid antipsychotics (especially first-generation) due to extrapyramidal side effect risk, which can worsen parkinsonian symptoms — this is a specific, important LBD prescribing hazard, more pronounced than in other dementias.

**P:** average survival 5–7 years from symptom onset. In LBD, neuropsychiatric and motor symptoms occur within 1 year of each other. Dementia can also develop in Parkinson's disease, but typically >1 year after motor symptoms.

### Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (FTLD)

**D:** spectrum of primary neurodegenerative brain diseases affecting the frontal and/or temporal lobes.
- Behavioural variant ≈ Pick's disease
- Progressive nonfluent aphasia ≈ chronic progressive aphasia (CPA)
- Semantic dementia

**R:** mutations in some genes, ?traumatic brain injury

**A:** genetics (~30%), traumatic brain injury

**P:** neuronal loss, gliosis, microvascular changes of frontotemporal lobes. Abnormal neuronal/glial inclusions: FTLD-tau (hyperphosphorylated tau), TDP, FET.

**S/Smx:**
- All variants: onset ≤65yo, insidious onset, relatively preserved memory, changes in personality/social conduct
- Behavioural variant: disinhibition, apathy/inertia, increased appetite, perseveration
- CPA: effortful language output, loss of grammar, motor speech deficits
- Semantic dementia: progressive loss of knowledge about words and objects

**Ix:** MMSE (*why:* baseline cognitive assessment, though may under-detect FTLD early since memory — the domain MMSE weights heavily — is relatively preserved in FTLD, unlike AD; *what:* may be relatively normal early despite significant functional/behavioural impairment — a key diagnostic pitfall). MRI (*why:* demonstrates the characteristic regional atrophy pattern distinguishing FTLD from AD's more diffuse/hippocampal pattern; *what:* frontal/temporal lobe atrophy; behavioural variant shows focal gyral atrophy). Post-mortem biopsy (*why:* the definitive histological diagnosis, obviously not useful for antemortem management but relevant to eventual diagnostic confirmation/family counselling; *what:* may show Pick bodies (tau protein bodies on silver staining), gliosis, neurofibrillary tangles and senile plaques).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic:** supportive care; cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine not recommended (unlike AD/LBD — a genuine treatment distinction worth knowing, since prescribing these in FTLD offers no benefit and may worsen behavioural symptoms in some cases).

**P:** median survival from diagnosis 7–13 years — generally shorter survival and faster decline than AD.

### Cognitive-enhancing drug classes
**Cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEI)** (e.g. rivastigmine, donepezil, galantamine) — for mild-moderate AD. MOA: increased cholinergic activity. SE: GI upset, GU changes, sleep disorders. Note: donepezil relatively contraindicated with bradycardia.

**NMDA receptor antagonists** (e.g. memantine) — for severe AD, better tolerated. SE: GI upset, balance issues.

### Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus

**D:** condition with clinical features of hydrocephalus but without significant increased CSF pressure.

**R:** >65yo, vascular disease

**A/P:** not well understood; ?decreased blood flow to basal ganglia

**S/Smx:** "wet, wobbly, wacky" — gait apraxia not responding to levodopa; cognitive impairment; urinary incontinence.

**Ix:** CT head or MRI head (*why:* demonstrates the characteristic imaging pattern required for diagnosis; *what:* hydrocephalus with ventriculomegaly out of proportion to sulcal enlargement — this disproportion is the key distinguishing feature from simple age-related atrophy). Levodopa challenge (*why:* helps distinguish NPH's gait apraxia from Parkinson's disease, since NPH gait doesn't improve with levodopa; *what:* lack of response supports NPH over PD). LP (*why:* a large-volume "tap test" (removing a significant CSF volume) can predict response to shunting, used as a prognostic test before committing to surgery; *what:* symptomatic improvement after CSF removal supports a good shunt-response likelihood).

**Mx:**
