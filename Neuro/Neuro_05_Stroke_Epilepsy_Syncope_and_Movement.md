## Strokes

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a striking age-of-onset gap — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians experience stroke at substantially higher rates and at a much younger age than non-Indigenous Australians.** A remote-Australia healthcare network study found age-standardised stroke incidence roughly **3-fold higher** overall, with a median onset age of 54 — **17 years younger** than non-Aboriginal patients. The disparity is even starker in younger patients specifically: in Aboriginal patients aged under 55, ischaemic stroke incidence was **14-fold greater**, and intracerebral haemorrhage **19-fold greater**, than in non-Aboriginal patients of the same age — this isn't a modest shift in risk, it's a fundamentally different age-related risk profile.
> - **A genuinely actionable, current gap in screening guidance**: national guidelines currently recommend cardiovascular/stroke risk screening for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from age 35 — but a 2018 Australian National University study found significant cardiovascular/stroke risk already present in people **under 35**, with the Stroke Foundation explicitly calling for screening to start earlier than current guidelines recommend. This mirrors the same "standard screening threshold doesn't capture true risk in this population" pattern already established for AUSDRISK in Type 2 Diabetes (see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), not repeated here) — a recurring theme where general-population screening ages/tools are demonstrably too conservative for this population's actual risk profile.
> - **This connects directly to content already established elsewhere in this project**: rheumatic heart disease (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.22a Rheumatic Heart Disease (RHD), not repeated here) is itself a stroke risk factor via cardioembolic mechanism, and the broader cardiovascular risk burden described for CKD (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), not repeated here) and T2DM above compounds directly into this elevated stroke risk — these aren't separate, unconnected disparities, but overlapping and compounding ones in the same population.

### Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

**D:** transient episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischaemia, without acute infarction ± symptoms lasting <24h.

**Mx:**
1. Aspirin 300mg STAT unless contraindicated (contraindicated in patients already on anticoagulation — CT head to exclude haemorrhage; or already on aspirin)
2. Arrange specialist review within 24h; if >1 TIA ("crescendo TIA") — admit; advise not to drive (see Austroads Driving Standards further down this file — 2 weeks minimum for a single TIA in a private driver, longer for crescendo TIA at clinician discretion)
3. At specialist review: MRI brain (more sensitive for small infarcts); urgent carotid Doppler unless not a candidate for endarterectomy

> [!info] Gap-filled — the ABCD² score is genuinely known and worth being able to name, but current Australian guidance carries a specific, important caution about relying on it in isolation. **ABCD² score:** **A**ge ≥60 (1 point), **B**lood pressure ≥140/90 at first assessment (1 point), **C**linical features (unilateral weakness = 2, speech disturbance without weakness = 1, other = 0), **D**uration (≥60min = 2, 10–59min = 1, <10min = 0), **D**iabetes (1 point) — total 0–7, historically used to stratify low (0–3), moderate (4–5), and high (6–7) risk of stroke in the days following a TIA. **The Australian Stroke Foundation's own Living Clinical Guidelines explicitly warn against using ABCD² in isolation to determine urgency of investigation** — a "low-risk" ABCD² score does not exclude high-risk features (atrial fibrillation, symptomatic carotid stenosis) and should never delay urgent assessment of a patient with a genuinely suspected TIA, given relying on the score alone has been shown to risk missing these treatable causes. **The practical, current-guideline-consistent takeaway: every suspected TIA gets urgent (within 24h) assessment regardless of ABCD² score** — the score can inform triage prioritisation in a busy service, but is not a tool for deciding who can safely wait.

**Secondary prevention:**

> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — Australian guidance has a materially different first step from the UK protocol below (short-term DAPT), though the remaining drug classes are broadly similar.
> **Antiplatelet:** the Australian Living Guidelines now give a **strong recommendation for dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel together) for the first 3 weeks after a minor stroke or TIA** — this is a specific, upgraded, time-limited recommendation not present in the UK-sourced clopidogrel-monotherapy-first approach below; after the 3-week DAPT period, transition to single antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel, or aspirin + dipyridamole if clopidogrel contraindicated) for long-term secondary prevention. Statin: as per the UK figure — no material AU-specific dose difference identified. BP control: antihypertensives as needed. If AF present and haemorrhage excluded: anticoagulation with warfarin or a DOAC (dabigatran, apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) — consistent with the UK approach, no material change.

### Ischaemic Stroke

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head to exclude haemorrhagic stroke.

**Acute Mx:** all patients — give 300mg aspirin if no bleed.

> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — Australian practice differs from the UK protocol below in drug choice (tenecteplase now prominent) and reflects a more recently updated evidence base; the time windows themselves are largely consistent.
> **IV thrombolysis:** **tenecteplase (0.25mg/kg, max 25mg) is now used as an alternative to alteplase (0.9mg/kg, max 90mg) within 4.5h of onset** — a genuinely Australian-endorsed option not mentioned in the UK-sourced note below, and often preferred for large vessel occlusion specifically. Time windows:
> - Within 4.5h of symptom onset — if haemorrhage definitively excluded + no contraindication (alteplase or tenecteplase)
> - Extended to **up to 9h** after last-known-well (or from the midpoint of sleep for wake-up strokes) if brain imaging shows the patient will benefit — consistent with the UK figure already here, no change needed to this specific window.
> - New (2024 update): **recommendation against thrombolysis in non-disabling ischaemic stroke** — not part of the UK-sourced note below.
> - Lower BP to ≤185/110 before thrombolysis — no AU-specific change to this threshold.
>
> **Thrombectomy:** for large vessel occlusion (internal carotid, proximal MCA/M1 segment, or tandem cervical+intracranial occlusion) — undertaken **between 6 and 24 hours** after last-known-well if CT perfusion or MRI shows salvageable brain tissue (this AU figure frames it as a single 6–24h window based on imaging-confirmed salvageable tissue, rather than the UK's separate "within 6h" vs "within 24h" tiering — practically similar in effect, but don't teach it as two hard-separated time-based categories in an AU context). Basilar artery occlusion — thrombectomy should be undertaken in selected patients. M2-segment MCA occlusion — may be considered based on individual patient/imaging factors, a more selective indication than proximal occlusions.

> [!info] Young <55yo: screen for thrombophilia, autoimmune disorders, or cardiac disorders (e.g. ASD).

> [!info] Rosier score for stroke
> −1 for LOC/syncope or seizure; +1 for each of: asymmetric facial weakness, asymmetric arm weakness, asymmetric leg weakness, speech disturbance, visual field defect. Stroke likely if Rosier score >0.

> [!info] Gap-filled from CSV — NIHSS (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale), not covered in the source notes. A standardised 0–42 point scale quantifying stroke severity across 11 domains (level of consciousness, gaze, visual fields, facial palsy, motor arm/leg, limb ataxia, sensory, language, dysarthria, extinction/inattention) — used to objectively grade severity at presentation, track change over time (e.g. improvement/deterioration after thrombolysis), and as part of eligibility criteria for thrombolysis/thrombectomy in clinical practice and trials. Higher scores indicate more severe stroke; a score is typically documented at baseline and repeated at intervals to monitor trajectory. Unlike the Rosier score above (a bedside screening tool asking "is this a stroke?"), NIHSS answers "how severe is this stroke?" once the diagnosis is already suspected/confirmed.

> [!danger] Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis
> Previous ICH, seizure at stroke onset, intracranial neoplasm, suspected SAH, stroke or TBI in preceding 3 months, LP in preceding 7 days, GI haemorrhage in preceding 3 weeks, active bleeding, pregnancy, oesophageal varices, uncontrolled HTN >200/120

> [!warning] Relative contraindications: concurrent anticoagulation (INR >1.7), haemorrhagic diathesis, active diabetic haemorrhagic retinopathy, suspected intracardiac thrombus, major surgery/trauma in preceding 2 weeks. Note also the 2024 AU guideline update's new consensus-based recommendation on dabigatran reversal prior to thrombolysis where relevant, not detailed further in this source.

**Secondary prevention:**
> [!info] Verified against the Stroke Foundation Australia and New Zealand Living Clinical Guidelines for Stroke Management, Aug 2026 — as with TIA above, the short-term DAPT recommendation is the main AU-specific addition; remaining content is broadly consistent.
> **Antiplatelet:** as for TIA above — dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel) for the first 3 weeks after a minor ischaemic stroke is now a strong recommendation in current Australian guidance, then transitioning to single antiplatelet (clopidogrel first-line; aspirin + modified-release dipyridamole if clopidogrel contraindicated) for long-term prevention. Statin: as per the UK figure (started >48h after due to haemorrhagic transformation risk) — no material AU-specific change identified. Carotid artery endarterectomy: only if carotid stenosis >50–70%, performed within 7 days — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Carotid Artery Stenosis for the fuller AU-verified detail on this threshold and timing, not repeated here. If secondary to AF: start DOAC or warfarin 14 days after ischaemic stroke — no material AU-specific change identified.

### Haemorrhagic Stroke
Specifically referring to intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH).

**R:** HTN, ↑age, M>F, heavy alcohol use, illicit drug use, family history, blood/clotting disorders, sickle cell disease, anticoagulation, pregnancy

**A:** cerebral amyloid angiopathy and HTN can directly cause ICH. Secondary ICH: cerebral infarction or tumour, drug misuse (e.g. cocaine, methamphetamine), AVMs.

**P:** vascular rupture → bleeding into brain parenchyma → direct damage to brain tissue ± expanding haematoma causing mass effect.

**S/Smx:** similar to ischaemic stroke (clinically indistinguishable).

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head; once ICH confirmed, CT angiogram.

**Mx:**
- Stabilise patient (ABCDE) — consider endotracheal intubation
- Refer immediately to neuro and neurosurgery
- Rapid control of SBP if >150–220: aim for SBP ≤140, ensuring drop does not exceed 60mmHg within 1h
- Urgent reversal of anticoagulation
- Supportive care as necessary
- Definitive Mx if undertaken: coiling or craniotomy and clipping

### Arterial Territory Syndromes

| Artery | Territory | Presentation |
|---|---|---|
| Anterior cerebral artery | Medial and superolateral cerebral hemispheres (except occipital lobe) | Contralateral hemiparesis (lower>upper), contralateral sensory loss (lower>upper) |
| Middle cerebral artery | Lateral aspect of cerebral hemispheres | Contralateral hemiparesis (upper>lower), contralateral sensory loss (upper>lower), contralateral homonymous hemianopia, aphasia |
| Posterior cerebral artery | Inferior cerebellum and occipital lobe | Contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing, visual agnosia |
| Branches of PCA supplying midbrain (Weber's) | Midbrain | Contralateral weakness of upper/lower limbs, ipsilateral CN III palsy |
| Posterior inferior cerebellar artery (lateral medullary/Wallenberg) | Posteroinferior cerebellum | Ataxia, nystagmus, ipsilateral facial pain/temp loss, contralateral limb/torso pain/temp loss |
| Anterior inferior cerebellar artery | Inferior cerebellum | Ataxia, nystagmus, ipsilateral facial paralysis and deafness, contralateral limb/torso pain/temp loss |

*CL = contralateral (opposite side); IL = ipsilateral (same side)*

### Bamford-Oxford Classification

| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| TACS (total anterior circulation stroke) | All 3 of: unilateral weakness ± sensory deficit, homonymous hemianopia, higher cerebral dysfunction (e.g. dysphasia, visuospatial disorder) |
| PACS (partial anterior circulation stroke) | 2 of 3 above, OR: CN palsy + contralateral motor/sensory deficit; bilateral motor/sensory deficit; conjugate eye movement disorder; cerebellar dysfunction (vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia); isolated homonymous hemianopia |
| POCS (posterior circulation stroke) | Any 1 of: CN palsy + contralateral motor/sensory deficit; bilateral motor/sensory deficit; conjugate eye movement disorder; cerebellar dysfunction; isolated homonymous hemianopia |
| Lacunar | Any 1 of: pure sensory stroke, pure motor stroke, sensori-motor stroke, ataxic hemiparesis |

## Brain Bleeds

> [!info] Monro-Kellie doctrine: brain composition — parenchyma 80%, CSF 10%, blood 10%.

### Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH)

**D:** intracranial haemorrhage into the subarachnoid space ("on the surface of the brain"). Divided into traumatic and spontaneous.

**R:** HTN, smoking, family history, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease

**A:** intracranial (berry) aneurysm — 85%, associated with ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, coarctation of the aorta; AVMs; pituitary apoplexy; infective aneurysms

**S/Smx:** headache — sudden onset "thunderclap", peak intensity within 1 min of onset, occipital location, "worst headache of my life" ± history of a less severe 'sentinel' headache in the weeks prior. Nausea and vomiting. Meningism (photophobia, neck stiffness). Seizures, coma. ECG changes if a large bleed — ST elevation.

**Ix:** non-contrast CT head — SAH shows as a bright white (blood) signal, typically in the basal cisterns and sulci; ideally done within 6h of onset.
- If CT head within 6h and normal → do not do LP
- If CT head >6h and normal → do LP (should be done ≥12h after symptom onset to allow development of xanthochromia; normal or raised opening pressure)
- If CT head confirms SAH, refer ASAP to neurosurgery → order CT angiogram to guide neurosurgical Mx, order bloods/G&S/cross-match to prepare for surgery

**Mx:**
- Stabilise patient (ABCDE)
- Nimodipine to prevent vasospasm
- Supportive: bed rest, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis
- Reversal of anticoagulation
- Definitive Mx if undertaken should be within 24h: coiling, or craniotomy and clipping by neurosurgeons

**Complications:**
- Rebleeding — in 10% of patients, most common in the first 12h of initial SAH; if suspected, repeat CT head; associated with high mortality
- Hydrocephalus — bleep neurosurgeons; temporarily treated with external ventricular drain; may need long-term ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt
- Vasospasm (delayed cerebral ischaemia) — usually 7–14 days after SAH; prevented with euvolaemia; may require vasopressor treatment
- Hyponatraemia — usually secondary to SIADH
- Seizures — due to brain parenchyma damage

**P:** poorer prognostic factors — reduced consciousness on admission, ↑age, increased amount of blood visible on CT head.

### Subdural Haematoma

**D:** collection of blood between the dura and arachnoid layer of the meninges. Divided into acute, subacute, chronic.

**R:** trauma, coagulopathy/anticoagulant use, >65yo (especially chronic), ± alcoholic

**A:** trauma most common (e.g. falls, shaken baby syndrome)

**P:** torsional forces disrupt the bridging cortical veins → bleeding into the dural venous sinuses. Elderly people and alcoholics at higher risk due to brain atrophy.

**S/Smx:** wide range depending on how the trauma was sustained. Can be asymptomatic. Confusion, decreased consciousness, neurological deficit. Acute: may present with other associated injuries or be picked up on CT scan ordered for head injury. Chronic: presents over weeks to months with slowly developing confusion, decreased consciousness, neuro deficit.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* the diagnostic test, and the shape/density pattern both confirm the diagnosis and help estimate the age of the bleed, guiding urgency of Mx; *what:* sickle ("C")-shaped collection not limited by suture lines — since subdural blood can spread freely across the skull vault rather than being constrained by dural attachment points, unlike extradural bleeds below; acute — bright white signal (blood); chronic — hypodense (dark) compared to brain tissue ± mass effect (e.g. midline shift), as the haematoma liquefies and is progressively resorbed over time).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** ABCDE + resuscitate as for any head injury; reverse anticoagulation if applicable; ICP monitoring/management as needed for larger bleeds with mass effect.
- **Definitive:** conservative — watch and wait for small, asymptomatic collections; surgical — burr hole drainage (often sufficient for chronic SDH specifically, given its more liquid consistency) or decompressive craniectomy for larger/symptomatic acute collections.
- **Chronic/long-term:** monitor for recurrence, particularly in elderly/alcoholic patients where ongoing brain atrophy continues to predispose to further bleeds; address modifiable risk factors (anticoagulation review, falls risk assessment).

### Extradural (Epidural) Haematoma

**D:** collection of blood above the dural meninges — blood collects between the dura and skull.

**A:** trauma, typically a low-impact hit to the head or a fall. Collection tends to occur at the pterion (confluence of 4 skull bones) where the skull is thin and the middle meningeal artery sits underneath.

**S/Smx:**
> [!danger] Classically "talk and die": patient initially loses consciousness, then regains consciousness ("lucid interval") appearing to improve, then loses consciousness again due to expanding haematoma and brain herniation. If severe, can cause brain herniation → blown pupil due to compression of CN III.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* the diagnostic test — essential and urgent given how rapidly an EDH can expand and deteriorate, particularly during the deceptive "lucid interval"; *what:* egg-shaped bright white lesion; blood movement limited by suture lines of the skull (also described as biconvex or lentiform shaped) — this shape distinction from SDH's sickle shape reflects the dura's firm attachment at suture lines, constraining extradural blood to a lens shape).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute:** careful monitoring is essential given patients may present during the deceptively reassuring lucid interval — a normal-appearing patient with a confirmed EDH still needs urgent definitive management, not discharge.
- **Definitive:** conservative if genuinely asymptomatic and small (with close monitoring); craniotomy and evacuation of haematoma for symptomatic/significant bleeds — this is a time-critical surgical emergency given the risk of rapid deterioration and herniation.

### Cerebral Aneurysm

**D:** focal abnormal dilation of the wall of an artery in the brain.

**R:** smoking (formation, growth, rupture), moderate-high alcohol consumption (rupture), family history (especially in siblings; ?autosomal dominant), previous SAH, heritable connective tissue disease (e.g. ADPKD, Ehlers-Danlos)

**A:** haemodynamically induced injury to the vascular wall.

**S/Smx:** can be asymptomatic, picked up incidentally on imaging. Headache (variable character). Uncommon (?secondary to mass effect of the aneurysm): seizures, nuchal rigidity, decreased consciousness, focal neuro deficit.

**Ix:** CT head (*why:* first-line screen, may show the aneurysm incidentally or identify complications if symptomatic; *what:* may show the aneurysm itself if large enough, or be normal for small unruptured aneurysms) + catheter-based angiogram or CT angiogram (*why:* the definitive test for characterising aneurysm size, morphology, and location — essential for the observation-vs-treatment decision below; *what:* defines the aneurysm precisely, feeding directly into the size/growth-based Mx decision).

**Mx:**
- Unruptured: case by case — choice of observation vs treatment undertaken by neurosurgeons and patient; if symptomatic, generally treated; if enlarging (>1mm/year), treat; small aneurysms (<7mm) can generally be observed
- Ruptured: see ICH or SAH — coiling or clipping

## Seizures and Epilepsy

**Seizure:** a transient occurrence of signs/symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain.

> [!info] Epilepsy diagnosis — must fulfil any of:
> 1. ≥2 unprovoked seizures occurring >24h apart
> 2. 1 unprovoked seizure and probability of further seizures after 2 unprovoked seizures occurring over 10 years, similar to general recurrence risk
> 3. Diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with both an incidence gap and a distinct, actionable treatment-quality gap — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Epilepsy prevalence in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians is roughly double that of non-Indigenous Australians** (11 per 1,000 vs approximately half that rate, per national health survey data), and **hospital admission rates for seizures/epilepsy are around 5.6 times higher**. The disparity is even starker in acute presentations specifically: a major Far North Queensland study found Aboriginal patients comprised only ~13% of the hospital's catchment population, but **30% of ED presentations for seizure, 31% of epilepsy-related inpatient admissions, and 44% of status epilepticus admissions** — status epilepticus being the most acute, life-threatening seizure presentation, making this a genuinely severe over-representation in the highest-acuity category specifically.
> - **A genuinely distinct treatment-quality gap, not just an incidence/access gap**: research has specifically found **suboptimal anti-epileptic drug use is common among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients presenting to the emergency department with seizures** — this mirrors the same pattern already established elsewhere in this project (lung cancer surgery rates, renal transplant access, joint replacement access): Indigenous Australians are both more likely to develop a condition and less likely to receive optimal treatment for it once they do, two compounding gaps rather than one.
> - **Under-recognition and misdiagnosis are specifically flagged as contributing factors** — seizures may go unrecognised or be misdiagnosed, leading to inappropriate treatment, and unstable living circumstances can further compromise ongoing treatment adherence and follow-up. This is a genuine clinical vigilance point: a lower index of suspicion or a less thorough seizure history in this population risks compounding the disparity rather than closing it.
> - Elderly Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients specifically are a growing, high-risk group — epilepsy onset is generally more common in older age, and this population segment is projected to grow, meaning medication regimen complexity and lifestyle adjustment considerations in older patients matter increasingly rather than being a niche concern.

**Classification based on:** (1) where the seizure began in the brain, (2) level of awareness during the seizure, (3) other features of the seizure.

### Generalised Tonic-Clonic Seizures (GTCS)
1. Loss of consciousness
2. Tonic stiffening phase
3. Repetitive clonic jerking

Other symptoms: tongue biting, incontinence, irregular breathing + post-ictal confusion.

> [!info] Verified against TGA safety guidance and Epilepsy Action Australia, Aug 2026 — the underlying clinical rationale (valproate teratogenicity driving sex-specific first-line choice) is the same in Australia as in the UK, but **Australia has explicitly not adopted the UK's 2024-tightened regulatory mechanism** (mandatory pregnancy prevention programme with two-specialist sign-off before initiating/continuing valproate in women of childbearing potential) — this is a genuine, deliberate AU/UK divergence, not an oversight. Australian guidance (TGA Product Information) advises valproate should be avoided in women/people who can bear children unless alternatives are ineffective/not tolerated, with contraception advised while on treatment — but without the UK's mandatory formal programme and dual specialist sign-off. Some Australian epileptologists have specifically pushed back against importing the UK's stricter approach, citing cases of harm from inappropriately switching stable patients off valproate. The clinical drug-choice logic below (avoid valproate first-line in women of childbearing potential) still broadly holds and doesn't need correcting — but don't describe Australia as following "the same rules" as the UK's 2024 update; the underlying principle is shared, the regulatory process is not.
> **Mx (GTCS):** males — sodium valproate; females of childbearing potential — lamotrigine or levetiracetam (valproate avoided as first-line here due to teratogenicity/neurodevelopmental risk, per the above — not because of a mandatory programme requirement as in the UK)

### Generalised Seizure Subtypes
Tonic-clonic (= grand mal), tonic, clonic, typical absence (= petit mal), myoclonic (brief, rapid jerks), atonic (drop).

**Absence seizures:** involve loss of consciousness + patient stares into space (10–20s) and abruptly returns to normal.
> [!info] Mx: ethosuximide first-line regardless of sex (2nd line carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, zonisamide) — the same regardless-of-sex choice applies in Australia; ethosuximide isn't a valproate-alternative-driven choice the way GTCS/atonic Mx is, so the sex-based caveat doesn't materially apply here — no change needed from the source note beyond removing the unnecessary "verify" flag.

**Atonic seizures:** loss of consciousness + brief lapses in muscle tone (≤3 min) — patient drops to the ground.
> [!info] Mx: males — sodium valproate; females of childbearing potential — lamotrigine — same underlying valproate-avoidance rationale as GTCS above (see the box there for the AU-specific regulatory nuance); the drug choice itself is consistent with Australian practice.

### Focal Seizures
Start in a specific area of the brain, typically the temporal lobe; further divided by awareness and by motor vs non-motor features.

- **Temporal lobe seizures:** usually have an aura (e.g. rising epigastric sensation, experiential phenomena — deja vu/jamais vu, rarely hallucinations); seizures are typically automatisms lasting ~1 min (e.g. lip smacking, grabbing, plucking)
- **Frontal lobe seizures:** motor seizures — e.g. Jacksonian march (twitching starts on one side of the body and spreads proximally); post-ictal weakness (Todd's paresis = focal unilateral weakness, usually of the limbs)
- **Parietal lobe:** sensory seizures
- **Occipital lobe:** visual seizures (e.g. flashers)

> [!info] Mx (focal seizures): lamotrigine or levetiracetam first-line; carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, or zonisamide second-line — these drug class choices are internationally standard, including in Australia; no valproate-specific sex caveat applies here since valproate isn't the first-line choice for focal seizures in the first place.

### Alcohol Withdrawal Seizures
Peak incidence ~36h after last drink — **one point on a withdrawal timeline, not an isolated event.** **Delirium tremens peaks later, at 48–72h**, and is the phase that carries the mortality, so a patient who has had a withdrawal seizure at 36h is heading into the higher-risk window rather than past it. The full timeline, the CIWA-Ar/AWS severity scoring and the AU-verified regimen are owned by [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol withdrawal. Carried here by the G21 round (2026-08-29): this entry gave the seizure timing alone.
> [!info] Verified against NSW Health/WA Mental Health Commission alcohol withdrawal guidelines (cross-referenced from the earlier localisation of this same content in [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol withdrawal), Aug 2026 — **diazepam is the benzodiazepine of choice in Australia; chlordiazepoxide is not registered/available in Australia**, so don't reach for it as an equal-first-line option the way the UK-sourced note does. See [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) → Alcohol withdrawal for the fuller AU-specific regimen (diazepam dosing/tapering, thiamine dosing) — not repeated here.

### Status Epilepticus

**D:** (1) a single seizure lasting >5 min, OR (2) ≥2 seizures within a 5-min period without the individual returning to baseline in between.

> [!danger] Medical emergency.

**Mx:**
- Airway: secure airway
- Breathing: high-flow oxygen
- Circulation: IV access if possible
- Disability: check blood glucose

> [!info] Verified — the overall structure (benzodiazepine first-line → second-line agent if ongoing at 5–10min → general anaesthesia if refractory beyond ~45min) reflects standard international practice, including the drug classes used in Australia (levetiracetam, phenytoin, and sodium valproate are all standard eTG/AMH second-line choices here) — no major structural correction needed, but note: buccal midazolam and PR diazepam (no-IV-access options) and IV lorazepam (IV-access option) are all used in Australia in the same roles as below; the specific repeat-timing and second-line agent choice are standard practice rather than a UK-specific protocol.
> Benzodiazepines: no IV access — PR diazepam or buccal midazolam; IV access — IV lorazepam; repeat once after 5–10 min. If >5–10 min, start second-line agent (e.g. IV levetiracetam, phenytoin infusion, or sodium valproate). If >45 min (refractory), general anaesthesia or phenobarbital.

## Anticonvulsants / Antiepileptics

> [!info] Verified — the adverse effect profiles, mechanisms, and monitoring principles below (LFT/FBC monitoring for valproate/carbamazepine, trough-level monitoring for phenytoin, Stevens-Johnson risk for lamotrigine/carbamazepine, CYP450 induction effects) are pharmacological properties of the drugs themselves, not jurisdiction-specific prescribing conventions — no change needed. The drug names/classes are all standard AMH/eTG choices in Australian practice. See the Seizures and Epilepsy section above for the AU-specific nuance on sodium valproate prescribing in women of childbearing potential (Australia has not adopted the UK's 2024 mandatory pregnancy-prevention-programme requirement, though the same underlying teratogenicity caution applies) — not repeated here.

**Sodium valproate:** modulates voltage-gated Na channels (binds inactivated Na channel, use-dependent blocking action).
- AE: CYP450 inhibitor, teratogenic (neural tube defects), liver damage, pancreatitis, GI upset, weight gain, hair loss (may regrow curly), tremor, ataxia, thrombocytopenia, hyponatraemia
- To stop, reduce dose over 4 weeks
- Monitoring: LFT and FBC (baseline and during first 6 months)
- Pregnancy: females on sodium valproate should be on contraception

**Lamotrigine:** Na channel blocker. AE: Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Monitoring: no specific requirements.

**Levetiracetam:** ?Ca channel blocker. AE: blood dyscrasias, QT prolongation. Monitoring: no specific requirements.

**Carbamazepine:** binds voltage-gated Na channels, increases refractory period.
- AE: P450 enzyme inducer (auto-inducer — may increase clearance of itself; seizures may return 3–4 weeks after starting/increasing dose, needing titration until this stops), dizziness, ataxia, drowsiness, headache, visual disturbances (especially diplopia), Stevens-Johnson syndrome, leukopenia, agranulocytosis, hyponatraemia (secondary to SIADH)
- Monitoring: LFT and FBC (no clear guidance on frequency)

**Phenytoin:** MOA similar to carbamazepine.
- AE: dizziness, ataxia, diplopia, nystagmus, slurred speech, dyskinesia, gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism, coarsening facial features, megaloblastic anaemia (secondary to folate metabolism dysfunction), peripheral neuropathy, toxic epidermal necrolysis, hepatitis/liver failure, aplastic anaemia, teratogenic (cleft palate, congenital heart disease), CYP450 inducer
- Monitoring: check if starting/adjusting dose, suspected toxicity, or suspected non-adherence — measure trough (lowest) levels immediately before dose

> [!note] Driving standards for epilepsy/seizures are covered under Austroads Driving Standards (Neurological Conditions), further down this file. Epilepsy in children is covered elsewhere in this source (paediatrics section).

## Psychogenic Non-Epileptic Seizures (PNES)

**D:** episodic events that appear similar to epileptic seizures but without abnormal EEG findings. Do not refer to as "pseudoseizures".

**R:** trauma (including PTSD), acute stressors, mood and personality disorders; can occur alongside epilepsy

**A/P:** poorly understood; previously thought of as a conversion disorder. Patients have actual structural neurological changes and should not be thought of as "making up" their seizures.

**S/Smx:** PNES appears similar to epileptic events, but some features point toward PNES: pelvic thrusting, family member with epilepsy, occurring only with others around, gradual onset, prolonged event (>10 min), forced eye closure during the seizure, crying during/after the seizure, remembering events during the seizure, lack of post-ictal confusion. PNES can still have features resembling epileptic seizures, including urinary incontinence and tongue biting.

**Ix:** EEG (*why:* the key distinguishing test — a normal EEG during a clinical event strongly supports PNES over epileptic seizure, given epileptic seizures should show ictal EEG abnormalities; *what:* normal EEG during the event itself, ideally captured on video telemetry). Video telemetry (*why:* combines EEG with video recording, allowing direct correlation between the clinical event and EEG trace — the most definitive diagnostic approach; *what:* captures a typical event with no EEG correlate). Serum prolactin (*why:* a supportive adjunct test — prolactin characteristically rises after a true epileptic (especially generalised tonic-clonic) seizure but not after PNES; *what:* ↑prolactin favours epileptic seizures, though a normal level doesn't exclude epilepsy since the rise can be missed if not timed correctly).

**Mx:**
- **Immediate/acute** (at diagnosis): explain the diagnosis empathetically — how this is communicated matters significantly, given patients have often been previously told (incorrectly) they are "faking" their seizures; emphasise this is a real, involuntary condition with a different mechanism from epilepsy, not a fabrication.
- **Chronic/long-term:** no consensus on best treatment; CBT + treat comorbid psychiatric conditions (trauma, mood/personality disorders) — addressing the underlying psychological drivers rather than anti-epileptic medication, which has no role here unless genuine comorbid epilepsy is also present.

## Syncope

**D:** sudden and transient loss of consciousness associated with loss of postural tone, resolving spontaneously and completely without intervention.

**A:** divided into cardiac and non-cardiac causes

**P:** common end-point in syncopal syndromes is transient insufficiency of cerebral blood flow.

**Ix:** cardiovascular examination (*why:* screens for a structural/arrhythmic cardiac cause, which carries a worse prognosis than reflex syncope and needs active exclusion; *what:* murmurs, irregular pulse, or other signs of structural heart disease). Postural blood pressure measurement — lying-standing BP (*why:* directly tests for orthostatic hypotension as the cause, a common and easily-confirmed diagnosis; *what:* ↓SBP >20 or ↓DBP >10 is diagnostic of orthostatic hypotension). ECG for all patients (*why:* screens for arrhythmic and structural causes (long QT, pre-excitation, conduction disease, evidence of prior MI/structural disease) — a mandatory baseline test in any syncope work-up given how much a cardiac cause changes management/prognosis; *what:* may show conduction abnormalities, arrhythmia, or be normal). Other tests depend on clinical features (e.g. echo if structural heart disease suspected, tilt-table testing for suspected reflex syncope, ambulatory ECG monitoring if arrhythmia suspected but not captured on resting ECG).

### Cardiac syncope
- Arrhythmias: bradycardias (AV conduction, e.g. heart block — Stokes-Adams attack; sinus node dysfunction), tachycardias (supraventricular or ventricular)
- Structural: valvular heart disease, MI, HOCM
- Others: pulmonary embolism

> [!note] Stokes-Adams attack = transient LOC secondary to AV block causing decreased cardiac output.

### Non-cardiac syncope

**Reflex (neurally-mediated) syncope:** symptomatic hypotension resulting from neural reflex vasodilation and/or bradycardia.
- Vasovagal syncope: stimuli (emotional, pain, etc.) → vagal nerve stimulation → vasodilation & ↓BP → ↓cerebral perfusion → syncope
- Carotid sinus syndrome: pressure on carotid sinus → ↓HR, vasodilation, ↓BP
- Situational: cough, micturition, GI stimulation (e.g. swallowing, defecation)
- Reflex anoxic seizures (not a true seizure): syncope in response to pain/emotional stimuli; ?neurally-mediated transient asystole in children (6mo–3yo); child goes pale → drops to floor → rapid recovery; no long-term sequelae

**Orthostatic syncope:** dysautonomia (e.g. Parkinson's disease, diabetic neuropathy), hypovolaemia, drug-induced (e.g. β-blockers)

**Syncope secondary to anatomical problems:** subclavian steal syndrome (subclavian artery steals blood from vertebrobasilar circulation, reducing cerebral blood flow)

**Neurological causes:** SAH, traumatic brain injury, migraine (uncommon)

**Metabolic:** hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hyperventilation + hypocapnia

**Mx of reflex syncope:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute Mx tier — episodes are self-resolving by definition):** patient education usually sufficient — prevent known triggers (e.g. good hydration), know prodromal symptoms, respond and try to abort (physical counter-pressure manoeuvres — squatting, arm tensing, leg crossing), standing training (progressively longer periods).
- **Pharmacological (for refractory/frequent episodes):** fludrocortisone (volume expansion), midodrine (α1-agonist).

> [!note] Presyncope — feeling of almost passing out without loss of consciousness; same causes as syncope. Cataplexy = sudden muscle weakness triggered by strong emotions (e.g. laughter).

## Movement Disorders

### Parkinson's Disease (PD)

**D:** chronic progressive neurological disorder characterised by motor symptoms of resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability.

**R:** ↑age, M>F, family history, genetics, MPTP exposure

**A:** considered a sporadic disorder, with some genetic forms; environmental factors, oxidative stress.

**P:** selective loss of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta + Lewy bodies and neurites (composed of synuclein).

> [!info] S/Smx — mnemonic "TRAP"
> **T**remor (resting), **R**igidity (cogwheel), **A**kinesia/bradykinesia, **P**ostural instability. Others: mask-like facies, micrographia, depression (40%), sleep disorder. Drug-induced parkinsonism tends to be more symmetrical than idiopathic PD.

> [!tip] Clinical features
> Parkinsonian gait — slowed movement, ↓arm swing, rigidity, freezing, shuffling steps, postural instability; asymmetric resting tremor that improves on voluntary movements; mask-like face; glabella tap — patient cannot stop blinking.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis based on history and examination (*why:* PD remains a clinical diagnosis — no blood test or routine imaging confirms it; Ix is used to exclude alternative causes (e.g. drug-induced parkinsonism, Parkinson-plus syndromes — see below) rather than to positively diagnose PD; *what:* the TRAP features above, with asymmetry and a good initial levodopa response supporting idiopathic PD over the alternatives).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — chronic progressive disease managed via specialist titration over time):** referral to specialist. If motor symptoms affect QoL → levodopa. If motor symptoms don't affect QoL → dopamine agonist, levodopa, or MAO-B inhibitor. Risk of impulse control disorders with these medications — ensure patients know warning signs. Medication review to remove drug-drug interactions.

> [!warning] Risk factors for faster progression: older age at symptom onset, rigidity/bradykinesia as presenting symptoms, associated comorbidities, decreased response to dopaminergic medications.

**P:** after 5 years of levodopa treatment, motor complications develop; eventually symptoms such as freezing, falling, and dementia develop. Cognitive dysfunction and non-dopaminergic symptoms are the main determinants of increased disability in the first 5 years of disease.

### PD drug classes

**Levodopa:** precursor to dopamine. Usually combined with a decarboxylase inhibitor (e.g. carbidopa) to decrease peripheral metabolism → fewer side effects.
- SE: end-of-dose wearing off, on-off phenomenon (large variations in motor performance), dyskinesias at peak dose (dystonia, chorea, athetosis)

> [!danger] Do NOT abruptly stop levodopa. If a patient cannot take PO medications, a dopamine agonist (e.g. cabergoline) can be given as rescue medication.

**MAO-B inhibitors** (e.g. selegiline): not often used first-line. MOA — inhibits dopamine breakdown. SE: arrhythmias, anticholinergic effects, can potentiate levodopa effects (may need to reduce levodopa dose 10–30%). Avoid with cheese (tyramine reaction).

**Dopamine agonists** (e.g. ropinirole, apomorphine, cabergoline, bromocriptine): MOA — activate dopamine receptors. SE: increased risk of impulse control disorders; excessive daytime somnolence (do not drive/operate machinery); hallucinations in older patients; increased risk of pulmonary, retroperitoneal, and cardiac fibrosis with cabergoline and bromocriptine (baseline echo, CXR, ESR, creatinine; monitor closely).

**COMT inhibitors** (e.g. entacapone, tolcapone): MOA — inhibits dopamine breakdown; often used as adjunct to levodopa.

**Antimuscarinics** (e.g. procyclidine, benzatropine): MOA — blocks cholinergic receptors; often used to treat drug-induced parkinsonism.

> [!warning] Dopaminergic drug adverse effects: dyskinesia, on-off phenomenon, psychosis, ↓arterial BP, mouth dryness, insomnia, nausea/vomiting, excessive daytime sleepiness.

### Parkinson-Plus Syndromes

**D:** disorders with classic features of PD, plus distinguishing features.

**Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP):** rigidity and frequent falls; problems with balance and mobility; difficulty looking up/down and blinking (staring expression, double vision); behaviour change (apathy, impulsivity); dysarthria and dysphagia ("chaotic dysarthrophonia", less spontaneous speech); slowness of thought, irritability, mood changes. Named for the supranuclear gaze palsy causing restriction of voluntary eye movements in the vertical plane.

**Corticobasal Degeneration (CBD):** limb rigidity or akinesia, limb dystonia, limb myoclonus, orobuccal or limb apraxia, cortical sensory deficit, alien limb phenomena — usually asymmetrical, with cortical signs.

**Multiple System Atrophy (MSA):** autonomic failure (urinary incontinence, orthostatic hypotension within 3 min of standing — SBP drop ≥30 or DBP drop ≥15 mmHg); parkinsonian symptoms not responsive to levodopa; cerebellar syndrome (gait ± limb ataxia, dysarthria, oculomotor dysfunction).

### Motor Neuron Disease (MND)

**D:** group of rare neurodegenerative disorders that selectively affect motor neurons. Divided into 4 subtypes:
- ALS — 50% (see below)
- Primary lateral sclerosis: only UMN signs first; slower progression than ALS
- Progressive muscular atrophy: only LMN signs first; best prognosis
- Progressive bulbar palsy: worst prognosis

### Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

**D:** progressive disease characterised by degeneration of motor neurons with cortical, brainstem, and ventral cord locations.

**R:** genetics (≤7%), >40yo

**A:** unknown; various theories

**P:** progressive loss of cortical (frontotemporal), bulbar (pons, medulla), and ventral cord motor neurons → retrograde axonal degeneration → denervation and reinnervation in affected muscles.

**S/Smx:** UMN + LMN signs; clinical diagnosis. Asymmetrical limb involvement (70%), then spreads to a contiguous motor neuron segment (e.g. cervical → thoracic). UMN signs: weakness, spasticity, ↑reflexes (Babinski's, jaw jerk). LMN signs: weakness, atrophy, fasciculations (tongue fasciculations = bulbar onset MND). No remissions — progressive disease.

**Ix:** nerve conduction studies (*why:* demonstrates the LMN component and excludes mimics such as peripheral neuropathy or a treatable neuromuscular junction disorder; *what:* normal motor conduction velocity despite clinical weakness — a key distinguishing feature, since the pathology is at the anterior horn cell/motor neuron level, not the peripheral nerve itself). MRI (*why:* excludes structural mimics — cord compression or myelopathy can produce a similar UMN+LMN picture and must be ruled out before accepting an ALS diagnosis; *what:* typically normal in ALS itself, used to exclude alternative causes). ± LP (*why:* excludes an infective/inflammatory mimic when the diagnosis is less certain; *what:* normal in ALS, used to exclude infection).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — this is a progressive neurodegenerative disease managed via multidisciplinary care over time):** riluzole (reduces glutamate receptor stimulation — prolongs life ~3 months); BiPAP at night once respiratory function declines (survival benefit ~7 months); nutrition via PEG once swallowing is significantly affected — each of these interventions targets a different domain of progressive decline (pharmacological, respiratory, nutritional) rather than being alternatives to each other.
- **Chronic/long-term:** multidisciplinary care (physio, OT, speech pathology, palliative care involvement early given the progressive, ultimately terminal course); advance care planning discussions given the eventual respiratory failure that characterises disease progression.

**P:** mean survival 3–5 years. Prolonged survival with NIV (BiPAP), enteral nutrition, younger age at diagnosis, limb onset, baseline FVC >75%. Negative prognostic factors: older age, bulbar onset, frontotemporal dementia, FVC <75%.

### Huntington Disease

**D:** slowly progressive neurodegenerative disorder with specific symptoms.

**R:** expansion of the CAG repeat length at the N-terminal end of the huntingtin gene (≥40 copies), family history

**A:** autosomal dominant, trinucleotide repeat disorder

**P:** expanded CAG repeat generates an elongated polyglutamine tail on the huntingtin protein, leading to cleavage and generation of toxic fragments — primarily affects the striatum.

**S/Smx:** earliest symptoms relate to loss of executive function (↓concentration, increased errors in complex tasks, slips, misjudgments, needing more help). Behavioural symptoms (irritability, impulsivity, personality changes). Motor symptoms (chorea, twitching, rigidity, bradykinesia, saccadic eye movements). "Anticipation" — disease presents earlier in successive generations.

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis (*why:* the clinical picture in the context of a positive family history is often highly suggestive; *what:* the S/Smx pattern above). CAG testing (*why:* the definitive genetic diagnostic test, and central to genetic counselling for at-risk relatives given the autosomal dominant inheritance and anticipation phenomenon; *what:* confirms the pathogenic CAG repeat expansion, with repeat length correlating loosely with age of onset). MRI/CT (*why:* supports the diagnosis and excludes alternative causes of chorea; *what:* may show caudate/striatal atrophy in established disease, though normal early on).

**Mx:**
- **Definitive/chronic (no acute tier — a chronic progressive disease):** counselling and supportive care (ensure good support system, including genetic counselling for at-risk family members given the inheritance pattern); behavioural symptoms — SSRIs, CBT; motor symptoms (specialist Mx) — tetrabenazine, antipsychotics, benzodiazepines.
- **Chronic/long-term:** staged care planning as the disease progresses through the stages below, with increasing focus on safety, dependency care, and eventually end-of-life planning.

**P:** ~20 years from diagnosis to death. Stage I: disability. Stage II: managing behaviour. Stage III: dependence. Stage IV: placement, end of life.
