## 0.1 Eyelid Disorders

> [!danger] A recurrent chalazion in the same place is a carcinoma until biopsied
> **CHALAZION — a chronic, painless, granulomatous lump from a blocked meibomian gland, treated with warm compresses, lid massage and lid hygiene, and incised if persistent.**
> **STYE (external hordeolum) — an acute, tender, superficial infection at the lash line, treated with warm compresses.**
> **THE RED FLAG: A CHALAZION THAT RECURS AT THE SAME SITE, particularly in an older patient, or one accompanied by LOSS OF EYELASHES (madarosis), destruction or distortion of the LID MARGIN, or chronic unilateral "blepharitis" that will not settle — IS SEBACEOUS CARCINOMA UNTIL BIOPSY SAYS OTHERWISE.**
> **Sebaceous carcinoma of the eyelid characteristically masquerades as a chalazion or as chronic blepharitis, and the diagnosis is typically delayed by months to years while it is repeatedly incised.**
> **The rule: incise a chalazion once; if it comes back in the same place, send it for histology.**

> [!warning] Eyelid malignancy — lash loss means infiltration
> **BASAL CELL CARCINOMA is the commonest eyelid malignancy, with a predilection for the LOWER LID and the MEDIAL CANTHUS.**
> **THE FEATURES THAT MATTER: a pearly nodule with surface TELANGIECTASIA · central ULCERATION · INDURATION · DISTORTION of the normal lid margin architecture · and — the most useful single sign — LOSS OF EYELASHES over the lesion, which indicates destruction of the follicles by infiltrating tumour.**
> **MEDIAL CANTHAL tumours are particularly important because the tissue planes there allow deep, silent spread into the orbit and along nerves, and reconstruction is difficult** — so they warrant specialist oculoplastic management and often Mohs surgery.
> **Also occurring: squamous cell carcinoma, sebaceous carcinoma (above) and melanoma.**
> **ANY PERSISTENT EYELID LESION WITH LASH LOSS, ULCERATION OR LID MARGIN DISTORTION NEEDS BIOPSY.** Cross-refer [[G5]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Entropion, ectropion, and the lid that will not close
> **· ENTROPION — the lid turns IN, so the LASHES ABRADE THE CORNEA. Causes irritation, watering, corneal abrasion and ultimately ulceration and scarring.** Temporised with taping and lubricants; corrected surgically.
> **· ECTROPION — the lid turns OUT, so the eye is EXPOSED and the punctum no longer sits against the globe. Causes exposure keratopathy and a watering eye (see 0.2).**
> **Both damage the cornea, by opposite mechanisms — one by abrasion, one by exposure.**
> **· FACIAL NERVE PALSY AND EXPOSURE KERATOPATHY — the single most important lid point in general practice.**
> **A patient who cannot close the eye (lagophthalmos) and who has lost the protective Bell phenomenon is at risk of corneal drying, ulceration, perforation and permanent visual loss WITHIN DAYS.**
> **EVERY PATIENT WITH A FACIAL PALSY NEEDS: frequent LUBRICATING DROPS during the day · OINTMENT at night · and the eye TAPED SHUT or a moisture chamber overnight · plus review of the cornea.**
> **This is routinely omitted from Bell palsy management, where all the attention goes to steroids and antivirals — and the cornea is what is actually at risk.** Cross-refer [[D5]] and [[F1]] 0.2.

## 0.2 The Watering Eye

> [!tip] Two mechanisms, and the counterintuitive one
> **· OVERPRODUCTION (reflex tearing) — from any ocular surface irritation: DRY EYE (the paradox — an under-lubricated surface triggers reflex tearing, so dry eye presents as a watery eye), blepharitis, trichiasis, a foreign body, corneal disease, or allergy.**
> **· IMPAIRED DRAINAGE — punctal stenosis or malposition, canalicular obstruction, NASOLACRIMAL DUCT OBSTRUCTION, or lid laxity and ECTROPION preventing the punctum contacting the tear lake.**
> **Treating a watering eye with more lubricant when the problem is drainage achieves nothing — and treating it as a drainage problem when the cause is dry eye or blepharitis leads to unnecessary procedures.**

> [!danger] A watering infant — exclude congenital glaucoma
> **CONGENITAL NASOLACRIMAL DUCT OBSTRUCTION is very common, presenting with watering and a sticky eye from the early weeks, with a WHITE, COMFORTABLE eye and normal corneal appearance.** **The great majority resolve spontaneously within the first year with massage and hygiene; probing is considered later.**
> **BUT THE TRIAD THAT MUST NOT BE MISSED: WATERING + PHOTOPHOBIA + BLEPHAROSPASM = CONGENITAL GLAUCOMA.**
> **Look also for a LARGE eye or cornea (BUPHTHALMOS) and a HAZY or cloudy cornea.**
> **A baby who screws the eyes shut in light and waters is not a blocked duct — it is an emergency requiring same-day ophthalmology, because raised pressure is destroying the optic nerve and stretching the globe.**
> **The distinguishing feature is that a blocked duct is a COMFORTABLE eye and glaucoma is an UNCOMFORTABLE one.**

> [!warning] Dacryocystitis
> **Infection of the lacrimal sac, producing a TENDER, RED, SWOLLEN mass BELOW AND MEDIAL TO the medial canthus — and the location below the medial canthal tendon is what distinguishes it from other periorbital swellings.**
> **Pressure over the sac may express pus or mucus from the punctum.**
> **It requires systemic antibiotics, may need drainage, and can progress to PRESEPTAL AND ORBITAL CELLULITIS.** Definitive management of the underlying obstruction (dacryocystorhinostomy) follows.
> **DACRYOADENITIS — inflammation of the lacrimal gland in the SUPEROLATERAL orbit, producing an "S-SHAPED" deformity of the upper lid.** Causes include viral infection, sarcoidosis, IgG4-related disease and lymphoma.

## 0.3 Proptosis and Orbital Disease

> [!danger] Thyroid eye disease — the sight-threatening complications
> **The commonest cause of proptosis in adults, and it can occur when the patient is euthyroid, hypothyroid or hyperthyroid.**
> **Features: LID RETRACTION (sclera visible above the limbus) and LID LAG · PROPTOSIS · RESTRICTIVE MYOPATHY causing diplopia (the inferior and medial recti are affected first, so upgaze and abduction are limited) · conjunctival injection and CHEMOSIS · and periorbital swelling.**
> **THE TWO SIGHT-THREATENING COMPLICATIONS:**
> **1. EXPOSURE KERATOPATHY — from proptosis and lid retraction preventing closure. Managed with lubrication and taping, and sometimes surgery.**
> **2. COMPRESSIVE OPTIC NEUROPATHY — enlarged muscles compressing the nerve at the orbital apex.** **THE SIGNS ARE REDUCED ACUITY, RED DESATURATION and an RAPD — not necessarily worse proptosis.** **It is an emergency requiring high-dose steroids, radiotherapy or urgent surgical decompression.**
> **SO: CHECK ACUITY, COLOUR VISION AND PUPILS AT EVERY REVIEW OF THYROID EYE DISEASE — the proptosis is what everyone looks at, and the optic nerve is what is lost.**
> **SMOKING is a strong, dose-related and MODIFIABLE risk factor for developing thyroid eye disease and for it worsening — and it reduces the response to treatment.** **This is one of the most compelling smoking cessation conversations in medicine and it should be had explicitly.**
> **RADIOIODINE treatment can precipitate or worsen thyroid eye disease, which affects the choice of treatment for the hyperthyroidism, sometimes with steroid cover.** Cross-refer [[I1]] 0.3.

> [!warning] The other causes of proptosis
> **· ORBITAL CELLULITIS — proptosis with PAINFUL, RESTRICTED eye movements, reduced acuity and systemic illness. An emergency.** Cross-refer [[M2]] 0.4 and [[F2]] 0.2.
> **· ORBITAL TUMOURS — lymphoma (a painless, "salmon-pink" conjunctival or orbital mass in an older patient), metastases, lacrimal gland tumours, cavernous haemangioma, and optic nerve glioma or meningioma.**
> **· IN CHILDREN, two that are urgent: RHABDOMYOSARCOMA — RAPIDLY PROGRESSIVE proptosis over days to weeks in a child, which is a malignancy requiring immediate referral · and METASTATIC NEUROBLASTOMA, which classically causes PERIORBITAL ECCHYMOSIS ("raccoon eyes") with proptosis.** **Rapidly progressive proptosis in a child is a cancer until proven otherwise.**
> **· IDIOPATHIC ORBITAL INFLAMMATION ("orbital pseudotumour") — painful proptosis with restricted movements, dramatically steroid-responsive, and a diagnosis of exclusion.**
> **· CAROTID-CAVERNOUS FISTULA — PULSATILE proptosis with an audible BRUIT (listen over the globe and temple), dilated "CORKSCREW" conjunctival vessels, chemosis and raised intraocular pressure, typically after head trauma.**
> **· CAVERNOUS SINUS THROMBOSIS — proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia and V1/V2 sensory loss, characteristically BECOMING BILATERAL.** Cross-refer [[F2]] 0.2.

> [!tip] Assessing proptosis
> **· AXIAL versus NON-AXIAL displacement.** **Axial (straight forward) suggests a lesion within the muscle cone or a diffuse process such as thyroid eye disease; NON-AXIAL (displaced up, down or sideways) suggests a mass OUTSIDE the cone, and the direction of displacement points to where it is.**
> **· Look from ABOVE AND BEHIND the seated patient, over the brow, to compare the two eyes — proptosis is far easier to see from that angle than from the front.**
> **· Assess RESISTANCE TO RETROPULSION (gently pressing the globe back through the closed lid) — increased resistance suggests an orbital mass or congestion.**
> **· And always: ACUITY, COLOUR VISION, PUPILS FOR AN RAPD, eye movements, and the fundus for disc swelling or choroidal folds.**

## 0.4 The Abnormal Pupil

> [!danger] Compare the pupils in bright light and in darkness — that identifies which one is abnormal
> **THE SINGLE MOST USEFUL CONCEPT IN ANISOCORIA.**
> **· IF THE DIFFERENCE IS GREATER IN BRIGHT LIGHT → the LARGER pupil is the abnormal one, because it is failing to CONSTRICT.**
> **Causes: THIRD NERVE PALSY · ADIE TONIC PUPIL · PHARMACOLOGICAL dilatation · iris trauma or sphincter damage · acute angle closure.**
> **· IF THE DIFFERENCE IS GREATER IN DARKNESS → the SMALLER pupil is the abnormal one, because it is failing to DILATE.**
> **Causes: HORNER SYNDROME · physiological anisocoria · pharmacological miosis (opioids, pilocarpine) · iritis with synechiae.**
> **PHYSIOLOGICAL ANISOCORIA is common — present in a substantial minority of people — and is identified by a difference that is ROUGHLY EQUAL IN LIGHT AND DARK, with normal reactions, normal lid position and no other signs.** **Old photographs (including on the patient's phone or driver's licence) are genuinely useful for showing it is long-standing.**

> [!danger] A painful Horner syndrome is a carotid dissection
> **HORNER SYNDROME: PTOSIS (partial and mild — the Müller muscle is sympathetically innervated, unlike the levator) · MIOSIS · and ANHIDROSIS, with apparent enophthalmos.**
> **THE ANHIDROSIS LOCALISES THE LESION — whole hemiface implies a central or preganglionic lesion; forehead only or absent implies postganglionic.** Cross-refer [[G6]] 0.5.
> **THE CAUSES BY NEURONE:**
> **· FIRST-ORDER (central) — brainstem stroke (LATERAL MEDULLARY/Wallenberg syndrome), cervical cord lesion, syringomyelia, multiple sclerosis, tumour.**
> **· SECOND-ORDER (preganglionic) — an APICAL LUNG TUMOUR (PANCOAST), thyroid or neck surgery, cervical rib, trauma, mediastinal mass.** **A Horner syndrome with arm pain or hand wasting is a Pancoast tumour until imaged.**
> **· THIRD-ORDER (postganglionic) — INTERNAL CAROTID ARTERY DISSECTION, cavernous sinus pathology, cluster headache.**
> **THE URGENT ONE: A HORNER SYNDROME WITH IPSILATERAL NECK, FACE OR HEAD PAIN IS A CAROTID DISSECTION UNTIL PROVEN OTHERWISE.**
> **It requires urgent vascular imaging, because dissection precedes stroke — often by hours to days — and antithrombotic treatment prevents it.** Cross-refer [[D3]] 0.4.
> **A HORNER SYNDROME IN AN INFANT, particularly with HETEROCHROMIA (a paler iris on the affected side, because sympathetic innervation is needed for iris pigmentation in the first years), may be congenital — but NEUROBLASTOMA must be excluded.**

> [!warning] The specific pupil syndromes
> **· THIRD NERVE PALSY — ptosis, the eye "down and out", and the pupil.** **PUPIL-INVOLVING (dilated, unreactive) = COMPRESSIVE = POSTERIOR COMMUNICATING ARTERY ANEURYSM until angiography says otherwise.** **PUPIL-SPARING = more often microvascular (diabetes, hypertension), painful but self-limiting.** Cross-refer [[E2]] 0.6.
> **· ADIE (tonic) PUPIL — typically a YOUNG WOMAN with a unilateral DILATED pupil that reacts POORLY OR NOT AT ALL TO LIGHT but constricts SLOWLY AND TONICALLY to a near target, then REDILATES SLOWLY.** **This is LIGHT-NEAR DISSOCIATION.** **It shows DENERVATION SUPERSENSITIVITY — constricting to DILUTE pilocarpine that would not affect a normal pupil, which is the confirmatory test.** **Benign. With absent deep tendon reflexes it is Holmes-Adie syndrome.**
> **· ARGYLL ROBERTSON PUPIL — small, irregular pupils with LIGHT-NEAR DISSOCIATION ("accommodates but does not react"), classically bilateral, associated with NEUROSYPHILIS.** Rare, but the classic teaching example, and a reason to consider syphilis serology in unexplained pupillary abnormality.
> **· PHARMACOLOGICAL DILATATION — the everyday one that causes alarm.** **NEBULISED IPRATROPIUM escaping from a poorly fitting mask is a well-recognised cause of a unilateral fixed dilated pupil in an emergency department or intensive care patient, and it triggers unnecessary neuroimaging.** Also hyoscine patches (transferred by the fingers to the eye), and plant alkaloids. **The test: a pharmacologically blocked pupil will NOT constrict to full-strength pilocarpine, whereas a third nerve palsy pupil will.**
> **· A FIXED DILATED PUPIL IN AN UNCONSCIOUS PATIENT suggests uncal herniation and is a neurosurgical emergency — but consider drugs, direct ocular trauma and pre-existing abnormality before acting on it alone.** Cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.7.

## 0.5 Ptosis and the Neuro-ophthalmic Patterns

> [!danger] Three patterns of ptosis, defined by the pupil
> **THIS IS THE HIGH-YIELD FRAMEWORK — look at the pupil, and the diagnosis follows.**
> **1. PTOSIS + DILATED PUPIL + eye "DOWN AND OUT" → THIRD NERVE PALSY → urgent imaging for an ANEURYSM.**
> **2. PTOSIS (partial) + CONSTRICTED PUPIL → HORNER SYNDROME → and if painful, carotid dissection.**
> **3. PTOSIS that is FATIGABLE and VARIABLE, WITH NORMAL PUPILS → MYASTHENIA GRAVIS.**
> **THE PUPIL IS NEVER INVOLVED IN MYASTHENIA — which is why myasthenia can mimic any pupil-sparing ocular motor palsy, and why a normal pupil with variable ptosis and diplopia should prompt the question "is it worse at the end of the day?" and a test of fatigability on sustained upgaze.** Cross-refer [[D4]] 0.3.
> **The other causes: APONEUROTIC (age-related dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis — the commonest cause overall, with a HIGH lid crease and PRESERVED levator function, bilateral and gradual) · MYOGENIC (myotonic dystrophy, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia, oculopharyngeal muscular dystrophy) · MECHANICAL (a lid mass, oedema, scarring) · and CONGENITAL.**

> [!danger] Congenital ptosis and strabismus threaten sight through amblyopia
> **AMBLYOPIA is the failure of visual development in one eye because the brain does not receive a clear, aligned image during the CRITICAL PERIOD of visual development in early childhood.**
> **IT IS TREATABLE ONLY WITHIN THAT WINDOW. After it closes, the visual loss is PERMANENT and untreatable.**
> **The three causes: STRABISMUS (the brain suppresses the deviating eye) · REFRACTIVE difference between the eyes (anisometropia) · and DEPRIVATION (congenital cataract, congenital ptosis obscuring the visual axis, corneal opacity).**
> **THEREFORE:**
> **· CONGENITAL PTOSIS covering the visual axis requires urgent referral.**
> **· STRABISMUS IS NEVER NORMAL BEYOND EARLY INFANCY.** **A persistent squint requires referral — not "he'll grow out of it".** **Test with the CORNEAL LIGHT REFLEX (asymmetry indicates deviation) and the COVER TEST.** **Note that a broad nasal bridge can produce PSEUDO-strabismus with a symmetrical light reflex, which is reassuring — but that distinction is made by testing, not by assuming.**
> **· LEUKOCORIA — a white pupillary reflex, or an ABSENT/ABNORMAL RED REFLEX — is RETINOBLASTOMA or congenital CATARACT until proven otherwise, and both are emergencies. Parents often notice it first in photographs, where one eye fails to show "red eye".** **Take that report seriously.** Cross-refer [[M3]] 0.6 and [[M4]] 0.5.
> **This is the entire justification for red reflex testing at every newborn and child health check.**

## 0.6 The Optic Disc, Nystagmus and Visual Hallucinations

> [!warning] Papilloedema
> **Bilateral optic disc swelling caused by RAISED INTRACRANIAL PRESSURE.**
> **Signs: blurred, elevated disc margins · loss of the physiological cup · engorged veins with loss of spontaneous venous pulsation · peripapillary haemorrhages and cotton wool spots.**
> **THE SYMPTOMS THAT ACCOMPANY IT: headache worse on LYING FLAT, in the MORNING, and with COUGHING or straining · nausea and vomiting · TRANSIENT VISUAL OBSCURATIONS lasting SECONDS on standing or bending · pulsatile tinnitus · and diplopia from a false-localising SIXTH nerve palsy.**
> **ACUITY IS PRESERVED EARLY — with an enlarged blind spot — which is why papilloedema is dangerous: the patient has no visual complaint until the field is already being lost.**
> **CAUSES: intracranial mass · IDIOPATHIC INTRACRANIAL HYPERTENSION (classically a young woman with obesity, and associated with certain drugs including tetracyclines, retinoids and hormonal agents) · cerebral VENOUS SINUS THROMBOSIS · malignant hypertension · meningitis and encephalitis · and hydrocephalus.** Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.3.
> **Distinguish from PSEUDOPAPILLOEDEMA — optic disc drusen or a small crowded hypermetropic disc — which requires imaging of the disc rather than of the brain.**

> [!tip] Nystagmus — peripheral versus central
> **· PERIPHERAL (vestibular): UNIDIRECTIONAL (fast phase always in the same direction) · HORIZONTAL or horizontal-torsional, NEVER purely vertical · SUPPRESSED BY VISUAL FIXATION and enhanced by removing fixation · accompanied by vertigo, nausea and sometimes hearing symptoms.**
> **· CENTRAL: DIRECTION-CHANGING (fast phase changes with gaze direction) · may be PURELY VERTICAL or torsional · NOT suppressed by fixation · with other neurological signs.**
> **DOWNBEAT NYSTAGMUS DESERVES SPECIFIC MENTION: it localises to the CRANIOCERVICAL JUNCTION and cerebellum, and its causes include ARNOLD-CHIARI MALFORMATION, cerebellar degeneration, DRUGS (lithium, anticonvulsants) and WERNICKE ENCEPHALOPATHY.** **It is not a peripheral sign, and it warrants MRI.**
> Cross-refer [[D5]] and [[D3]] 0.5.
> **End-point nystagmus at extremes of gaze is physiological and should not be over-interpreted.**

> [!danger] Charles Bonnet syndrome — explaining it is the treatment
> **FORMED, COMPLEX VISUAL HALLUCINATIONS — patterns, faces, figures, animals, landscapes — occurring in a person with SIGNIFICANT VISION LOSS, with FULLY INTACT INSIGHT (they know the images are not real), and with NO other psychotic features, no auditory hallucinations and no cognitive impairment.**
> **It is a release phenomenon in a visually deprived cortex, and it is BENIGN.**
> **THE CLINICAL POINT IS THIS: PATIENTS DO NOT DISCLOSE IT.** **They are frightened that it means they are developing dementia or a psychiatric illness, and they conceal it — sometimes for years — from family and from clinicians.**
> **So it must be ASKED ABOUT DIRECTLY in anyone with significant visual impairment: "some people who lose vision see things that aren't there — patterns, or people, or animals. Has anything like that happened to you?"**
> **AND THE EXPLANATION IS THE TREATMENT: telling them it is a recognised consequence of vision loss, that it does not mean they are losing their mind, and that it often diminishes over time, produces enormous relief.**
> **Prescribing an antipsychotic for it is the wrong response, and it is what happens when the syndrome is not recognised.** Cross-refer [[N3]] 0.3 and [[D2]] 0.3.
> **Note the contrast: hallucinations in dementia with Lewy bodies occur with fluctuating cognition, parkinsonism and reduced insight — a different picture entirely.**

**Ix:** **ACUITY, COLOUR VISION and PUPILS at every review of THYROID EYE DISEASE** (*why:* compressive optic neuropathy is the sight-threatening complication and it is detected by acuity, red desaturation and an RAPD rather than by worsening proptosis; *what:* reduced acuity, RAPD). **COMPARISON OF PUPIL SIZE IN BRIGHT LIGHT AND IN DARKNESS** (*why:* it identifies which pupil is abnormal and therefore which differential applies; *what:* the light/dark difference). **Old photographs, including the patient's phone or licence** (*why:* establishes whether anisocoria or ptosis is long-standing, and can spare an entire workup; *what:* the baseline). **URGENT VASCULAR IMAGING for a painful Horner syndrome** (*why:* carotid dissection precedes stroke and is treatable; *what:* dissection). **CT or MR ANGIOGRAPHY for a pupil-involving third nerve palsy** (*why:* posterior communicating artery aneurysm; *what:* aneurysm). **CHEST IMAGING for a Horner syndrome with arm symptoms or in a smoker** (*why:* Pancoast tumour; *what:* apical mass). **DILUTE PILOCARPINE for a suspected Adie pupil, and full-strength pilocarpine to exclude pharmacological blockade** (*why:* denervation supersensitivity confirms Adie, and a pharmacologically blocked pupil will not constrict at all; *what:* the response). **BIOPSY of any persistent eyelid lesion with lash loss, ulceration or lid margin distortion, and of any recurrent same-site chalazion** (*why:* basal cell and sebaceous carcinoma; *what:* histology). **RED REFLEX in every newborn and child health check, and urgent referral for leukocoria** (*why:* retinoblastoma and congenital cataract are both sight- and, for one, life-threatening, and treatable early; *what:* absent or abnormal red reflex). **CORNEAL LIGHT REFLEX AND COVER TEST for suspected squint** (*why:* distinguishes true strabismus from pseudostrabismus, and true squint risks permanent amblyopia; *what:* deviation). **FUNDOSCOPY for papilloedema in anyone with headache or transient obscurations** (*why:* acuity is preserved early so there is no visual complaint to prompt it; *what:* disc swelling). **MRI for downbeat or direction-changing nystagmus** (*why:* it localises centrally, to the craniocervical junction; *what:* structural lesion). **DIRECTLY ASKING about visual hallucinations in the visually impaired** (*why:* patients conceal them through fear of being thought psychotic, and explanation is the treatment; *what:* Charles Bonnet syndrome).

### Horner's Syndrome

**D:** a clinical triad resulting from disruption of the sympathetic nerve supply to the eye/face, localising to one of three neuron levels along the sympathetic pathway.

**S/Smx (the classic triad, all ipsilateral to the lesion):** partial ptosis (drooping of the upper eyelid — partial, not complete, since sympathetic fibres only supply the accessory levator muscle, unlike the more complete ptosis of a CN III palsy); miosis (constricted pupil, from unopposed parasympathetic activity); anhidrosis (reduced sweating — present or absent depending on which of the three neurons is affected, since sweat fibres to the face branch off at different points along the pathway).

> [!info] Localising by associated features (three-neuron pathway)
> **1st-order neuron** (hypothalamus → brainstem → spinal cord C8–T2): associated with brainstem/spinal cord pathology (e.g. lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome — see the Arterial Territory Syndromes table above) — anhidrosis affects the whole ipsilateral body/face.
> **2nd-order neuron** (spinal cord → sympathetic chain → superior cervical ganglion, passing near the lung apex and subclavian vessels): associated with **Pancoast tumour** (apical lung cancer — a critical diagnosis not to miss in a smoker presenting with Horner's), brachial plexus trauma, thyroid/neck surgery, central line insertion — anhidrosis affects the face only.
> **3rd-order neuron** (superior cervical ganglion → along the internal carotid artery → eye): associated with carotid artery dissection (a critical diagnosis in a younger patient with sudden-onset Horner's ± neck pain — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for carotid-related content), cluster headache (see above), cavernous sinus pathology — anhidrosis is typically absent, since sweat fibres to the face have already branched off before this point.

**Ix:** the priority is identifying the underlying cause given how variable (and sometimes life-threatening) it can be — CXR/CT chest (*why:* screens for Pancoast tumour, a critical and treatable-if-caught-early cause; *what:* apical lung mass). CT/MR angiogram of the neck (*why:* screens for carotid dissection, particularly urgent in a younger patient with acute-onset Horner's and neck pain, given the stroke risk from dissection; *what:* may show an intimal flap or narrowed true lumen). Apraclonidine or cocaine eye drops (*why:* a pharmacological test that can confirm the diagnosis and distinguish Horner's from other causes of anisocoria when the clinical picture is ambiguous; *what:* pupillary response pattern differs between the Horner's-affected and normal eye) — a specialist-ordered test, not routine first-line.

**Mx:** entirely dependent on the underlying cause identified above — Horner's syndrome itself is a sign, not a disease, so Mx targets the causative pathology (e.g. oncological management for Pancoast tumour, urgent anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy and vascular referral for carotid dissection).

## Acute Visual Loss

- **D:** loss of vision developing over seconds to days. The first three questions do almost all the diagnostic work: **is it painful or painless? one eye or both? and is the loss transient or persistent?**
- **DDx:**
  - **Sudden, painless, monocular — the classic emergency group:**
    - **Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)** — sudden profound loss, a pale retina with a **cherry-red spot** at the macula, and a **relative afferent pupillary defect**. Treated as a stroke of the eye: it requires urgent ophthalmology involvement *and* urgent stroke work-up, because it carries the same embolic risk as a cerebral TIA.
    - **Giant cell arteritis with arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy** — **the diagnosis that must not be missed**, because untreated it blinds the other eye. Suspect in anyone over 50 with visual loss plus headache, scalp tenderness, **jaw claudication** (the most specific symptom), temporal artery tenderness, polymyalgic symptoms, fever or weight loss.
    - **Central retinal vein occlusion** — less abrupt, "blood and thunder" fundus with widespread haemorrhages.
    - **Retinal detachment** — preceded by **flashes and floaters**, then a curtain or shadow advancing across the field.
    - **Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters and haze, commonly in proliferative diabetic retinopathy.
    - **Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy**; **occipital (cortical) stroke** — which causes a **homonymous field defect** and is often described by the patient as loss in one eye, so **always test the fields**.
  - **Sudden, painful:**
    - **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — painful red eye, haloes around lights, nausea and vomiting, a hazy cornea and a fixed mid-dilated pupil; the eye feels hard.
    - **Optic neuritis** — pain on eye movement, reduced colour vision (**red desaturation**), a relative afferent pupillary defect, typically in a younger patient; associated with multiple sclerosis.
    - **Keratitis or corneal ulcer** (especially in contact lens wearers), **anterior uveitis**, **endophthalmitis** after intraocular surgery or injection, **chemical injury** or trauma.
  - **Transient:** **amaurosis fugax** (embolic — a TIA of the eye requiring the same urgency and work-up as any TIA), migraine with aura, papilloedema with transient visual obscurations, hypoperfusion.
  - **Gradual but noticed suddenly:** cataract, macular degeneration, chronic glaucoma, diabetic maculopathy, compressive lesion — commonly "discovered" when the good eye is covered.
- **Focused Hx:** **exact time and speed of onset**; **one eye or both — and whether the patient has actually covered each eye to check**; painful or painless; **pain on eye movement**; **flashes, floaters, or a curtain/shadow**; haloes around lights; **transient episodes preceding this one**; **headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, shoulder and hip girdle stiffness, fever, weight loss** — the GCA screen, asked in every patient over 50; **contact lens wear**; trauma or chemical exposure; recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection; **diabetes and its control, hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, smoking, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or TIA, known vasculitis**; medications including anticoagulants and **amiodarone, ethambutol and hydroxychloroquine**; neurological symptoms; and previous ophthalmic history.
- **Examination:**
  - **Visual acuity in each eye separately, with the patient's glasses or through a pinhole** — this is the vital sign of the eye and must be recorded, not estimated.
  - **Pupils, specifically testing for a relative afferent pupillary defect with the swinging-flashlight test** — an RAPD indicates optic nerve or extensive retinal disease and is the single most informative bedside sign here.
  - **Visual fields to confrontation in each eye** — to identify a homonymous defect indicating a retrochiasmal (cerebral) cause.
  - **Colour vision** (red desaturation) where optic neuritis is suspected.
  - **Eye movements** and whether they provoke pain.
  - **External examination** — redness and its pattern (ciliary flush), corneal clarity, discharge, proptosis, lid position.
  - **Fundoscopy**, dilated where safe and appropriate — **but do not dilate if angle-closure glaucoma is suspected**.
  - **Palpate the globe** for hardness and **palpate the temporal arteries** for tenderness, thickening or absent pulsation.
  - **Cardiovascular examination** — pulse for atrial fibrillation, blood pressure, carotid bruits.
  - **Fluorescein staining** where a corneal cause is possible; **intraocular pressure measurement** where glaucoma is suspected.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **ESR and CRP urgently, plus FBC and platelets**, in any patient over 50 with visual loss — **the GCA screen. Inflammatory markers can be normal in biopsy-proven GCA, so a normal result does not exclude it**, and treatment should not be withheld on that basis alone.
  - **Glucose/HbA1c, lipids, UEC, coagulation profile**; **ECG** for atrial fibrillation.
  - **Temporal artery ultrasound and/or temporal artery biopsy** where GCA is suspected — arranged urgently, **but never allowed to delay treatment**.
  - **Urgent CT or MRI brain** where a cortical cause, a compressive lesion or optic neuritis is suspected; **MRI with contrast** for optic neuritis and demyelination.
  - **Carotid duplex and echocardiography** for embolic causes, as for a TIA.
  - **Intraocular pressure**; **B-scan ultrasound** where the fundal view is obscured by vitreous haemorrhage.
  - **Same-day ophthalmology review** is itself the key "investigation" in most of this differential — formal slit-lamp examination, dilated fundoscopy and optical coherence tomography are not intern-level procedures, and the correct action is referral with an accurate acuity, pupil and field assessment already documented.

> [!danger] **Two things that cause avoidable permanent blindness in this presentation.** First, **not asking about jaw claudication and scalp tenderness in an older patient**, and not starting treatment while awaiting biopsy — in suspected GCA, **high-dose corticosteroid is started immediately on clinical suspicion, before biopsy, because the fellow eye is at risk within days**. *(The specific corticosteroid dose and route are deliberately omitted — they differ between guidelines and the Australian sources are egress-blocked. Escalate immediately; do not delay the discussion while looking up a dose.)* Second, **failing to check visual acuity and pupils** and referring an eye without them, which removes the information the ophthalmologist needs to triage.

## Acute Visual Disturbance

> Distinct from **Acute Visual Loss** (Tier 1, above): this entry covers **distorted, double, obscured or transiently altered vision without necessarily losing it**, where the differential is broader and includes several neurological causes.

- **DDx:**
  - **Binocular diplopia (disappears when either eye is covered) — a cranial nerve or brainstem problem until proven otherwise:** **third nerve palsy — and a painful third nerve palsy with a dilated pupil is a posterior communicating artery aneurysm until excluded**; fourth and sixth nerve palsies (sixth is a false localising sign in raised intracranial pressure); **myasthenia gravis** (fatigable, variable, with ptosis); **thyroid eye disease**; orbital mass or fracture with muscle entrapment; **brainstem stroke**; multiple sclerosis; **Wernicke's encephalopathy**; cavernous sinus pathology.
  - **Monocular diplopia (persists with the other eye covered)** — almost always **ocular**: cataract, refractive error, corneal irregularity, dislocated lens, iris defect. Reassuring in that it is not neurological.
  - **Distortion and positive phenomena:** **metamorphopsia** (straight lines bent — macular pathology, especially **wet age-related macular degeneration**, which is time-critical for treatment), **photopsia** (flashes — vitreous traction, retinal tear or detachment), **floaters**, **scintillating scotoma with a marching edge** (migraine aura, typically evolving over 20–30 minutes and resolving), haloes (**angle closure or corneal oedema**).
  - **Field disturbance:** **homonymous hemianopia** (retrochiasmal — stroke or tumour), bitemporal hemianopia (**chiasmal compression — pituitary lesion**), altitudinal defect (ischaemic optic neuropathy or retinal vascular occlusion), arcuate defects (glaucoma).
  - **Transient:** **amaurosis fugax** (embolic — treat as a TIA), transient visual obscurations with papilloedema, migraine, hypoperfusion, **hypoglycaemia**.
  - **Toxic and metabolic:** **ethambutol, hydroxychloroquine, amiodarone, digoxin (xanthopsia), methanol**, vitamin A deficiency, hyperglycaemia causing refractive shift.
- **Focused Hx:** **one eye or both — and confirmed by the patient covering each eye**, because this single answer splits the differential; **constant or intermittent, and duration of episodes**; **diplopia: horizontal, vertical or oblique, and worse in which direction of gaze**; **fatigability and diurnal variation** (myasthenia); pain, especially **on eye movement**; **headache**; positive phenomena (flashes, floaters, zigzags) and their evolution over minutes; field loss and its shape; associated neurological symptoms; **vascular risk factors and AF**; medications; diabetes and recent glycaemic swings; alcohol and nutrition.
- **Examination:** **acuity each eye, pupils with the swinging-flashlight test, and visual fields to confrontation** — the same three that anchor every ophthalmic assessment; **colour vision**; **full eye movements in all directions, looking for the pattern of restriction, and for ptosis and fatigability on sustained upgaze**; **cover/uncover and alternate cover testing** where strabismus or skew is possible; **fundoscopy for papilloedema, macular changes and retinal vascular signs**; **cranial nerve examination**; blood pressure; and **an Amsler grid** where metamorphopsia is described.
- **First-line Ix:** **capillary glucose**; **FBC, UEC, ESR and CRP** (GCA in the over-50s); **HbA1c and lipids**; **ECG** for AF; **urgent CT or CT angiography** for a painful third nerve palsy with pupil involvement, or for suspected stroke; **MRI brain and orbits** for suspected demyelination, chiasmal compression or brainstem lesion; **acetylcholine receptor antibodies and thyroid function** where myasthenia or thyroid eye disease is suspected; **carotid duplex and echocardiography** for embolic phenomena; **urgent ophthalmology and optical coherence tomography** where macular pathology is suspected.

## Acute Floaters

- **D:** the sudden appearance or increase of drifting shapes in the visual field. **Chronic, stable floaters are common and benign; a sudden change is the presentation that matters**, because it can signal a retinal tear before detachment occurs — the point at which treatment is simple and vision is preserved.
- **DDx:**
  - **Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)** — the commonest cause, and usually benign; **but it is also the event that causes retinal tears**, so a first presentation needs examination rather than reassurance.
  - **Retinal tear and retinal detachment** — **the sight-threatening diagnosis**; suggested by **a sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light (photopsia), and a curtain, shadow or veil advancing across the field**, and by loss of central vision once the macula is involved.
  - **Vitreous haemorrhage** — sudden floaters, cobwebs or a red haze, sometimes with dense visual loss; commonly from **proliferative diabetic retinopathy**, retinal tear, or retinal vein occlusion.
  - **Posterior uveitis or vitritis** — floaters with pain, photophobia or systemic inflammatory disease; consider infection in the immunosuppressed (**toxoplasmosis, CMV retinitis**).
  - **Trauma**; **intraocular lymphoma** (rare, in older patients with persistent vitritis).
- **Focused Hx:** **onset — sudden or gradual**; **number and pattern: a few new floaters versus a sudden shower or "soot"**; **flashes of light, and whether they occur in the dark or on eye movement**; **any curtain, shadow, or field loss, and from which direction it advanced**; **central vision** (macular involvement changes urgency and prognosis); pain and photophobia; **myopia — a significant risk factor for retinal detachment**; previous retinal detachment in either eye or in family; **cataract or other intraocular surgery**; **diabetes and its retinopathy status**; trauma; anticoagulation; immunosuppression.
- **Examination:** **visual acuity each eye**; **visual fields to confrontation — a field defect corresponding to the reported curtain supports detachment**; **pupils and RAPD** (an RAPD suggests a large detachment or other significant retinal or optic nerve pathology); **red reflex** — reduced or absent in vitreous haemorrhage; **dilated fundoscopy** where the skill and equipment allow, looking for the detached retina (grey, mobile, with folds), pigment or haemorrhage in the vitreous, and retinal tears — recognising that **peripheral tears are frequently not visible on direct ophthalmoscopy, so a normal view does not exclude one**; assessment for signs of uveitis.
- **First-line Ix:** **the investigation is a dilated slit-lamp examination with indirect ophthalmoscopy and scleral indentation by an ophthalmologist** — this is a referral, not a test an intern performs. **B-scan ultrasound** where the fundal view is obscured by vitreous haemorrhage or dense cataract. **Glucose/HbA1c** where diabetic retinopathy is the likely source; **FBC, CRP and inflammatory or infective serology** where uveitis is suspected.

> [!danger] **The triage rule.** **New floaters with flashes, or with any field loss, curtain or shadow, need same-day ophthalmology assessment** — a retinal tear treated before detachment is a straightforward laser procedure, whereas a macula-off detachment often leaves permanent visual loss even after successful surgery. **New floaters without flashes or field loss** still need review, but the timeframe is set by local pathways. **Do not reassure a patient with new floaters on the basis of normal acuity** — acuity is preserved until the macula detaches, which is exactly the window in which intervention works.

## Acute Eye Pain

- **DDx — separate by whether the eye is red, and whether vision is affected:**
  - **Painful red eye with reduced vision — the sight-threatening group:** **acute angle-closure glaucoma** (severe pain, haloes, nausea and vomiting, hazy cornea, fixed mid-dilated pupil, hard globe), **keratitis and corneal ulcer** (especially **contact lens wearers — and any contact lens wearer with a red painful eye needs same-day ophthalmology review**), **anterior uveitis** (photophobia, ciliary flush, small irregular pupil, often with a systemic association such as ankylosing spondylitis or inflammatory bowel disease), **endophthalmitis** (after intraocular surgery or injection — an emergency), **scleritis** (severe boring pain that wakes the patient, often with connective tissue disease), **chemical injury** (**the one condition where treatment — copious irrigation — precedes assessment entirely**), **penetrating trauma**.
  - **Painful red eye with normal vision:** conjunctivitis (usually gritty rather than truly painful), episcleritis, subconjunctival haemorrhage (painless), blepharitis, foreign body, corneal abrasion, dry eye, **herpes zoster ophthalmicus** (dermatomal rash; **involvement of the nose tip — Hutchinson's sign — predicts ocular involvement**).
  - **Pain with a white eye:** **optic neuritis** (pain on eye movement), **giant cell arteritis**, migraine, cluster headache, sinusitis, trigeminal neuralgia, raised intracranial pressure, **orbital cellulitis** (pain on eye movement with proptosis, lid swelling, fever, and restricted movements — distinguishing it from preseptal cellulitis is the key clinical task, and it is an admission).
- **Focused Hx:** onset and severity; **vision change**; photophobia; discharge and its character; **contact lens wear, including sleeping in lenses and water exposure**; **trauma and chemical exposure, with the substance identified**; **recent eye surgery or intravitreal injection**; haloes; headache, nausea and vomiting; rash; systemic symptoms and known inflammatory or autoimmune disease; **jaw claudication and scalp tenderness** in the over-50s; previous episodes.
- **Examination:** **visual acuity each eye separately — first, and always**; pupils and **RAPD**; **pattern of redness** (diffuse conjunctival versus **ciliary flush** around the limbus, which points to intraocular inflammation); corneal clarity; **fluorescein staining under blue light** for abrasions, ulcers and **the dendritic ulcer of herpes simplex keratitis**; eye movements and whether they hurt; **proptosis and lid assessment**; globe tension by gentle palpation; **facial and scalp inspection for a zoster rash including the nose tip**; temporal artery palpation; and fundoscopy where safe.
- **First-line Ix:** largely **clinical and by slit lamp**. **Intraocular pressure measurement** where angle closure is suspected; **corneal scrape for microscopy and culture** in suspected microbial keratitis (an ophthalmology procedure); **ESR, CRP and FBC** in the over-50s where GCA is possible; **CT orbits** for suspected orbital cellulitis, orbital fracture or intraocular foreign body; **FBC, CRP, blood cultures** in orbital cellulitis; **HLA-B27, autoimmune screen and chest imaging** where recurrent uveitis prompts a systemic search — arranged, not urgent.

> [!danger] **Two actions that precede everything else.** **(1) Chemical injury: irrigate immediately, copiously and for a prolonged period, and check pH — do not delay to take a history or measure acuity.** Alkali injuries penetrate and are worse than acid. **(2) Do not dilate the pupil if acute angle-closure glaucoma is a possibility**, and do not pad an eye that may have an infected corneal ulcer. Contact-lens-related red eye is assumed to be microbial keratitis until an ophthalmologist says otherwise.
