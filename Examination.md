## 1.1 ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient)

`CF-PAIR` **2 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 The A–E Approach` · `Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)` — from 04_Neurology.md · F0-4_Resuscitation_Algorithms_and_Emergency_Procedures.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — arguably the single most fundamental systematic approach in acute clinical medicine, without a dedicated entry despite the extensive emergency content built throughout this entire project that assumes this framework as background (sepsis, anaphylaxis, DKA, status epilepticus, necrotising fasciitis, tumour lysis syndrome, neutropenic sepsis, and every other "danger"-flagged entry across all system files). This entry covers the systematic primary-survey approach; cross-references the specific emergency disease sections for detailed management rather than repeating it.

**Core principle:** ABCDE is a systematic, sequential approach used to assess and stabilise any acutely unwell or deteriorating patient — **treat problems as you find them at each step before moving to the next**, rather than completing a full assessment first and treating afterward; a life-threatening airway problem is addressed immediately, not noted and returned to after checking circulation. Reassess after any intervention and periodically throughout, since the patient's status can change rapidly. Call for help early and escalate proportionately to the severity found — this is a team-based approach in real practice, not something to manage alone if the patient is significantly unwell.

**A — Airway**
- **Assess:** can the patient talk normally? A patient speaking in full sentences generally has a patent airway; look, listen, and feel for airway obstruction (stridor, gurgling, snoring, see-saw chest/abdominal movement, absent breath sounds).
- **Red flags:** stridor, inability to speak, silent chest with respiratory effort (suggests complete obstruction — more concerning than a noisy partial obstruction).
- **Immediate actions:** simple airway manoeuvres (head tilt–chin lift, or jaw thrust if cervical spine injury is a concern), suction if secretions/vomit are visible, airway adjuncts (oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway) if needed, call for anaesthetic/senior help early for any airway compromise not immediately resolved by simple measures — see [[03a_Anaesthetics_Primer]] for further airway management detail, not repeated here.

**B — Breathing**
- **Assess:** respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, work of breathing (accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, recession), chest auscultation and percussion, symmetry of chest movement, tracheal position.
- **Red flags:** respiratory rate <8 or >30, oxygen saturation not improving with oxygen, silent chest, tracheal deviation (tension pneumothorax — a genuine "treat on clinical suspicion, don't wait for imaging" emergency), unequal air entry.
- **Immediate actions:** high-flow oxygen if hypoxic or critically unwell (per current resuscitation-context oxygen targets, distinct from the more conservative titrated-oxygen approach used in stable settings — see [[02_Respiratory]] for the general oxygen therapy principles, not repeated here); needle decompression/chest drain for suspected tension pneumothorax; nebulised bronchodilators for wheeze/bronchospasm — see [[02_Respiratory]] for the specific disease-level Mx (asthma, COPD exacerbation, pneumothorax), not repeated here.

**C — Circulation**
- **Assess:** heart rate, blood pressure, capillary refill time, skin colour/temperature, urine output if available, active bleeding sites, heart sounds/murmurs, JVP.
- **Red flags:** hypotension, tachycardia not explained by pain/anxiety alone, prolonged capillary refill, cool/mottled peripheries, active uncontrolled bleeding, muffled heart sounds with hypotension and raised JVP (Beck's triad — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.34 Cardiac Tamponade).
- **Immediate actions:** IV access (typically two large-bore cannulae in a significantly unwell patient), bloods including group & save/crossmatch if bleeding or a transfusion may be needed, IV fluid resuscitation, control of any visible external bleeding (direct pressure), blood transfusion if indicated (see [[10_08_Haemonc_-_Blood_Products_and_Transfusion]] Packed red cells for the specific transfusion thresholds, not repeated here) — see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Sepsis for the specific fluid/antibiotic bundle in suspected sepsis, and [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.20 Shock for the broader shock differential and management this feeds into, not repeated here.

**D — Disability**
- **Assess:** conscious level (AVPU — Alert, responds to Voice, responds to Pain, Unresponsive — or full GCS if more detail is needed), pupils (size, symmetry, reactivity), blood glucose (**check in every acutely unwell patient, given hypoglycaemia is a rapidly reversible cause of altered consciousness that must never be missed**), focal neurological deficit, pain score.
- **Red flags:** GCS <8 (classically prompts consideration of airway protection, given the risk of losing airway reflexes), new focal neurological deficit, unequal or non-reactive pupils, blood glucose critically low or high.
- **Immediate actions:** treat hypoglycaemia immediately if found (IV dextrose or IM/oral glucose depending on severity and consciousness level); consider and treat opioid toxicity if suspected (naloxone) given this is a rapidly reversible cause of reduced consciousness; further neurological assessment and imaging as guided by findings — see [[04_Neurology]] for the specific disease-level content (stroke, raised ICP, seizures) this feeds into, not repeated here.

**E — Exposure**
- **Assess:** fully expose the patient (with dignity/privacy maintained wherever possible) to look for rashes, wounds, bleeding, swelling, medical alert jewellery, injection sites, or other clues not visible with the patient clothed; **always check the patient's temperature** as part of this step, given fever or hypothermia are both major clues to the underlying process and are easy to omit if exposure is rushed.
- **Red flags:** non-blanching rash (see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes — approach and differential for the full differential and urgency principle), signs of necrotising fasciitis (pain out of proportion — see [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Necrotising fasciitis), occult bleeding/wounds, hypothermia or high fever.
- **Immediate actions:** treat the underlying cause once identified via exposure; maintain normothermia (warming for hypothermia, antipyretics/cooling as appropriate for fever) as part of general supportive care; avoid prolonged unnecessary exposure once the assessment is complete, given the risk of iatrogenic hypothermia in a critically unwell patient.

**After the primary survey:** take a focused history (from the patient, family, or collateral sources — the **AMPLE** mnemonic is commonly used: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events leading to presentation) once the immediate ABCDE assessment and any life-threatening problems have been addressed; reassess the full ABCDE sequence periodically and after any intervention, given a patient's status can change rapidly and a single assessment is a snapshot, not a guarantee of ongoing stability.

**Cross-reference:** this framework underlies essentially every emergency/danger-flagged entry throughout this project — see in particular [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Sepsis, [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.20 Shock and 0.33 Cardiac Tamponade, [[10_10a_Haemonc_-_Haematological_and_Oncological_Emergencies]], and [[04_Neurology]] for disease-specific emergencies this general approach applies to.

## 1.2 Vital Signs and Early Warning Scores (Recognising the Deteriorating Patient)

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 The Deteriorating Patient — Recognition` — from A1_Emergency_-_Deteriorating_Patient__Sepsis__Cardiac_Arrest.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — the systematic vital-signs-based escalation framework that underlies "call for help early and escalate proportionately" in the ABCDE entry above, without ever being made explicit — a genuinely foundational, constantly-used ward skill.

**The general principle:** rather than relying on subjective clinical impression alone, structured vital-sign-based early warning tools are used to standardise recognition of, and escalation for, a deteriorating patient — trending observations over time is often more informative than any single reading, given a patient moving in a concerning direction (even within an individually "normal" range) can be an earlier warning sign than waiting for a single reading to cross an abnormal threshold.

> [!info] Verified — **Australia does not use a single nationwide early warning score**, and specifically does **not** use the UK's NEWS2 as its default. **"Between the Flags" (BTF)**, developed by the NSW Clinical Excellence Commission and implemented across NSW public hospitals, is the most well-known Australian-originated system and is structurally different from NEWS2: rather than a weighted points-based aggregate score, BTF is a **single-parameter-trigger, two-tier system** — if *any single* vital sign (respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, conscious level) falls into a locally-defined **"yellow zone,"** it triggers a clinical review; falling into the **"red zone"** triggers a Rapid Response/Medical Emergency Team (MET) call — the name itself is a deliberate reference to Australian surf lifesaving (patients needing extra attention are "between the flags," just as beachgoers are directed to swim between the safety flags). Other Australian states/services use different tools — for example, **Queensland uses Q-ADDS** (Queensland Adult Deterioration Detection System), which is structurally closer to an aggregate scoring system like NEWS2/MEWS. **Check the specific hospital/health service's own observation chart and calling criteria** rather than assuming a single national standard, given this genuinely varies by state and even by facility.
> The specific numeric thresholds for yellow/red zones (or point thresholds for aggregate systems) are locally defined on each service's own observation chart rather than a single fixed Australia-wide number — not reproduced here for that reason; the concept (structured, vital-sign-triggered escalation with a graded response) is the transferable, high-yield principle regardless of which specific tool a given hospital uses.

**Core vital signs monitored by essentially all these systems:** respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (± supplemental oxygen requirement), heart rate, systolic blood pressure, temperature, conscious level (AVPU or GCS) — the same core parameters assessed in the ABCDE approach above, reflecting that early warning scores are essentially a structured, trended, escalation-triggering formalisation of the same B/C/D assessment.

**Response tiers (the general shared structure across BTF/Q-ADDS/NEWS2-style systems, though specific names/thresholds vary):**
- **Low-level trigger ("yellow zone" equivalent):** increased frequency of observations ± clinical review by the treating team, given this represents an early, potentially reversible warning sign.
- **High-level trigger ("red zone" equivalent):** urgent senior/Rapid Response Team or Medical Emergency Team activation — a genuinely different, more urgent response than a routine clinical review, reflecting a patient at significantly higher risk of imminent serious deterioration.
- **Any individual staff member's concern can trigger an escalation** regardless of the numeric score/zone in most of these systems — "worried" or "concerned" is itself a valid, deliberately-included escalation trigger in most Australian systems, recognising that structured tools don't capture every clinically relevant warning sign, and staff should never feel that a normal score overrides a genuine clinical concern.
- **Resuscitation status/end-of-life wishes can modify the response** — the calling criteria and response can be adjusted for a patient with a documented DNACPR or comfort-focused goals of care (see [[Communication]] Discussing "Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation" (DNACPR / Not-for-Resuscitation)), so that a deteriorating but not-for-resuscitation patient receives an appropriately adjusted (not necessarily full escalation) response consistent with their documented wishes.

**Cross-reference:** see the ABCDE Assessment entry above for the disease-level framework this vital-sign monitoring feeds into, and [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Sepsis for qSOFA as a condition-specific (rather than general ward-deterioration) early warning tool, not repeated here.

## 1.3 Observers in Clinical Examination

> [!note] Gap-filled — the term appeared **eight times across this file** as a procedural instruction inside examination sequences ("offer a observer"), with **no entry explaining what an observer is for, who can be one, or what happens if the patient declines**. Referenced enough to look covered; never taught. Verified against the **Medical Board of Australia's *Sexual boundaries in the doctor–patient relationship* guidelines, section 7.1 "Use of observers"**, Aug 2026. See also [[Communication]] Professional Boundaries, and the Inappropriate Patient for the wider boundaries framework, not repeated here.

> [!info] **Terminology:** the Medical Board's guidelines use **"observer"**. "Chaperone" is the older term — still widely understood, but superseded.

**An observer serves two purposes, and the first is routinely forgotten.** They are a **witness to the consultation**, able to give an account if improper behaviour is later alleged — protecting the doctor as well as the patient — **and** they may **provide comfort and reassurance** to a patient who is fearful, vulnerable or embarrassed. Offering one "for the patient's benefit" describes only half of why it exists.

**Who can be an observer:** someone **qualified for the role** — a registered or enrolled nurse, another registered health practitioner, or a person **appropriately trained so they understand what the support role involves**. They must be **acceptable to the patient**, and must **respect the patient's privacy, confidentiality and dignity**. A family member is not a substitute for a qualified observer.

> [!warning] **The patient may decline an observer, and this leaves the doctor with a genuine choice rather than an obligation.** Where the offer is declined, the doctor may **proceed without one**, or may **decline to proceed and assist the patient to find another doctor**. Both are legitimate; neither is abandonment, provided the patient is actually helped to access care. Document the offer and the response either way.

> [!danger] **An observer and a support person are two different rights, and conflating them is the common error.** Separately from any observer the practice provides, **the patient may ask to be accompanied by a support person of their own choosing** — a partner, relative or friend. That is the patient's entitlement and is not a substitute for a qualified observer, nor does providing one discharge the offer of the other. A patient may reasonably want both, or either, or neither.

> [!info] **"Intimate examination" is defined from the patient's perspective, not by a fixed anatomical list.** It usually means examination of the breasts, genitalia or an internal examination — but what a given patient experiences as intimate **may be shaped by their cultural values and beliefs**, and may extend beyond that list. The practical consequence: **ask and offer rather than assume**, and treat a patient's discomfort with an examination you consider routine as informative rather than as an overreaction. This is the same principle applied elsewhere in this project to culturally safe assessment.

**In practice, for any examination in this file marked "offer an observer":** make the offer explicitly rather than assuming, record the offer and the response, and record the observer's **name and title** where one is present. An undocumented offer protects nobody.

## 1.4 GALS Screen (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine)

> [!note] Gap-filled — the classic, standardised rapid musculoskeletal screening examination, without a dedicated entry despite the extensive Orthopaedics and Rheumatology disease content built across [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]] through [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] that this screen is designed to trigger further, joint-specific examination for.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (a brief screen of the joints, muscles, and spine — asking a few questions, then watching you move and briefly checking your joints), gain consent, expose appropriately (ideally down to underwear/shorts) with privacy maintained, position the patient standing initially.

**The three screening questions — ask before the physical assessment, given they focus and direct the rest of the exam:**
1. "Do you have any pain or stiffness in your muscles, joints, or back?" — screens for the presence of any MSK symptom at all, informing how much attention to pay to specific findings below.
2. "Can you dress yourself completely without any difficulty?" — screens for fine motor impairment and functionally significant restriction of joint range of movement (getting dressed requires a wide range of shoulder, elbow, and hand movements).
3. "Can you walk up and down stairs without difficulty?" — screens for impaired gross motor function and lower limb mobility/strength.

**Systematic approach — Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine (order can vary; often done as Gait → Spine → Arms → Legs in practice, given patient positioning efficiency, though the mnemonic itself lists Gait-Arms-Legs-Spine):**

1. **Gait:** observe the patient walking a short distance, turning, and walking back — assess symmetry, stride length, arm swing, ability to turn smoothly, and any limp (antalgic gait suggests a painful lower limb joint) or other abnormal pattern.
2. **Spine:** inspect from behind (symmetry, scoliosis — see [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] Scoliosis) and from the side (normal curvature, or abnormal kyphosis — see [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] Scheuermann's kyphosis (juvenile kyphosis)); ask the patient to touch their toes (screens lumbar flexion — a reduced Schober's test-type restriction here is relevant to [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]] Ankylosing spondylitis, not repeated here); ask the patient to tilt their ear to each shoulder (cervical lateral flexion); briefly palpate the spine and supraspinatus muscles for tenderness.
3. **Arms:** "put your hands behind your head" (screens shoulder abduction/external rotation and elbow flexion together — a single efficient movement covering multiple joints, relevant to [[11_02_Ortho_-_Upper_Limb__Shoulder__Elbow__Distal_Radius_Fractures_]] Rotator cuff injury and Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)); arms straight out in front, then palms down (inspect for synovitis/swelling — symmetrical small-joint swelling here raises RA, see [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)), then palms up (screens forearm supination); make a fist then check grip strength; precision grip (thumb-to-fingertip, screens fine motor/hand function); gently squeeze across the MCP joints (a positive "squeeze test" — pain here is a sensitive screen for inflammatory synovitis, given MCP joints are commonly affected early in RA).
4. **Legs:** with the patient lying supine — passive knee flexion and extension (feeling for crepitus, which raises osteoarthritis — see [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] Osteoarthritis (OA)); passive internal rotation of the hip (a sensitive screen for hip pathology, given internal rotation is often the first movement lost — relevant to [[11_04_Ortho_-_Hip]] and paediatric hip conditions in [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]]); patellar tap/bulge test (screens for a knee effusion); gently squeeze across the metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joints (the lower-limb equivalent of the MCP squeeze test above, screening for forefoot synovitis).

**Recording the result:** a fully normal GALS screen is conventionally recorded simply as "GALS: NAD" (no abnormality detected) — both positive and negative findings should be documented, not just abnormalities, given a normal screen is itself clinically informative.

**Key principle — GALS is a screen, not a diagnostic exam:** if any abnormality is found in gait, appearance, or movement of any region, **proceed to a full regional examination of the specific joint(s) involved** (examining the joint above and below the affected joint too) rather than trying to reach a diagnosis from the GALS screen alone — GALS tells you *that* there's a problem requiring further assessment, not *what* the problem is.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each of the three screening questions and each of the four regions as you assess them, explicitly stating normal vs abnormal findings for each rather than moving through silently.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** report the three screening question answers, then findings by region (Gait, Spine, Arms, Legs), concluding with either "GALS: NAD" or a description of the specific abnormality found and which regional examination you would proceed to as a result.

**Cross-reference:** see [[History-Taking]] Joint Pain (Generalised and Local) for the history-taking approach this examination pairs with, and the individual Orthopaedics/Rheumatology disease files cross-referenced throughout above for the specific differentials each finding feeds into.

## 1.5 Cardiovascular Examination

`CF-PAIR` **3 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `Dynamic manoeuvres` · `0.21.2 Heart sounds` · `0.21.4 Pulses` — from 01_Cardiovascular.md · B5_Murmurs__Endocarditis_and_Vascular_Disease.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

**Opening:**
- Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm patient's name and DOB, explain what the examination involves, gain consent
- Expose the patient's chest appropriately (maintaining dignity — offer an observer, see Observers in Clinical Examination above), position at 45°
- General inspection of the patient and surroundings — say aloud: "I'm looking around the bed for any relevant equipment (GTN spray, O2, medication charts) and assessing the patient's general state — comfortable at rest, any visible distress, cyanosis, or breathlessness"

**Step-by-step sequence:**

1. **Hands:** temperature, capillary refill (<2s), clubbing, splinter haemorrhages, Janeway lesions/Osler's nodes (endocarditis), tar staining (smoking), peripheral cyanosis, tremor. Say aloud: "I'm checking the hands for signs of endocarditis and peripheral perfusion."
2. **Pulse:** rate, rhythm, character (at the radial, then assess collapsing pulse at the brachial with arm raised) — say aloud what you're assessing for.
3. **Blood pressure:** state you would measure this, and offer to check both arms (radiofemoral delay, coarctation) if relevant to the clinical context.
4. **Face:** eyes (xanthelasma, corneal arcus, conjunctival pallor), mouth (central cyanosis, dentition as an endocarditis source).
5. **Neck:** JVP — position at 45°, look for the height and character of the JVP waveform (measure vertically from the sternal angle); say aloud what you're looking for (elevated JVP, waveform abnormalities e.g. cannon waves, Kussmaul's sign).
6. **Chest inspection:** scars (sternotomy, thoracotomy — prior CABG/valve surgery), visible pulsations, pacemaker/ICD box, chest wall deformity.
7. **Palpation:** apex beat (location, character — displaced/heaving/tapping), heaves and thrills (say aloud where and what you're feeling for at each valve area).
8. **Auscultation:** all 4 valve areas (aortic — 2nd right ICS; pulmonary — 2nd left ICS; tricuspid — 4th/5th left ICS; mitral — 5th left ICS midclavicular line) with the diaphragm, then the mitral area and left lateral position with the bell (mitral stenosis) and the left sternal edge sitting forward on expiration (aortic regurgitation). State you would auscultate the carotids for radiation/bruits.
9. **Lung bases:** auscultate for crackles (pulmonary oedema).
10. **Peripheral oedema:** sacral and ankle/pretibial oedema (pitting).
11. **Peripheral pulses:** if time/context permits — femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis (see PAD/ABPI below).

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** practice a structured summary — "This is [patient], a [age]-year-old [sex] who on examination has [key positive findings], with [pulse/BP], [JVP], [heart sounds/murmurs], [additional relevant findings]. This is consistent with [differential]. I would like to complete my examination by measuring BP in both arms/examining the abdomen for hepatomegaly/checking for peripheral oedema." — a distinct, practised skill separate from performing the exam itself.

**Cross-reference:** see [[01_Cardiovascular]] section 0.21 for murmur interpretation, heart sounds, and pulse character differentials — this template covers the *how to examine*, not the *what it means*.

## 1.6 Peripheral Vascular / ABPI Assessment

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain, consent, expose both lower limbs, general inspection (colour, hair loss, ulceration, gangrene, scars from previous vascular surgery).

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Inspect both legs side by side for colour, trophic changes, ulcers (note location — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] 0.36.8 for the venous/arterial/neuropathic ulcer differential), muscle wasting.
2. Palpate temperature (compare sides, work distally), capillary refill.
3. Palpate pulses in sequence: femoral → popliteal → posterior tibial → dorsalis pedis, saying aloud each one as you go and grading presence/strength.
4. Buerger's test if indicated: elevate the leg to 45° and look for pallor, then hang the leg over the bed edge and look for reactive hyperaemia (positive in significant PAD).
5. **ABPI measurement:** use a hand-held Doppler; measure systolic pressure at the brachial artery (both arms, use the higher reading), then at the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries in each leg; ABPI = highest ankle pressure ÷ highest brachial pressure, calculated separately for each leg.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each pulse check and the rationale for ABPI ("I'm calculating the ratio of ankle to arm systolic pressure to screen for peripheral arterial disease").

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** state pulse findings, any trophic changes/ulcers, and the calculated ABPI with its interpretation.

**Cross-reference:** see [[01_Cardiovascular]] section 0.36.1–0.36.2 for PAD disease detail and ABPI value interpretation (>1.2 calcified, 0.9–1.2 normal, <0.9 likely PAD, <0.5 severe) — that interpretation isn't repeated here.

## 1.7 Respiratory Examination

**Opening:**
- Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm patient's name and DOB, explain what the examination involves, gain consent
- Expose the patient's chest appropriately (offer an observer), position sitting up at 45°
- General inspection — say aloud: "I'm looking for respiratory distress, use of accessory muscles, audible wheeze/stridor, cyanosis, scars, chest wall deformity, and any equipment around the bed (O2, inhalers, sputum pot)."

**Step-by-step sequence:**

1. **Hands:** clubbing (see [[02_Respiratory]] for the conditions associated with it — bronchiectasis, IPF, lung cancer, CF), tar staining, fine tremor (β-agonist use) or flapping tremor/asterixis (CO2 retention — ask the patient to hold their arms out, wrists cocked back, for ~15 seconds), peripheral cyanosis.
2. **Pulse and respiratory rate:** count respiratory rate discreetly (patients alter their breathing if they know it's being counted) while ostensibly still checking the pulse.
3. **Face:** central cyanosis (under the tongue), Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhydrosis — associated with a Pancoast tumour, see [[02_Respiratory]] Lung Cancers), conjunctival pallor.
4. **Neck:** trachea position (deviation — see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Chest X-Ray — Systematic Approach for the pulling/pushing logic), lymphadenopathy (supraclavicular nodes especially, relevant to lung cancer staging), JVP if right heart strain/cor pulmonale suspected (see [[02_Respiratory]] COPD complications).
5. **Chest inspection:** shape/symmetry (barrel chest in COPD, pectus deformities), scars (thoracotomy, chest drain sites), visible use of accessory muscles, chest wall movement symmetry.
6. **Palpation:** chest expansion (hands on the chest wall, thumbs meeting in the midline, assess symmetry of movement — reduced on the side of pathology e.g. effusion, pneumothorax, collapse), tactile vocal fremitus (optional/less commonly performed now).
7. **Percussion:** systematically compare side to side, apex to base, including the axillae — say aloud what you're doing and comparing. Dull = consolidation/effusion (stony dull specifically for a large effusion); hyper-resonant = pneumothorax/hyperinflation (COPD).
8. **Auscultation:** systematically compare side to side, apex to base, including the axillae, using the diaphragm, asking the patient to breathe deeply through their mouth. Note breath sounds (vesicular vs bronchial — bronchial breathing over an area of consolidation is a key sign) and added sounds (crackles — fine [IPF] vs coarse [bronchiectasis, pulmonary oedema]; wheeze — polyphonic [asthma/COPD] vs monophonic [focal obstruction, e.g. tumour]; pleural rub). Also assess vocal resonance ("99" or "1-1-1") — increased over consolidation, reduced/absent over an effusion.
9. **Sacral/ankle oedema:** screen for right heart failure/cor pulmonale if relevant.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** "This is [patient], a [age]-year-old [sex] who on examination has [key positive findings — e.g. reduced expansion, dullness, bronchial breathing] on the [side/zone], consistent with [differential, e.g. consolidation]. I would like to complete my examination by checking sputum, temperature, and oxygen saturations, and would like to see a chest X-ray."

**Cross-reference:** see [[02_Respiratory]] for the disease-level interpretation of specific findings (consolidation, effusion, pneumothorax, etc.) and [[Investigation-Interpretation]] for the CXR systematic approach that typically follows this exam.

## 1.9 Abdominal Examination

**Opening:**
- Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm patient's name and DOB, explain what the examination involves, gain consent
- Expose from nipples to knees ideally (maintaining dignity — cover appropriately between steps, offer an observer), position lying flat with one pillow, arms by their sides
- General inspection — say aloud: "I'm looking around the bed for relevant clues (vomit bowl, stoma bags, NG tube, medication charts) and assessing the patient's general state — comfortable at rest, jaundiced, cachectic, or in visible distress."

**Step-by-step sequence:**

1. **Hands:** clubbing (IBD, cirrhosis, malignancy — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] for the disease associations), leuconychia (hypoalbuminaemia), koilonychia (iron deficiency), palmar erythema and Dupuytren's contracture (chronic liver disease), asterixis (hepatic encephalopathy — ask the patient to hold their arms out, wrists cocked back).
2. **Arms:** bruising/petechiae (coagulopathy), track marks (IVDU — hepatitis/cirrhosis risk), muscle wasting.
3. **Face:** conjunctival pallor (anaemia), jaundice — check the sclera in natural light specifically (more sensitive than skin colour), Kayser-Fleischer rings if relevant (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Wilson's Disease), angular stomatitis/glossitis (iron/B12 deficiency).
4. **Neck/chest:** lymphadenopathy — specifically Virchow's node (left supraclavicular — Troisier's sign, associated with gastric malignancy, see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Colorectal Cancer/gastric cancer content), spider naevi (count and note distribution — chronic liver disease if >5, in the SVC distribution), gynaecomastia.
5. **Abdomen inspection:** scars (note position and ask about previous surgery — relevant to adhesion-related SBO risk, see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO)), visible masses, distension, striae, caput medusae, visible peristalsis (suggests obstruction), stomas (note type/output — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Colorectal Cancer stoma types table).
6. **Palpation:** ask about pain first and examine that area last. Light palpation in all 9 regions (say the region names aloud as you go — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Abdominal Pain — Regional Anatomy and DDx for what each region contains), then deep palpation. Specifically check for guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness (peritonism — see red flags in the same section), Murphy's sign (cholecystitis — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]]), McBurney's point tenderness (appendicitis). Palpate for organomegaly — liver edge (percuss the upper border first, then palpate up from the RIF on inspiration), spleen (palpate from the RIF towards the left costal margin on inspiration — the spleen is not normally palpable), kidneys (ballot bimanually).
7. **Percussion:** general percussion for tenderness, liver span (upper and lower borders), splenic dullness, shifting dullness for ascites (percuss from the umbilicus outward to the flank, mark the dull-to-resonant transition, roll the patient and re-percuss — a shift confirms free fluid, see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ArLD) for the ascites content).
8. **Auscultation:** bowel sounds (absent — ileus, see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Ileus; tinkling/hyperactive — mechanical obstruction, see Small/Large Bowel Obstruction), bruits (renal artery stenosis, AAA — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Aortic Aneurysm).
9. **Complete the examination:** offer to check external genitalia and perform a digital rectal examination (DRE — see below), check the legs for oedema, and state you would dip the urine.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** "This is [patient], a [age]-year-old [sex] who on examination has [key positive findings], with [relevant palpation/percussion/auscultation findings]. This is consistent with [differential]. I would like to complete my examination with a DRE, urinalysis, and observations."

**Cross-reference:** see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] for the disease-level interpretation of specific findings and [[Investigation-Interpretation]] for relevant follow-up investigations (e.g. AXR/CT after an abnormal abdominal exam).

## 1.10 Digital Rectal Examination (DRE)

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination clearly and sensitively (many patients find this examination embarrassing or distressing — explicit, calm explanation matters more here than for most exams), gain explicit consent, offer a observer (and document that one was offered/present), ensure privacy.

> [!danger] Do NOT perform a DRE if an anal fissure is suspected (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Anal Fissures) — it is contraindicated due to pain and sphincter spasm, and won't add useful diagnostic information in that context.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Position the patient in the left lateral position, knees drawn up towards the chest.
2. Inspect the perianal area first, before inserting anything — look for external haemorrhoids, skin tags, fissures, fistula openings, excoriation, masses (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Haemorrhoids, Anal Fissures, Anal Fistulae for what these findings mean).
3. Warn the patient before touching, then perform digital examination with a lubricated, gloved finger — assess anal tone (resting and on voluntary squeeze), and any masses/tenderness/blood on the glove.
4. In males, note prostate size/consistency/tenderness as part of the same examination (relevant to urological differentials, not covered in this GI-focused file).
5. Withdraw and inspect the glove for blood (fresh, melaena-like, or mucus) before disposing.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each step, particularly the warning before insertion and what you are assessing for.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe perianal inspection findings, sphincter tone, any masses, and the appearance of any material on the glove.

**Cross-reference:** see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Lower GI Bleed (LGIB) for the differential of PR findings by cause, and the Haemorrhoids/Anal Fissures/Anal Fistulae sections for specific anorectal pathology this exam screens for.

## 1.12 Pre-Anaesthetic Assessment (Airway + Fitness for Anaesthesia)

`CF-PAIR` **3 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `0.5 Pre-Operative Assessment` · `0.1 Preoperative Assessment` · `0.2 Airway Assessment and Anaesthetic Technique` — from 03a_Anaesthetics_Primer.md · AN1_Perioperative_Care.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the purpose of the assessment (identifying anything that could make anaesthesia riskier and planning around it), gain consent for the discussion/examination.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. **History:** PMH (especially cardiorespiratory disease), medications/allergies, previous anaesthetic history (personal and family — screen specifically for any family history of anaesthetic complications, relevant to malignant hyperthermia risk), fasting status.
2. **Discuss risks:** dental damage, sore throat, PONV, and — for higher-risk patients — MI/stroke risk; tailor the depth of this discussion to the planned procedure and the patient's baseline risk.
3. **Assign ASA grade** (functional status) — say aloud which grade you're assigning and why: 1 (normally fit and well) through 6 (brain dead, organ donation) — see [[03a_Anaesthetics_Primer]] General Anaesthesia for the full grading table.
4. **Airway assessment — "LEMON":**
   - **L**ook externally — abnormal neck anatomy, large tongue, dental issues
   - **E**valuate 3-3-2 — 3 fingers between the patient's own teeth (mouth opening), 2 fingers between the hyoid and mentum (chin), 2 fingers between the hyoid and the thyroid notch
   - **M**allampati score — ask the patient to open their mouth and protrude their tongue, grade the visible oropharyngeal structures (Class I–IV, higher class = harder anticipated intubation)
   - **O**bstruction/obesity — screen for anything suggesting upper airway obstruction or a body habitus that could complicate airway management
   - **N**eck mobility — ask the patient to flex/extend their neck; reduced mobility (trauma, elderly, ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis with C-spine involvement) makes laryngoscopy harder
5. State aloud what a difficult-airway finding would change about the anaesthetic plan (e.g. awake fibreoptic intubation, senior anaesthetist involvement, difficult airway trolley on standby) — this anticipatory-planning step is often specifically assessed.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** "This patient is ASA [grade], with [relevant PMH/risk factors]. On airway assessment, [LEMON findings] — I would/would not anticipate a difficult airway. My plan would be [outline]."

**Cross-reference:** see [[03a_Anaesthetics_Primer]] General Anaesthesia for the ASA grading table, and Pre-op Instructions for the current medication-cessation guidance this assessment feeds into.

## 1.13 Cranial Nerve Examination

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this, but it's implied by extensive content already built in [[04_Neurology]] (Bell's Palsy, Horner's Syndrome, brainstem stroke syndromes, myasthenia gravis) that depends on a systematic cranial nerve exam — added given how foundational and high-yield this station is.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (testing the nerves of the face and head), gain consent.

**Step-by-step sequence (CN I–XII):**
1. **CN I (olfactory):** ask about any change in sense of smell (rarely formally tested with actual scents in an OSCE setting, but ask the question).
2. **CN II (optic):** visual acuity (Snellen chart), visual fields (confrontation testing — screens for the hemianopia/quadrantanopia patterns detailed in [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation and Bitemporal Hemianopia box), pupillary light reflex (direct and consensual — relevant to Horner's syndrome and raised ICP assessment, see [[04_Neurology]]), fundoscopy (papilloedema — see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP).
3. **CN III, IV, VI (oculomotor, trochlear, abducens):** eye movements in an "H" pattern, screening for diplopia and specific palsies (CN III palsy — ptosis + "down and out" eye + dilated pupil, relevant to raised ICP/herniation; CN VI palsy — cannot look laterally, a false localising sign in raised ICP given its long intracranial course, see [[04_Neurology]] Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumour Cerebri) Hypertension); nystagmus (relevant to cerebellar and vertigo assessment — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo).
4. **CN V (trigeminal):** facial sensation in all three divisions (ophthalmic, maxillary, mandibular), corneal reflex, motor function (clench jaw, feel masseter/temporalis bulk) — relevant to [[04_Neurology]] Trigeminal Neuralgia.
5. **CN VII (facial):** "raise your eyebrows," "screw your eyes shut," "puff out your cheeks," "show me your teeth" — **the single most important test here is whether the forehead is spared or involved**, distinguishing an UMN (stroke) from LMN (Bell's palsy) facial weakness — see [[04_Neurology]] Bell's Palsy for the full explanation of this sign.
6. **CN VIII (vestibulocochlear):** hearing (whisper test or finger rub, formal audiometry if indicated), Rinne and Weber tests to distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss — see the dedicated Rinne and Weber Tests entry below for the full technique and interpretation, not repeated here; ask about vertigo — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo.
7. **CN IX, X (glossopharyngeal, vagus):** ask the patient to say "ah" and watch for symmetrical palatal elevation (uvula deviates away from the side of the lesion), gag reflex (rarely tested directly given discomfort, but mention it), swallowing/voice quality.
8. **CN XI (accessory):** shoulder shrug and head turn against resistance (trapezius and sternocleidomastoid).
9. **CN XII (hypoglossal):** tongue protrusion — deviates **toward** the side of a LMN lesion (unlike the uvula, which deviates away) — a commonly confused point worth stating explicitly when narrating.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each nerve as you test it and what a positive/abnormal finding would mean; explicitly state the forehead-sparing distinction when examining CN VII, since this is one of the highest-yield teaching points in the whole cranial nerve exam.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** report by nerve number in order, stating clearly which nerves were normal and which were abnormal, then localise (e.g. "CN VII palsy with forehead involvement, consistent with a lower motor neuron lesion such as Bell's palsy, rather than a central/UMN cause").

**Cross-reference:** see [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation, Bell's Palsy, Horner's Syndrome, and Myasthenia Gravis for the disease-level differentials this exam feeds into.

## 1.14 Peripheral Neurological Examination (Upper/Lower Limb)

> [!note] As with the cranial nerve exam above, no exact-matching CSV row, but this is the essential bedside counterpart to nearly all the [[04_Neurology]] content on weakness, UMN vs LMN patterns, and specific peripheral conditions (GBS, myasthenia, peripheral neuropathy, radiculopathy) — added given its foundational importance.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination, gain consent, expose the limbs appropriately while maintaining dignity.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. **Inspection:** look for muscle wasting, fasciculations (LMN sign — see [[04_Neurology]] Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) Sclerosis), tremor, involuntary movements, scars, deformity, pes cavus/high-arched feet (Charcot-Marie-Tooth, Friedreich's ataxia — see [[04_Neurology]]).
2. **Tone:** assess tone at each major joint — increased tone can be spastic (velocity-dependent "clasp-knife," UMN pattern) or rigid (constant resistance throughout range, extrapyramidal — cogwheel rigidity in Parkinson's, see [[04_Neurology]] Parkinson's Disease (PD)); decreased tone (hypotonia) suggests a LMN, cerebellar, or acute UMN (spinal shock) cause.
3. **Power:** test each major muscle group against resistance, grade using the MRC scale (0 = no movement, through to 5 = normal power); compare proximal vs distal and left vs right — the pattern (proximal-predominant vs distal-predominant, symmetrical vs asymmetrical) is itself diagnostically useful — see [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis.
4. **Reflexes:** biceps, triceps, supinator (upper limb); knee, ankle (lower limb); plantar response (Babinski sign — upgoing plantar is a UMN sign, though normal in infants). Hyperreflexia supports a UMN cause; hyporeflexia/areflexia supports a LMN, NMJ, or peripheral nerve cause (e.g. the classic areflexia of Guillain-Barré Syndrome — see [[04_Neurology]]).
5. **Coordination:** finger-nose-finger test (intention tremor/past-pointing — cerebellar), heel-shin test, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia) — see the DANISH cerebellar mnemonic in [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation.
6. **Sensation:** light touch, pinprick (spinothalamic tract), vibration and proprioception (dorsal columns) — testing each modality separately matters, since a dissociated sensory loss pattern (e.g. loss of one modality with preservation of another) localises to specific tract lesions — see [[04_Neurology]] Spinal Disorders (Brown-Séquard syndrome, subacute combined degeneration) for how these patterns map to specific cord syndromes. Map any deficit to a dermatomal, peripheral nerve, or "glove and stocking" (peripheral neuropathy) distribution.
7. **Gait:** observe walking, including heel walking, toe walking, and tandem (heel-to-toe) gait — screens for foot drop, cerebellar ataxia, and the specific gait patterns associated with named conditions (parkinsonian shuffling gait, high-stepping gait of foot drop — see [[04_Neurology]] Parkinson's Disease (PD) and Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease).
8. **Complete the examination:** offer to examine the cranial nerves (see above) and check for a sensory level on the trunk if a cord lesion is suspected.

**What to say aloud:** narrate the pattern you're building as you go (e.g. "I'm finding reduced power distally with preserved proximal strength and reduced reflexes, which would suggest a peripheral neuropathy rather than a myopathy").

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** summarise using the UMN/LMN framework — "This patient has [increased/decreased] tone, [pattern of] weakness, [increased/decreased] reflexes, and [an upgoing/downgoing] plantar response, consistent with a [UMN/LMN] lesion at the level of [localisation]." Then state the differential this points toward.

**Cross-reference:** see [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis for the full structured DDx this exam feeds directly into, and Brain Lesion Localisation/Spinal Disorders for how specific patterns localise to specific anatomical levels.

## 1.15 Dermatological Examination

`CF-PAIR` **4 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `Skin lesion morphology — reference terms` · `0.1 Describing a Rash` · `0.1 Assessing a Pigmented Lesion` · `0.5 Nails` — from 09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous.md · G1_Acute_Rash_and_Erythema.md · G5_Lumps__Ulcers_and_Nails.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Dermatology category: "Skin and soft tissue surgery including skin cancers," "Skin lesions" among others, Medium yield) — a systematic examination technique without a dedicated template despite 9 full disease-category files having been built ([[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]] through [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]]). This entry covers the examination approach and systematic lesion description; cross-references the disease sections for diagnosis-specific detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking closely at the skin, and any specific lesion of concern), gain consent, ensure adequate lighting and privacy, expose the relevant area (with observer as appropriate) — a full skin examination should extend beyond just the presenting lesion, given incidental findings are common and occasionally more significant than the presenting complaint.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **General inspection from a distance first:** overall distribution pattern (localised vs generalised, symmetrical vs asymmetrical), general skin colour/pigmentation, obvious scarring or previous excision sites, nail and hair involvement, any signs of systemic illness (pallor, jaundice, cachexia).
2. **Close inspection and systematic description of the primary lesion(s)** — using the standard morphological vocabulary already established in [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Skin lesion morphology — reference terms (macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, pustule, nodule, wheal, erosion, ulcer — not repeated here): **site and distribution**, **size** (measured, not estimated, and photographed/documented for future comparison where malignancy is a concern), **shape** (regular vs irregular border), **colour** (uniform vs variegated), **surface characteristics** (scaly, crusted, weeping, ulcerated, umbilicated), **secondary change** (excoriation from scratching, lichenification from chronic rubbing, secondary infection).
3. **Palpation:** texture (smooth, rough, indurated), temperature (warm suggests active inflammation/infection), tenderness, fluctuance (suggests an abscess/fluid collection), mobility/fixation to underlying structures (fixation raises concern for malignant infiltration), blanching on pressure (the glass test — see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes — approach and differential for the full differential this feeds into, not repeated here).
4. **Regional lymphadenopathy:** examine the draining lymph node basin for any lesion with infective or malignant potential — a routinely-omitted step that is directly relevant to staging in skin cancer and to identifying spread in infective conditions.
5. **Mucosal and nail examination where relevant:** oral mucosa (relevant to the SJS/TEN spectrum, lichen planus, pemphigus — see the relevant disease sections, not repeated here), nail changes (pitting and onycholysis in psoriasis, nail-fold telangiectasia in connective tissue disease, melanonychia/Hutchinson's sign as a red flag for subungual melanoma — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma).

**Dermoscopy (if available/trained):** a magnified, polarised-light examination technique used particularly for pigmented lesion assessment — screens for the specific dermoscopic features referenced throughout [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] (e.g. milia-like cysts and comedo-like openings in seborrhoeic keratosis, arborising vessels and leaf-like pigmentation in pigmented BCC), not a substitute for biopsy when malignancy is suspected but a useful adjunct to clinical judgement.

**Suspicious pigmented lesion assessment:** apply the **ABCDE** framework (Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variability, Diameter >6mm, Evolution) alongside the **"ugly duckling sign"** (a lesion that looks different from the patient's other naevi) — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma for the full detail and differential, not repeated here.

**What to say aloud:** narrate the systematic description as you go (site, size, shape, colour, surface, palpation findings), rather than jumping straight to a diagnosis — examiners are typically assessing the systematic process as much as the final answer.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe the lesion(s) systematically using the framework above before offering a differential, then state the differential in order of likelihood with the features that support/argue against each (e.g. "this is a 1.2cm, irregularly-bordered, variably-pigmented macule on the back with recent evolution reported — my leading differential is melanoma, given the ABCDE features present, and I would want to proceed to dermoscopy and consider excision biopsy").

**Cross-reference:** see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]], [[09_03a_Dermatology_-_Non-Melanoma_Skin_Cancer]], and [[History-Taking]] Skin Lesion / Rash for the disease-level differentials and history-taking approach this exam feeds into.

## 1.16 Lymph Node Examination

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this, but it's implied by extensive content already built that depends on a systematic lymph node exam — [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]], [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]], and [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy — added given how foundational and high-yield this examination is, similarly to the Cranial Nerve and Dermatological Examination entries above.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (feeling the lymph node regions), gain consent, adequately expose the relevant areas (neck, axillae, groin at minimum) with appropriate privacy/observer for axillary and groin examination.

**Systematic approach — examine all major node groups even if only one area is symptomatic, given the distinction between localised and generalised lymphadenopathy is itself diagnostically important:**
1. **Head and neck** — a genuinely systematic sub-sequence is expected: submental, submandibular, pre-auricular, post-auricular, occipital, then working down the neck — anterior cervical chain, posterior cervical chain, supraclavicular (palpate from behind the seated patient, which is the standard technique for this examination). **Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy is a particular red flag** — classically associated with intra-abdominal or intra-thoracic malignancy (e.g. Virchow's node on the left specifically, associated with gastric cancer) and should never be dismissed as reactive without further work-up.
2. **Axillary** — examine with the patient's arm relaxed and supported by the examiner (tensing the arm makes axillary examination harder, a common technique error), systematically covering the five axillary node groups (anterior/pectoral, posterior/subscapular, lateral/brachial, central, apical) — directly relevant to breast cancer staging, see the Breast Examination entry below.
3. **Inguinal** — palpate along the inguinal ligament; note that mild, small, non-tender inguinal lymphadenopathy is a common normal finding (reflecting minor lower-limb trauma/infection over a lifetime) in a way that wouldn't be considered normal in other node groups — a useful "don't over-call it" point specific to this site.
4. **Epitrochlear** (medial to the elbow) — a less commonly examined but occasionally high-yield site, associated with specific causes (e.g. some lymphomas, cat scratch disease if the exposure is on the ipsilateral hand/arm — see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Cat scratch disease).
5. **Abdominal/hepatosplenomegaly** — palpate for splenomegaly and hepatomegaly as part of the same systematic assessment, given lymphoproliferative and myeloproliferative disorders frequently cause organomegaly alongside lymphadenopathy (see [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Splenomegaly for the disease-level causes, not repeated here).

**For each palpable node, systematically describe:** site, size (measured), number (single vs multiple/matted), consistency (soft, firm, rubbery, hard/stony), mobility (mobile vs fixed to surrounding structures or skin), tenderness, overlying skin changes (erythema, warmth suggesting infective cause; skin tethering/ulceration suggesting malignant infiltration).

**Key discriminating features — benign/reactive vs malignant pattern:**
- **Reactive/infective:** tender, soft/rubbery, mobile, often with an identifiable local source of infection, may fluctuate in size, associated systemic infective symptoms.
- **Malignant/lymphomatous:** classically non-tender, firm/rubbery to hard, may be matted (fixed to adjacent nodes) or fixed to surrounding tissue, progressive enlargement, associated B-symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss — see [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]] for the disease-level detail on this pattern, not repeated here) — though note this is a general pattern, not an absolute rule, and any persistently enlarged or atypical node ultimately needs appropriate work-up rather than being reassured on examination findings alone.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each node group as you examine it and describe findings systematically using the framework above, rather than jumping to "no lymphadenopathy found" without describing the process.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe distribution (localised vs generalised — a genuinely important distinction, since localised lymphadenopathy points toward a local cause in the draining region while generalised lymphadenopathy broadens the differential to systemic causes per [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy), then the characteristics of the most significant node(s) using the systematic description above, then a differential.

**Cross-reference:** see [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]], [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]], and [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy for the disease-level differentials this exam feeds into.

## 1.17 Breast Examination

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 Triple Assessment` — from O7_Breast_Disease.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this specific examination technique, but it's implied by extensive content already built in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] (breast cancer, fibroadenoma, and the other breast lump differentials) that depends on a systematic breast exam — added given how foundational and high-yield this station is.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination clearly and sensitively (looking at and feeling the breast tissue and the draining lymph node areas), gain explicit consent, **offer and arrange a observer regardless of examiner/patient gender combination** — this is a standard, expected step for this specific examination given its intimate nature, and omitting it is a common and significant OSCE mark-scheme penalty. Position the patient sitting initially for inspection, then reclined at 45° for palpation.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Inspection (sitting, arms relaxed by sides, then raised above the head, then hands pressed on hips to tense pectoral muscles)** — each position can reveal different findings: symmetry, contour, skin changes (dimpling — often more apparent with arms raised, given tethering to underlying tissue becomes more visible under tension; peau d'orange — a specific sign of dermal lymphatic involvement, classically associated with inflammatory breast cancer, see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer), nipple changes (inversion, retraction, discharge, eczematous change — see Paget's disease of the breast in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer, not repeated here), visible masses.
2. **Palpation (reclined at 45°, examine each breast systematically)** — use the flat of the fingers (not fingertips/pinching, which can create a false sense of a "lump" from normal glandular tissue) in a systematic pattern (commonly a clockwise/spiral pattern from the nipple outward, or a quadrant-by-quadrant approach) covering all breast tissue including the axillary tail (the tissue extending into the axilla — a commonly missed area if the examination stops at the anatomical breast border).
3. **For any palpable lump, systematically describe** — using the general lump-description framework already established in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Fibroadenoma (site, size and shape; colour, contour, consistency; tethering, tenderness; fluctuance ± transillumination, not repeated here) — plus specifically for breast lumps: mobility (mobile vs fixed to skin or underlying chest wall — fixation is a red flag for malignant infiltration), relationship to the nipple.
4. **Nipple examination** — inspect for discharge (note colour/character if present — bloody discharge is a specific red flag, see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Duct papilloma for the classic association, not repeated here), inversion, or eczematous change.
5. **Lymph node examination** — axillary, supraclavicular, and infraclavicular nodes are an essential, integral part of the breast examination, not a separate optional step — see the Lymph Node Examination entry above for the systematic technique, not repeated here; axillary nodal status is directly relevant to breast cancer staging and surgical planning (see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer Mx, not repeated here).

**What to say aloud:** narrate the systematic inspection positions and palpation pattern as you go, and describe any lump found using the full framework above before offering a differential.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe inspection findings first, then palpation findings for each breast systematically, then lymph node findings, then a differential in order of likelihood with the features supporting/arguing against each.

**Cross-reference:** see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] for the full differential (breast cancer, fibroadenoma, fibrocystic changes, fat necrosis, lipoma, galactocele, phyllodes tumour, mammary duct ectasia, duct papilloma) this exam feeds into, not repeated here.

## 1.18 Fundoscopy (Direct Ophthalmoscopy)

`CF-PAIR` **2 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `Fundoscopy is the examination that most often makes the diagnosi` · `0.2 Drugs for Eye Examinations and Procedures` — from B2_Hypertension_Spectrum.md · NEW_Drugs_11_Eye.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Explicit CSV row ("Fundoscopy, Otoscopy," Ophthalmology category) — fundoscopy specifically is added here given how constantly it's referenced throughout [[05_Ophthalmology]] (retinal detachment, vein/artery occlusion, papilloedema, glaucoma, AMD, diabetic and hypertensive retinopathy) without ever having its own systematic technique write-up. Otoscopy — now that the ENT category is built — is added as its own entry immediately below.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking at the back of the eye using a light and lens), gain consent. Dim the room lights — pupil dilation (physiological or pharmacological with tropicamide, if permitted/available) significantly improves the view and is worth doing whenever practical, particularly for a genuinely thorough exam rather than a quick screen.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Ask the patient to fix their gaze on a distant point over your shoulder and keep looking at it throughout — this keeps the eye still and directs your view toward the optic disc as you approach along the correct visual axis.
2. Hold the ophthalmoscope in your right hand and use your right eye to examine the patient's right eye (and left/left for the other side) — standard convention that avoids your head/the instrument blocking the patient's fixation point and keeps you out of each other's way.
3. Start ~30cm away and identify the red reflex first (*why:* an absent or abnormal red reflex is itself diagnostically significant — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Retinal Detachment (red reflex lost), Cataracts (decreased red reflex), and is the standard first screening step in neonates/children for congenital cataract or retinoblastoma; *what:* a normal red-orange reflex; absence suggests a media opacity (cataract, vitreous haemorrhage) or, rarely, a more serious posterior pathology).
4. Move closer (to within a few cm) and locate the optic disc — following a blood vessel toward where it thickens/converges is a reliable way to find the disc if it's not immediately in view.
5. Systematically assess the optic disc: colour (pale suggests optic atrophy), margins (blurred/swollen — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Papilloedema and [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP), cup-to-disc ratio (increased — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Open-Angle Glaucoma fundoscopy signs).
6. Follow the four major vascular arcades out from the disc in each quadrant, assessing vessel calibre and any AV nicking (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Hypertensive Retinopathy), haemorrhages, exudates, cotton wool spots (see Diabetic Retinopathy and Hypertensive Retinopathy), and the venous pattern specifically if vein occlusion is suspected (see Retinal Vein Occlusion).
7. Finally, ask the patient to look directly at the light to bring the macula into view (briefly, given this is uncomfortable) — assess for drusen, macular scarring, or haemorrhage (see AMD).

**What to say aloud:** narrate each structure as you assess it and what an abnormal finding at each step would mean, particularly the red reflex check, since this is often specifically assessed as a standalone competency.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe systematically in the same order examined — red reflex, disc (colour/margin/cupping), vessels, retina/quadrants, macula — then state the differential the findings point toward.

**Cross-reference:** see the specific [[05_Ophthalmology]] disease sections referenced throughout above for the disease-level significance of each fundoscopic finding, not repeated here.

## 1.19 Otoscopy

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 Examining the Ear and Assessing Hearing` — from F1_Ear.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — the Ophthalmology-category CSV row explicitly named both Fundoscopy and Otoscopy together, and the ENT category (referenced constantly throughout [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]], [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]], and [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]]) is now built, resolving this project's oldest outstanding forward-reference.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking inside the ear canal with a light and a cone-shaped attachment), gain consent, ask about pain in either ear first and examine that side last/most gently if pain is present.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Select the largest speculum that comfortably fits the ear canal (a larger speculum gives a better, more stable view and is more comfortable than repeatedly needing to reposition a too-small one).
2. **Straighten the ear canal before inserting the otoscope** — in adults, gently pull the pinna **up and back**; in young children, pull the pinna **down and back** — this genuinely different technique between adults and children reflects the different natural angle of the ear canal at different ages, and using the wrong direction makes the view meaningfully harder to obtain.
3. Hold the otoscope like a pen, with the ulnar side of your hand resting against the patient's face/head — this acts as a stabilising fulcrum, so if the patient moves suddenly the otoscope moves with their head rather than being driven further into the canal, a genuine safety technique rather than just a comfort measure.
4. Insert gently under direct vision, advancing slowly and looking for the tympanic membrane as you go — do not advance blindly or forcefully, given the ear canal has a natural bend and forcing the speculum risks pain or trauma.
5. Systematically inspect the ear canal on the way in: wax (cerumen — may obscure the view and require removal before a full assessment is possible), discharge (colour/character — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Causes of a discharging ear for the differential this feeds into, not repeated here), erythema/swelling (otitis externa — see the same file's Otitis externa entry), foreign bodies.
6. **Systematically assess the tympanic membrane** using the "cone of light" (a reflection normally seen anteroinferiorly, more prominent in children) as an orientation landmark, then work through: colour (pearly grey is normal; red/bulging suggests acute otitis media — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Otitis media (acute); dull/grey/yellow with visible fluid level or bubbles suggests glue ear — see the same file's Glue ear entry), position (bulging vs retracted vs normal), translucency, and any perforation (note the size and position — anterior/posterior, and whether central or marginal, since marginal perforations carry higher cholesteatoma risk — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Causes of a discharging ear, where cholesteatoma sits, not repeated here), or a visible landmark abnormality (loss of the light reflex, or the handle/lateral process of the malleus being unusually prominent, both associated with a middle ear effusion per the file above).

**What to say aloud:** narrate the canal findings and each systematic TM assessment point as you go, particularly explicitly stating whether the light reflex and landmarks are visible/normal, since this is often specifically assessed.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe canal findings first (wax, discharge, erythema), then TM findings systematically (colour, position, light reflex, any perforation), then a differential.

**Cross-reference:** see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] for the full disease-level differential (otitis externa, acute otitis media, glue ear, chronic otitis media, cholesteatoma) this exam feeds into, not repeated here.

## 1.20 Rinne and Weber Tests

> [!note] Gap-filled — referenced as part of the Cranial Nerve Examination (CN VIII) above and constantly assumed throughout [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Hearing loss — differential diagnosis, but the actual technique and interpretation had never been written up as its own entry — a genuinely classic, high-yield bedside pair of tests using a 512Hz tuning fork to distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss before any formal audiometry is available.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (using a tuning fork to test hearing by bone and air conduction), gain consent. A **512Hz tuning fork** is standard — lower frequencies give a falsely prolonged bone conduction sensation, and higher frequencies decay too quickly to reliably compare, so this specific frequency isn't arbitrary.

**Technique — strike the tuning fork gently on a soft surface (e.g. your elbow or knee, not a hard surface) to set it vibrating without an overly harsh overtone.**

### 1.20.1 Rinne's test (compares air vs bone conduction in one ear at a time)

1. Place the vibrating fork's base firmly on the mastoid process (bone conduction) and ask the patient to say when they can no longer hear it.
2. Immediately move the fork (still vibrating) to just beside the ear canal, prongs facing forward (air conduction), and ask if they can now hear it again.
3. **Normal result (Rinne positive):** air conduction is heard for longer than bone conduction (as expected physiologically, given air conduction is normally more efficient) — the patient can still hear the fork by air after bone conduction has faded.
4. **Abnormal result (Rinne negative):** bone conduction is heard for longer than or equal to air conduction — suggests a **conductive** hearing loss in that ear, given the normal air-conduction advantage is lost or reversed.
5. Repeat on the other ear.

> [!danger] A "false negative" Rinne is a genuinely important pitfall: in **severe unilateral sensorineural hearing loss**, sound conducted through the skull bone during "bone conduction" testing can be picked up by the *opposite, better-hearing* cochlea, giving a falsely negative-looking result (mimicking conductive loss) in an ear that's actually profoundly sensorineural-deaf — this is exactly why Rinne is always interpreted **together** with Weber's test below, never in isolation, since Weber's test resolves this ambiguity.

### 1.20.2 Weber's test (compares bone conduction between the two ears)

1. Place the vibrating fork's base firmly in the midline of the forehead (or vertex).
2. Ask the patient **"where do you hear the sound — in the middle, or more to one side?"**
3. **Normal result:** the sound is heard centrally/equally in both ears.
4. **Lateralises to the affected ear:** suggests a **conductive** loss in that ear — the affected ear's bone conduction pathway isn't competing with as much ambient air-conducted noise, so the sound is paradoxically perceived as louder on the conductively-impaired side.
5. **Lateralises to the unaffected (better-hearing) ear:** suggests a **sensorineural** loss in the other ear — the affected cochlea simply cannot process the sound as well, so the good ear "wins."

**Combining Rinne and Weber to reach a conclusion — this pairing is the actual clinical skill, not either test alone:**

| Rinne | Weber | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Positive (normal) bilaterally | Central | Normal hearing |
| Negative in one ear | Lateralises to the **same** (abnormal) ear | Conductive hearing loss in that ear |
| Positive (normal) in both ears | Lateralises to **one** ear | Sensorineural hearing loss in the **opposite** (non-lateralising) ear |
| Negative in one ear (the "false negative" pitfall above) | Lateralises to the **opposite** ear | Severe sensorineural hearing loss in the "negative" ear — the Weber result reveals the true picture Rinne alone would have missed |

**What to say aloud:** narrate each step and ask the patient to respond clearly at each stage, then explicitly state your interpretation using the combined table logic above rather than reporting Rinne and Weber as two disconnected findings.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** state the Rinne result for each ear, the Weber lateralisation, then the combined interpretation and which side/type of hearing loss this indicates.

**Cross-reference:** see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Hearing loss — differential diagnosis for the full conductive vs sensorineural differential these findings feed into, not repeated here.

## 1.21 Vertigo Examination (Dix-Hallpike and the HINTS Exam)

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.2 Acute Vestibular Syndrome and the HINTS Examination` — from D5_Dizziness__Vertigo_and_Gait.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre and head impulse test are referenced diagnostically throughout [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] and [[History-Taking]] Vertigo and Dizziness, but neither the specific technique nor the broader HINTS bedside exam (a genuinely high-yield central-vs-peripheral discriminator) had ever been written up as an examination entry.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination clearly (specific head movements/positions to check for dizziness, and watching your eyes), gain consent, **warn the patient explicitly that the test is designed to reproduce their vertigo and this is expected, not a sign something has gone wrong** — given the whole point of Dix-Hallpike is provoking the symptom, an unwarned patient can understandably panic mid-test.

### 1.21.1 Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre (diagnostic test for BPPV)

1. Sit the patient upright on the examination couch with legs extended, positioned so that when they lie back their head will hang slightly off the end of the couch.
2. Turn the patient's head 45° toward the side being tested.
3. Rapidly (but smoothly, not roughly) lower the patient from sitting to lying, with the head extended approximately 20-30° below the horizontal (hanging off the end of the couch), maintaining the 45° rotation throughout.
4. **Observe the eyes closely for nystagmus and ask the patient if they feel their usual vertigo** — a positive test for BPPV shows a brief latency (a few seconds) before onset, then rotatory/torsional nystagmus beating toward the undermost ear, lasting under a minute, with the vertigo and nystagmus **fatiguing** (reducing) on repeat testing — this fatiguability is itself a useful confirmatory feature of BPPV specifically.
5. Slowly return the patient to sitting, then repeat testing the other side if the first side was negative.
6. If positive, the same position can transition directly into the **Epley manoeuvre** (the repositioning treatment for BPPV) — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) for the treatment-level detail, not repeated here.

> [!tip] A **negative** Dix-Hallpike doesn't fully exclude BPPV (particularly if performed between episodes, or if the causative canal isn't the one tested) — a compatible history with a negative single test can still warrant a treatment trial or repeat testing rather than immediately abandoning the diagnosis.

### 1.21.2 HINTS exam (bedside discrimination of peripheral vs central acute vestibular syndrome)

> [!info] HINTS stands for **H**ead Impulse, **N**ystagmus, **T**est of **S**kew — used specifically in a patient with acute, continuous vertigo (not episodic/positional vertigo, which Dix-Hallpike addresses instead) to help distinguish a peripheral cause (vestibular neuronitis/labyrinthitis) from a dangerous central cause (posterior circulation stroke) — genuinely higher-yield for this specific scenario than CT imaging, given early posterior fossa stroke can be missed on CT.

1. **Head Impulse test:** ask the patient to fixate on your nose; briskly and unpredictably rotate their head a small amount to one side, watching their eyes. **Normal (abnormal-appearing) result — a peripheral cause:** a brief corrective saccade (the eyes briefly lose fixation off your nose then rapidly "catch up" back to it) — this reflects a genuinely impaired vestibulo-ocular reflex on the affected side, which is what's expected in peripheral vestibular failure. **Abnormal (normal-appearing) result — concerning for a central cause:** the eyes stay fixated on your nose throughout with no corrective saccade — a **normal-looking** head impulse test in a patient with acute continuous vertigo is the counterintuitive, genuinely important finding that should raise concern for a central cause, since an intact vestibulo-ocular reflex despite significant vertigo suggests the problem isn't peripheral.
2. **Nystagmus:** observe for direction and whether it changes direction with gaze. **Peripheral:** unidirectional, horizontal nystagmus that doesn't change direction with gaze position. **Central (concerning):** direction-changing (bidirectional) nystagmus, or vertical/torsional nystagmus — any of these should raise concern for a central cause.
3. **Test of Skew:** alternately cover each eye while the patient fixates on a distant point, watching for vertical correction as each eye is uncovered. **Peripheral:** no skew deviation. **Central (concerning):** a vertical corrective movement as the covered eye is uncovered (skew deviation) suggests a brainstem/central cause.

> [!danger] The mnemonic for the concerning (central-suggestive) HINTS pattern is sometimes given as "INFARCT": **I**mpulse **N**ormal, **F**ast-phase **A**lternating, **R**efixation on **C**over **T**est — i.e. a normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, and a positive skew test together suggest a central cause and warrant urgent stroke work-up (see [[Examination]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient) and [[04_Neurology]] for the disease-level stroke pathway this feeds into, not repeated here) — genuinely counterintuitive given a "normal" head impulse test result is the worrying one in this specific context, opposite to how a normal test result is usually reassuring.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each of the three HINTS components and your interpretation as you go, and explicitly state whether the overall pattern is peripheral-favouring or central-concerning.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe the clinical context first (acute continuous vs episodic/positional vertigo, which determines whether Dix-Hallpike or HINTS is the appropriate test), then the specific findings for whichever test was performed, then the resulting differential and any escalation this triggers.

**Cross-reference:** see [[History-Taking]] Vertigo and Dizziness for the history that determines which of these tests is indicated, and [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] for the disease-level detail (BPPV, vestibular neuronitis, labyrinthitis, Ménière's disease) each finding feeds into, not repeated here.

## 1.22 Mental State Examination (MSE)

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 Psychiatric Assessment and the Mental State Examination` — from N1_Mental_State_Examination_and_Risk.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Psychiatric history and mental state examination," High yield) — genuinely absent despite the extensive Psychiatry disease content built across [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] through [[14a-2_Psych_-_Overdose_and_Poisoning_Management]] that the MSE is designed to structure and document. This entry covers the standard MSE domains; cross-references the disease sections for the specific findings each domain feeds into rather than repeating them.

**Opening:** the MSE is conducted throughout a psychiatric consultation via observation and structured questioning, not as a separate, isolated add-on — much of it (appearance, behaviour, speech, mood) can be assessed simply by attentive observation during the history-taking, though certain domains (particularly cognition and insight) require specific, deliberate questions.

**The standard MSE domains — commonly remembered via the mnemonic "ASEPTIC" (Appearance/behaviour, Speech, Emotion/mood, Perception, Thought, Insight/judgement, Cognition):**

1. **Appearance and behaviour:** general appearance (grooming, hygiene, dress — self-neglect can be a marker of depression, psychosis, or cognitive decline), eye contact, rapport, psychomotor activity (agitation or retardation — see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Depression (Major depressive disorder) for the depressive pattern, not repeated here), any abnormal movements, attitude toward the interviewer (cooperative, guarded, hostile, disinhibited).
2. **Speech:** rate, rhythm, volume, quantity, and tone — **pressured speech** (rapid, difficult to interrupt) is a classic finding in mania (see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Bipolar disorder, not repeated here); slowed, quiet, or minimal speech can reflect depression or psychomotor retardation; note fluency and any dysarthria, which point toward an organic/neurological cause rather than a primary psychiatric one (see [[04_Neurology]] for the relevant differential, not repeated here).
3. **Emotion — mood and affect:** **mood** is the patient's subjectively reported, sustained emotional state (ask directly, and quote their own words) — **affect** is the clinician's objective observation of the patient's emotional expression during the interview (range, reactivity, and congruence with the reported mood and the content of speech); note whether affect is congruent or incongruent with mood/thought content (e.g. laughing while describing a distressing delusion is affect-thought incongruence, a finding relevant to psychotic disorders — see [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]]).
4. **Perception:** ask directly about hallucinations in any sensory modality (most commonly auditory in schizophrenia — see [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]] Schizophrenia, not repeated here) and illusions (a misperception of a real external stimulus, distinct from a hallucination which occurs without any external stimulus) — direct, specific questions are usually needed here, since patients often don't volunteer perceptual disturbances unprompted.
5. **Thought — form and content:** **thought form** refers to the structure/flow of thinking (assessed from how the patient speaks, not what they say) — screen for the disorganised thinking patterns detailed in [[14_03_Psych_-_Psychotic_Disorders_and_Antipsychotics]] Features that define psychotic disorders (DSM-5) (loosening of association, tangentiality, circumstantiality, flight of ideas — the latter classically manic rather than psychotic, see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Bipolar disorder), not repeated here. **Thought content** refers to what the patient is actually thinking about — screen specifically for delusions (see the same psychotic-features entry for the types and definition, not repeated here), obsessions (see [[14_02_Psych_-_Anxiety_and_Related_Disorders]] Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD)), and **always explicitly ask about suicidal and self-harm ideation, intent, and plan** — see [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] Suicide for the SADPERSONS risk factors and broader risk assessment approach this feeds into, not repeated here; this is not an optional domain to skip if time is short, given its direct safety implications.
6. **Insight and judgement:** **insight** — does the patient recognise they have a mental illness, understand its nature, and accept the need for treatment (a spectrum, not simply present/absent — a patient may accept they're unwell but not accept the specific diagnosis or treatment recommended, which is itself clinically informative); **judgement** — the patient's capacity to make reasonable decisions about everyday situations and their own safety, often assessed through how they describe recent real-life decisions rather than a hypothetical scenario, which gives a more genuine picture than an artificial test question.
7. **Cognition:** orientation (time, place, person), attention/concentration, memory (immediate, short-term, long-term) — a brief bedside screen (e.g. serial 7s, or asking the patient to recall three objects after a delay) is often sufficient to screen for a gross deficit, with formal cognitive testing (e.g. MMSE, MoCA) reserved for when a more detailed assessment is indicated; a significant cognitive deficit should prompt consideration of an organic/delirium-related cause rather than assuming a primary psychiatric diagnosis, particularly in a patient without a prior psychiatric history — see [[04_Neurology]] Delirium for the relevant differential and the "serial 7s to distinguish organic from psychiatric memory loss" tip already established there, not repeated here.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each domain as you assess it during presentation, using the standard ASEPTIC structure so the assessment is systematic and easy to follow, rather than describing findings in the order they happened to come up in conversation.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** work through each domain in the standard order above, explicitly stating both positive and negative findings (e.g. "no evidence of psychotic symptoms" is itself a meaningful, reportable finding, not something to omit because it's normal) — conclude with a summary formulation and differential.

**Cross-reference:** see [[History-Taking]] for the presentation-led history-taking entries (e.g. Bruising and Bleeding, Joint Pain) this pairs with in the same way GALS pairs with Joint Pain history — the MSE is the psychiatric examination counterpart to a full psychiatric history, which isn't separately built here given the disease-specific diagnostic criteria boxes throughout [[14_01_Psych_-_Mood_Disorders__Depression__Suicide__Bipolar_]] through [[14_05c_Psych_-_Unexplained_Symptoms__Somatoform__Dissociative__Factitious_Disorders_]] already function as the history-taking framework for each specific presentation.

## 1.23 Approach to Paediatric Examination

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `**· Get down to the child's level, introduce yourself to THEM, a` — from M1_The_Seriously_Unwell_Child.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Approach to Paediatric Examination," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as a general approach despite the extensive Paediatrics disease content built across [[15_01a_Paeds_-_Paediatric_and_Newborn_Life_Support]] through [[15_24b_Paeds_-_Screening__SIDS__Vaccination_Schedule]]. This entry covers the genuinely different *approach* a paediatric examination requires compared to an adult one — the individual system examinations elsewhere in this file (cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, etc.) still apply, adapted per the principles below, rather than needing entirely separate paediatric versions of each.

**Opening — genuinely different from an adult consultation, not just a smaller-scale version of it:**
- Get down to the child's eye level rather than towering over them, particularly for toddlers and preschoolers — a simple positioning change that measurably reduces distress and resistance.
- Build rapport with both the child and the accompanying parent/caregiver — establish who the history is coming from and involve the child directly wherever their developmental level allows, rather than speaking only to the parent.
- Be honest about anything that will be uncomfortable, in age-appropriate language — telling a child something will hurt when it will, rather than falsely reassuring them, preserves trust for the rest of the examination and for future encounters.
- Allow the child to inspect equipment first (e.g. let them hold or look at the stethoscope) — this demystifies the examination and is a genuinely effective way to reduce fear, not just a nicety.

**Order of examination — the single most important structural difference from an adult exam:**
- **Least distressing components first, most distressing last** — general observation and auscultation before palpation, and invasive/uncomfortable components (ear and throat examination, anything requiring the child to lie flat or be restrained) saved for the end, regardless of the "logical" head-to-toe order taught for adults — a crying, distressed child from an early uncomfortable step makes every subsequent step harder, so sequencing genuinely changes the quality of information you can gather.
- **Opportunistic examination** — much of the exam can and should happen while still taking the history and building rapport, before formally "starting" the examination; a squirming toddler on a parent's lap can often be auscultated, observed for work of breathing, and have their gait/movement assessed long before they'd tolerate a formal, structured exam.
- **Age-specific positioning:** infants and toddlers are often best examined on the parent's lap rather than a couch, given this is both more comforting for the child and gives a more representative picture (a toddler forced onto an unfamiliar couch may cry and tense up in a way that obscures findings); older children can generally be examined more similarly to adults.

**General inspection — disproportionately informative in paediatrics, more so than in adult examination:**
- **Developmental milestones should be reviewed opportunistically as part of every examination**, not only when development is the presenting concern — see [[15_19a_Paeds_-_Developmental_Milestones_and_Delay]] Developmental milestones and red flags for the specific ages and red flags this feeds into, not repeated here; a doctor examining a child for an unrelated complaint (e.g. a rash) should still be alert to whether the child's behaviour, movement, and interaction are broadly appropriate for their age.
- **Growth parameters** (height/length, weight, head circumference, plotted on the appropriate growth chart) are a routine, expected part of a paediatric examination in a way they generally aren't for a healthy adult — see [[Investigation-Interpretation]] Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation for the systematic approach to this, not repeated here.
- General appearance and behaviour, observed from the moment of first contact (in the waiting room or as the family enters) rather than only once formally "examining" — a child's spontaneous behaviour is often more informative than what can be elicited on command, particularly in younger children who won't cooperate with direct instruction.

**Vital signs — genuinely different normal ranges by age, unlike a single adult reference range:**
- Heart rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure normal ranges all vary substantially and continuously with age in children — a heart rate that would be alarming tachycardia in an adult is normal in an infant, and vice versa; always interpret paediatric vital signs against the age-appropriate reference range rather than an adult one, and note that **cardiac output in infants and younger children is regulated predominantly by heart rate change** (rather than stroke volume, as in adults), making tachycardia a genuinely important early sign of compromise in this age group specifically — see [[Examination]] Vital Signs and Early Warning Scores (Recognising the Deteriorating Patient) for the general escalation framework this feeds into, not repeated here.

**Common pitfalls specific to paediatric examination:**
- Distinguishing wheeze from stridor — genuinely different sounds with different differentials (lower vs upper airway pathology respectively), commonly confused by inexperienced examiners; see [[13_05b_ENT_-_Stridor__Croup__Epiglottitis__Laryngomalacia__OSA]] Stridor — overview for the differential this feeds into, not repeated here.
- Recognising illness severity through observation before hands-on findings — e.g. a child with peritonism classically lies very still (in contrast to the restless writhing of colicky pain), a pattern worth knowing given it's observable before any palpation occurs.
- Being familiar with the common, important rash patterns in children specifically (e.g. the purpuric rash of meningococcal septicaemia, versus the purpura of ITP or HSP) given rashes are a very common paediatric presentation and the differential carries genuinely different urgency depending on the pattern — see the relevant disease files (e.g. [[15_14_Paeds_-_Anaemia__Sickle_Cell__Hereditary_Spherocytosis__HSP]]) for the specific differential, not repeated here.

**Cross-reference:** the individual system examinations elsewhere in this file (cardiovascular, respiratory, abdominal, ENT, neurological) remain the technique reference for each system — this entry covers the paediatric-specific *approach and sequencing* that should be layered on top of those techniques, not a replacement for them.

## 1.24 Newborn Examination

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Newborn examination and screening," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as its own structured entry despite individual newborn findings (red reflex, minor skin conditions, cephalohaematoma) being scattered across other files. This is a distinct, formally structured examination — the "Newborn Infant Physical Examination" performed on every baby, classically within the first 72 hours — not just an assumed extension of the general paediatric approach above. Verified against Queensland Health's Newborn Baby Assessment (routine) clinical guideline, Aug 2026.

**Opening:** wash hands, confirm parental consent, and ask about any specific concerns before starting — a systematic **top-to-toe** approach is standard, but be genuinely opportunistic: if the baby's eyes are open, take that moment for the red reflex; if settled, that's the best window for heart/lung auscultation, rather than rigidly forcing a fixed order regardless of the baby's state.

- **General inspection:** colour (jaundice, cyanosis, pallor), tone, posture, activity, cry quality (a weak, high-pitched, or absent cry, or failure to respond to consoling, are genuine red flags); dysmorphic features.
- **Head and neck:** fontanelles (size, tension — bulging or sunken are both abnormal), sutures, caput succedaneum vs cephalohaematoma (see [[15_23b_Paeds_-_Minor_Neonatal_Problems]], not repeated here), facial symmetry (assess specifically while crying, for facial nerve palsy), neck for masses.
- **Eyes:** **red reflex** using an ophthalmoscope (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] for the full findings this screens for, not repeated here) — genuinely one of the most important individual steps, since an absent/abnormal reflex can indicate congenital cataract or retinoblastoma.
- **Mouth:** inspect the palate to the uvula (don't just look — palpate with a gloved little finger to exclude a submucous cleft that visual inspection alone can miss), tongue-tie, cleft lip.
- **Cardiorespiratory:** auscultate heart sounds and murmurs, respiratory rate and effort; **pre- and post-ductal oxygen saturations** (right hand = pre-ductal; either foot = post-ductal) — normal is ≥96% with **no more than a 2% difference** between the two; a larger gradient raises concern for a duct-dependent congenital cardiac lesion.
- **Abdomen:** shape (a scaphoid/concave abdomen can suggest a diaphragmatic hernia with abdominal contents displaced into the chest), umbilical stump (discharge, infection, periumbilical hernia), palpate for masses/organomegaly.
- **Genitalia and anus:** inspect for ambiguous genitalia, undescended testes; **confirm anal patency**.
- **Hips — Barlow and Ortolani manoeuvres**, screening for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH): performed with the baby supine, hips and knees flexed to 90°. **Barlow's manoeuvre** (provocative — attempts to dislocate a reducible hip): adduct the hip while applying gentle posterior pressure through the thigh; a palpable "clunk" as the femoral head moves out of the acetabulum is a positive test. **Ortolani's manoeuvre** (reductive — confirms an already-dislocated hip can be relocated): abduct the hip while applying gentle anterior pressure; a palpable "clunk" as the femoral head relocates into the acetabulum is positive. Both should only be performed on a settled, quiet baby, and each hip is examined individually. A **Galeazzi test** (comparing knee height with hips/knees flexed, feet flat on the bed) screens for a leg-length discrepancy suggesting unilateral DDH.
- **Back and spine:** inspect for scoliosis, midline skin abnormalities (hair tufts, sacral dimples, naevi) that can indicate underlying spinal dysraphism; symmetry of buttocks/scapulae.
- **Neurological:** observe behaviour, posture, tone, and spontaneous movements throughout; elicit primitive reflexes — Moro, palmar grasp, rooting/sucking — absent or exaggerated reflexes, or an altered conscious state, are red flags.
- **Growth:** plot weight, length, and head circumference on an age-specific growth chart against any prior measurements.

> [!danger] Urgent findings warranting same-day escalation, not routine follow-up: jaundice within the first 24 hours of life; central cyanosis; a >2% pre-/post-ductal saturation gradient; an enlarged, bulging, or sunken fontanelle; subgaleal haemorrhage; absent or exaggerated reflexes, seizures, or an altered conscious state; a positive Barlow/Ortolani test (routine orthopaedic referral rather than same-day, but still requires action, not reassurance).

**To complete the examination:** explain to the parents the exam is finished and offer to redress the baby; share findings and the reason for any referrals; check for further questions; document findings and any planned investigations/referrals.

## 1.25 Scoliosis Screening (Adam's Forward Bend Test)

`CF-PAIR` **`NEW_Exam_Manoeuvres §0.10 Adam's Test` arrived in Part 4 and was NOT merged into this section.** Both kept in full.

> [!note] Gap-filled — the specific screening technique for scoliosis, distinct from the general GALS spinal inspection above; built here given scoliosis screening is commonly performed as its own dedicated step in paediatric/adolescent examination (and historically in school screening programs), and given [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] Scoliosis covers the disease-level detail this screen feeds into, not repeated here.

**Technique:** with the child standing and bare-chested (privacy maintained, observer as appropriate), ask them to bend forward at the waist with knees straight and arms hanging loosely, as if reaching to touch their toes — observe the back **from behind, at eye level with the child's back**, looking specifically for asymmetry.

**What a positive test looks like:** a **rib hump** (unilateral prominence of the ribs on one side, most visible in the thoracic region) or asymmetry of the paraspinal muscles/flank — this asymmetry becomes visible specifically during forward flexion because the rotational component of a structural scoliosis (not just the lateral curve visible on standing inspection) is what produces the rib hump, making this test more sensitive for structural scoliosis than standing inspection alone.

**Why this test specifically, rather than just standing inspection:** a structural scoliosis involves vertebral rotation as well as lateral curvature — forward flexion accentuates this rotational component into a visible rib hump in a way that simple standing inspection of the back can miss, particularly for milder curves.

**Interpretation:** a positive test (visible asymmetry/rib hump) warrants further assessment — clinical measurement (e.g. a scoliometer, measuring the angle of trunk rotation) and, where indicated, spinal X-ray to quantify the curve (Cobb angle) and guide referral — see [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] Scoliosis for the disease-level Ix/Mx detail this feeds into, not repeated here. A negative test (symmetrical back on forward flexion) is reassuring but doesn't exclude a very mild curve, so ongoing routine review through childhood/adolescence (when scoliosis most commonly develops or progresses, particularly through the growth spurt) remains appropriate regardless of a single negative screen.

## 1.26 Trendelenburg's Sign and Gait

`CF-PAIR` **`NEW_Exam_Manoeuvres §0.9 Trendelenburg Test` arrived in Part 4 and was NOT merged into this section.** Both kept in full.

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Trendelenburg's gait," Low yield) — genuinely absent as a dedicated entry despite appearing in passing within `11_07a_Ortho_-_Dermatomes_and_Myotomes_Reference` (**now in Part 2 of this file**) as a superior gluteal nerve test — this entry covers the actual technique, positive finding, and broader differential the sign points to.

**What it tests:** the integrity of the hip abductor mechanism on the **standing** leg — primarily gluteus medius and minimus function, innervated by the superior gluteal nerve — during single-leg stance, which is the functional demand placed on hip abductors with every step of normal gait.

**Technique (Trendelenburg's test):** ask the patient to stand on one leg (the leg being tested) while lifting the other foot off the ground — observe the position of the pelvis, specifically the contralateral (unsupported) side.

**Normal (negative) result:** the pelvis on the unsupported side stays level or rises slightly, given normal hip abductors on the standing leg hold the pelvis level against gravity.

**Positive result:** the pelvis on the **unsupported (contralateral) side drops** — this is a genuinely easy point of confusion worth being explicit about: **a positive test means weak abductors on the standing leg, and the finding is observed on the opposite side to the affected hip** — i.e. standing on the *right* leg with the *left* side of the pelvis dropping indicates *right* hip abductor weakness.

**Trendelenburg gait:** the gait pattern produced by this same underlying weakness during walking — the patient's trunk lurches or leans **towards** the affected (weak) side with each step on that leg, an adaptive strategy that shifts the body's centre of gravity over the weak hip and reduces the abductor force needed to keep the pelvis level, at the cost of the visible waddling/lurching gait pattern this produces. Bilateral hip abductor weakness produces a bilateral, more pronounced waddling gait (both sides lurching alternately).

**Differential for a positive Trendelenburg sign/gait — genuinely broad, spanning several categories rather than one specific diagnosis:**
- **Neurological (superior gluteal nerve palsy):** iatrogenic injury (e.g. during hip surgery, given the nerve's proximity to the surgical approach), or other causes of the nerve lesion — see `11_07a_Ortho_-_Dermatomes_and_Myotomes_Reference` (**now in Part 2 of this file**) for the myotome/nerve reference this connects to, not repeated here.
- **Mechanical/orthopaedic hip pathology:** hip osteoarthritis (see [[12_01_Rheum_-_Rheumatoid_Arthritis__Osteoarthritis__Psoriatic_Arthritis]] Osteoarthritis (OA)), developmental dysplasia of the hip, Perthes' disease, slipped capital femoral epiphysis (see [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]] for all three, not repeated here) — any structural hip abnormality that mechanically disadvantages the abductor lever arm can produce the same clinical sign as a genuine nerve palsy.
- **Muscular:** muscular dystrophy (proximal muscle weakness, see [[15_19b_Paeds_-_Cerebral_Palsy_and_Muscular_Dystrophies]] Muscular dystrophies) or other causes of proximal myopathy affecting the hip abductors directly.

**Why this matters clinically:** a positive Trendelenburg sign tells you *that* there's hip abductor dysfunction on the standing side, not *why* — the broad differential above means the finding should prompt further, targeted assessment (hip examination, neurological examination, CK if myopathy is suspected) rather than being treated as diagnostic of any single condition on its own.

## 1.27 Leg and Skin Ulcers — Differential and Approach

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Ulceration (specify site)," "Leg ulcers," Medium yield) — a classic OSCE differential (distinguishing ulcer types by examination) genuinely absent despite [[01_Cardiovascular]] covering the vascular disease context and [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] noting diabetic wound-healing risk, neither of which brings the ulcer differential itself together in one place.

**Systematic approach to any ulcer — the same structure regardless of type, since it's what distinguishes one type from another:** site, edge (defined/punched-out vs irregular/sloping), size and depth, wound bed appearance, exudate, surrounding skin changes, and pain character (including whether elevation or dependency changes it) — examine **both legs**, not just the affected side, given signs of the underlying vascular disease (varicosities, venous skin changes, absent pulses) are often present bilaterally even when the ulcer itself is unilateral.

**Venous ulcers (the most common type):**
- **Site:** classically over the medial malleolus/gaiter region (lower leg, above the ankle).
- **Appearance:** large, shallow, with irregular borders; wound bed often pink/red (granulating) with moderate-to-heavy exudate given the underlying venous congestion.
- **Surrounding skin:** venous skin changes — haemosiderin (brown) pigmentation, lipodermatosclerosis (firm, woody induration), varicose eczema, atrophie blanche; varicose veins often visible.
- **Pain:** only mildly painful in typical cases — **elevation relieves pain** (reduces venous congestion), a key discriminator from arterial ulcers below.
- **A/P:** chronic venous insufficiency/hypertension — venous valve incompetence leads to venous congestion, tissue hypoxia, and impaired healing.
- **Ix:** ABPI in both legs is essential before starting compression therapy — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Ankle-Brachial Pressure Index (ABPI) for the technique and interpretation, not repeated here, given misapplying compression to a leg with significant arterial insufficiency can cause serious harm.
- **Mx:** compression bandaging/stockings is the mainstay (only once significant arterial disease has been excluded by ABPI); leg elevation when resting; wound care/dressings; treat any underlying varicose veins if contributing.

**Arterial (ischaemic) ulcers:**
- **Site:** distal — toes, tips of toes, heels, or lateral malleolus/pressure points — reflecting the watershed areas of poorest perfusion.
- **Appearance:** small, deep, with well-defined ("punched-out") borders; wound bed often pale/necrotic with minimal exudate given poor perfusion limits both healing and discharge.
- **Surrounding skin:** thin, shiny, hairless, cool to touch — signs of chronic ischaemia; may see ischaemic rubor (dusky-red discolouration when the leg is dependent) — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for the peripheral arterial disease detail this connects to, not repeated here.
- **Pain:** significantly more painful than venous ulcers, classically **worse with elevation and relieved by dependency** (hanging the leg down improves perfusion) — the inverse of the venous pattern above, and the single most useful bedside discriminator between the two.
- **A/P:** critical limb ischaemia from peripheral arterial disease — insufficient arterial blood flow prevents adequate tissue perfusion and healing.
- **Ix:** ABPI (low, supporting arterial insufficiency); duplex ultrasound or angiography to characterise the lesion if intervention is being considered — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for the fuller Ix pathway, not repeated here.
- **Mx:** vascular referral — revascularisation (angioplasty, bypass) addresses the underlying cause; **keep the wound dry** (unlike venous ulcers) to reduce infection risk; **compression is contraindicated** given it would further compromise an already-inadequate arterial supply — this is the single most important safety point distinguishing arterial from venous ulcer management.

**Neuropathic (diabetic) ulcers:**
- **Site:** pressure-bearing areas of the foot, particularly the plantar surface — reflecting repetitive unperceived trauma in an insensate foot.
- **Appearance:** surrounded by callus, wound bed variable (pink/red or, with coexisting ischaemia, brown/black); borders often "punched-out" with undermining.
- **Pain:** classically painless or minimally painful, given the underlying mechanism is peripheral neuropathy causing loss of protective sensation — the patient often doesn't notice the initial trauma at all.
- **A/P:** peripheral neuropathy (loss of protective sensation) plus repetitive pressure/friction causes unrecognised tissue breakdown; often coexists with diabetic peripheral arterial disease, which worsens healing and broadens the differential toward a mixed picture — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] for the diabetic wound-healing risk context, not repeated here.
- **Mx:** offloading pressure (specialised footwear/orthotics) is central to both treatment and prevention; optimise glycaemic control; screen for and manage coexisting arterial disease given how commonly the two coexist; low threshold for assessing for osteomyelitis given how easily a neuropathic ulcer can track to bone without the pain that would otherwise prompt earlier presentation.

**Pressure ulcers:** see [[Examination]] for the general skin/pressure-area assessment this connects to — occur over bony prominences (sacrum, heels, hips) in patients with reduced mobility or sensation, from sustained unrelieved pressure causing local tissue ischaemia; staged by depth (from non-blanching erythema through to full-thickness tissue loss); Mx centres on pressure redistribution (repositioning schedules, pressure-relieving mattresses/cushions) and wound care, with the underlying immobility/sensory deficit addressed as the actual driver rather than treating the wound in isolation.

**Malignant ulcers:** consider when an ulcer fails to heal despite appropriate treatment, has a rolled/everted edge, is friable or bleeds easily, or arises in a longstanding scar/chronic wound (Marjolin's ulcer — squamous cell carcinoma arising in a chronic wound or burn scar) — biopsy any ulcer with atypical features or one that fails to respond to standard management as expected, rather than persisting with the same treatment indefinitely.

**Cross-reference:** see [[01_Cardiovascular]] for peripheral arterial disease and Ankle-Brachial Pressure Index (ABPI), and [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] for the diabetic wound context, not repeated here.

## 1.28 Wound Management — Basic Principles

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Wound and wound management (1°, 2° closure, drainage...)," Medium yield) — a practical skills topic genuinely absent as a standalone entry.

**Primary closure (primary intention):** wound edges are directly apposed (sutures, staples, adhesive strips, or glue) — appropriate for clean wounds with minimal tissue loss and low infection risk, typically within a limited time window from injury (beyond which infection risk from closing a contaminated wound outweighs the cosmetic benefit of primary closure).

**Secondary closure (secondary intention):** the wound is left open and heals by granulation, contraction, and epithelialisation from the wound edges inward — appropriate for contaminated, infected, or high-tissue-loss wounds where primary closure would trap infection or fails to appose viable tissue; takes longer and typically leaves a larger scar than primary closure, but is the safer choice when primary closure isn't appropriate.

**Delayed primary closure (tertiary intention):** a wound is deliberately left open initially (e.g. to allow observation for infection, or wash-out of a contaminated wound) and then surgically closed after several days once infection risk has been judged acceptably low — a middle path used when neither immediate primary closure nor full healing by secondary intention is ideal.

**Drainage:** indicated for a collection (abscess, haematoma, seroma) where simple closure would trap infected or accumulating fluid — incision and drainage (I&D) of an abscess is a core general principle across this project's disease files (see the various abscess entries throughout, e.g. [[03_Gastrointestinal]] for perianal abscess, not repeated here) — the general principle "drain the pus, don't just cover it" applies broadly regardless of the specific anatomical site.

**General wound assessment before deciding management:** mechanism and time since injury, contamination level, tissue viability, and tetanus immunisation status — all directly inform the primary-vs-secondary-vs-delayed-closure decision above, rather than closure technique being chosen independently of these factors.

## 0.1 The Deteriorating Patient — Recognition
`CF-PAIR §1.2` **This block and Part 1 §1.2 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

**D:** Physiological decline in a ward inpatient that precedes arrest or ICU transfer, usually detectable in observations hours beforehand.

**R/Causes:** Post-operative day 1–3, recent ICU step-down, age, frailty, sepsis, opioid or sedative administration, new oxygen requirement, missed or incomplete observation rounds, out-of-hours and weekend admission, language barrier or delirium limiting symptom reporting.

**A/P:** Physiological insult → compensation (tachycardia, tachypnoea, vasoconstriction) maintains normal BP and conscious state → compensatory reserve exhausted → hypotension and altered mentation appear late → tissue hypoperfusion → organ failure → arrest. The clinical point: **blood pressure falls last.** Respiratory rate rises first and is the single most predictive and most commonly unrecorded observation.

**S/Smx:** Rising respiratory rate, new or increasing oxygen requirement, tachycardia, narrowing pulse pressure, falling urine output, new confusion or agitation, "just not right" from nursing staff or family. The last of these carries genuine predictive weight and is a formal escalation criterion in most Australian track-and-trigger systems.

> [!danger] The three that get missed
> **Rising respiratory rate with a normal saturation** — compensation, not reassurance. **New confusion in an older patient** — assume hypoxia, hypoglycaemia or sepsis before assuming delirium. **A single grossly abnormal observation** — one red-zone parameter triggers escalation on its own; you do not need a total score.

> [!info] Track-and-trigger
> Australian hospitals use a colour-zoned observation chart with graded escalation (nursing review → registrar → rapid response/MET). Zone boundaries and the MET call criteria differ between health networks. `UNVERIFIED — do not memorise generic numbers; obtain the observation chart and MET criteria for your own health network, as these are what you will be examined and audited against.`

**Ix:** Full set of vital signs including respiratory rate counted for a full minute and conscious state (*why:* the escalation decision is made on observations, not on tests, and an estimated respiratory rate is the commonest source of a missed deterioration; *what:* rising RR, widening trend across the chart rather than a single reading). Bedside glucose (*why:* hypoglycaemia is instantly reversible and mimics almost every neurological deterioration; *what:* low value demanding immediate treatment). VBG or ABG with lactate (*why:* separates a compensating patient from one already in anaerobic metabolism, and gives CO₂ which pulse oximetry cannot; *what:* rising lactate, or a rising CO₂ signalling ventilatory failure in a patient who looks merely drowsy). FBC, UEC, CRP (*why:* baseline organ function and infective marker to direct the search for a cause; *what:* new renal impairment, leucocytosis or leucopenia). Blood cultures before antibiotics if infection is plausible (*why:* yield falls sharply once antibiotics are given; *what:* organism and sensitivities). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia and ischaemia are silent causes of deterioration in the elderly and diabetic; *what:* new arrhythmia, ischaemic change). Erect CXR (*why:* pneumonia, pulmonary oedema and pneumothorax are the highest-frequency ward causes of a new oxygen requirement; *what:* consolidation, effusion, upper-lobe diversion, absent lung markings).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E assessment at the bedside before any investigation. Airway patency, oxygen titrated to target saturation, IV access, fluids or vasopressor decision per [[A1]] 0.2 if sepsis is suspected, bedside glucose, and escalation according to local criteria. Escalate *while* assessing, not after — the call is not a failure of your own assessment. Document the trigger, the time, who was called, and the response.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Directed at the identified cause. The parallel task is a resuscitation-status and goals-of-care conversation if none is documented: deterioration is the point at which an undocumented ceiling of care causes harm in both directions. See [[A10]] Capacity Concern and Treatment Refusal.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Post-event review, incident documentation, and where relevant an ICU outreach follow-up. Structured handover using ISBAR at every transition of care.

## 0.1 The A–E Approach
`CF-PAIR §1.1` **This block and Part 1 §1.1 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

**D:** A sequential assessment framework that treats life threats in the order they kill, and that is restarted from the top after every intervention and every deterioration.

**A/P:** The ordering is physiological, not arbitrary — an obstructed airway kills in minutes, inadequate breathing in slightly longer, circulatory failure over longer again. Assessing in this order guarantees the fastest-killing problem is addressed first, and **restarting from A after each intervention** catches the deterioration your treatment caused.

> [!tip] The sequence
> **A — Airway:** patent, threatened or obstructed? Talking normally means patent. Stridor, gurgling, snoring or silence with effort mean it is not.
> **B — Breathing:** rate, effort, saturation, air entry, percussion, trachea. Give oxygen.
> **C — Circulation:** heart rate, blood pressure, capillary refill, JVP, peripheral temperature. IV access, fluids, bleeding control.
> **D — Disability:** conscious level, pupils, **glucose**, focal deficit.
> **E — Exposure:** expose fully, look at the back, temperature, rashes, wounds, drains, lines.

> [!info] The F and G vary by context
> The extension to **ABCDEFG** is not standardised. In paediatric teaching it usually adds **Fluids and Glucose**, captured by the mnemonic **"DEFG — Don't Ever Forget Glucose"**. Other sources use F for Family or Fluids and G for Glucose or Goals of care. `UNVERIFIED — confirm which expansion your curriculum and examiners use; do not assume the one you learned is universal.`

> [!danger] Three rules that get broken
> **Do not move past a letter until it is addressed.** A cannula is not more urgent than an obstructed airway, however uncomfortable the pause feels.
> **Restart at A after every intervention and every change.** Deterioration after treatment is common and the framework only works if it loops.
> **Call for help at the point you recognise the problem, not after you have finished assessing it.** Both can happen at once.

**Ix:** Full vital signs with a manually counted respiratory rate (*why:* respiratory rate rises first and is the most predictive and most frequently estimated observation; *what:* rising trend across the chart). Bedside glucose (*why:* the D of the sequence, instantly reversible, and mimics every neurological catastrophe; *what:* hypoglycaemia). VBG with lactate (*why:* quantifies perfusion and ventilation faster than any other single test; *what:* acidosis, raised lactate, raised CO₂). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia and ischaemia as silent causes; *what:* rhythm, ischaemic change). Directed further investigation once the sequence identifies where the problem sits.

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Treat each abnormality as it is found, before moving on. Escalate while assessing.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Directed at the cause identified. Cross-refer [[A1]] 0.1 The Deteriorating Patient.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Documentation, structured handover with ISBAR, and review of whether the deterioration was preventable.

**D:** the systematic clinical evaluation of a patient before anaesthesia/surgery, aiming to identify factors that increase perioperative risk, optimise modifiable conditions where possible, and plan the anaesthetic approach accordingly.

**History:**
- **Presenting surgical condition** and planned procedure — informs anaesthetic technique, positioning, expected blood loss/fluid shifts, and duration.
- **Past anaesthetic history** — previous anaesthetics and any complications (difficult intubation, PONV, awareness, prolonged recovery), family history of anaesthetic complications (specifically screening for malignant hyperthermia susceptibility — see General Anaesthesia above for the fuller detail on this emergency, not repeated here — and pseudocholinesterase deficiency, relevant to suxamethonium metabolism).
- **Medical comorbidities** — cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, hepatic, and endocrine disease all carry specific perioperative implications; diabetes specifically has a comprehensive dedicated management pathway — see [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Perioperative Diabetes Management for the full AU-verified detail (the ADS-ANZCA national guideline), not repeated here.
- **Medications** — full medication reconciliation, since several classes need specific perioperative adjustment (anticoagulants, antiplatelets, diabetes medications, and others) — see General Anaesthesia above for the AU-verified DOAC/warfarin perioperative timing and bridging detail, not repeated here.
- **Allergies** — drug allergies specifically, and any history of anaphylaxis under a previous anaesthetic (raising suspicion for a specific anaesthetic-related allergen, e.g. neuromuscular blocking agents, latex, or chlorhexidine).
- **Fasting status** — see the "Sip Til Send" ANZCA PG07 (2024) guidance already established elsewhere in this source, not repeated here.
- **Airway history** — previous difficult intubation, obstructive sleep apnoea, limited neck mobility, dental issues — directly informs airway management planning.
- **Social history** — smoking (increases perioperative respiratory complication risk; cessation even shortly before surgery has some benefit), alcohol use (relevant to withdrawal risk and anaesthetic drug interactions), and functional capacity (a practical, low-cost way to estimate cardiovascular reserve — the ability to climb ≥2 flights of stairs or achieve ≥4 METs of exertion without symptoms is a widely used threshold suggesting adequate reserve for most non-cardiac surgery without further cardiac work-up).

**Examination:** general examination with particular attention to the airway (see Airway Adjuncts above for the specific airway assessment features relevant to predicting a difficult airway, not repeated here), cardiovascular and respiratory systems, and any specific findings relevant to the planned procedure or identified comorbidities.

> [!info] ASA Physical Status Classification — a widely used, simple system summarising overall perioperative risk, referenced throughout this source
> **ASA I:** normal healthy patient | **ASA II:** mild systemic disease (e.g. well-controlled hypertension, smoker) | **ASA III:** severe systemic disease, not life-threatening (e.g. poorly controlled diabetes, stable angina) | **ASA IV:** severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life (e.g. recent MI, severe heart failure) | **ASA V:** moribund, not expected to survive without the operation | **ASA VI:** declared brain-dead, organs being harvested. An "E" suffix denotes emergency surgery, which independently increases risk regardless of the underlying ASA class.

**Ix:** investigations should be **targeted to the history/examination findings and the planned procedure, not ordered routinely/reflexively** — a genuinely important modern principle, since indiscriminate pre-operative testing in low-risk patients undergoing low-risk procedures doesn't improve outcomes and can generate false-positive findings that delay surgery unnecessarily. Common targeted investigations include:
- **FBC** — if anaemia is suspected, significant blood loss is anticipated, or as a baseline before major surgery.
- **U&Es** — if renal impairment is known/suspected, in patients on medications affecting electrolytes (diuretics, ACEI/ARB), or before major surgery with expected significant fluid shifts.
- **Coagulation studies** — if a bleeding history is present, the patient is anticoagulated, or significant blood loss is anticipated.
- **Group & hold/crossmatch** — see Group & Hold / Crossmatch below for the fuller detail, not repeated here.
- **ECG** — for patients with known cardiac disease, significant cardiovascular risk factors, or as a baseline before major surgery, particularly in older patients.
- **HbA1c/glucose** — see the diabetes screening detail in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Perioperative Diabetes Management, not repeated here.
- **Pregnancy test** — for people of childbearing potential, given the implications for anaesthetic drug choice and timing.
- **Further cardiac/respiratory work-up** (e.g. echocardiogram, spirometry, cardiology/respiratory referral) — reserved for patients with reduced functional capacity, significant symptoms, or high-risk surgery, rather than routine screening.

**Optimisation:** the pre-operative assessment is also the opportunity to optimise modifiable risk factors before surgery where time allows — e.g. improving glycaemic control in poorly controlled diabetes (see the ADS-ANZCA guideline's specific HbA1c threshold and delay-vs-proceed framework in [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]], not repeated here), treating active infection, optimising heart failure or COPD control, correcting significant anaemia, and smoking cessation advice — all aimed at reducing perioperative risk rather than simply documenting it.

**Consent:** discussing the procedure, anaesthetic technique, and material risks with the patient is a core part of this process — see [[Clinical-Process-EBM-Consent-Capacity]] for the general principles of informed consent, not repeated here.

## 0.1 Preoperative Assessment
`CF-PAIR §1.12` **This block and Part 1 §1.12 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] The purpose is four things, and knowing them shapes the assessment
> **1. Identify and OPTIMISE modifiable risk** before the operation.
> **2. Plan the anaesthetic** — airway, technique, monitoring.
> **3. Inform CONSENT** with a realistic discussion of individual risk.
> **4. Plan the POSTOPERATIVE pathway** — level of care, analgesia, disposition.
> **The assessment that changes nothing was not worth doing** — which is the principle governing investigation below.

> [!info] Risk stratification tools
> **· ASA physical status classification (I–V, with E for emergency)** — a global descriptor of systemic disease, widely used and simple. `UNVERIFIED — the class definitions.`
> **· FUNCTIONAL CAPACITY in metabolic equivalents (METs).** **The practical question: "can you climb two flights of stairs without stopping?"** A patient who can generally has adequate reserve; one who cannot, or whose activity is limited by symptoms rather than by joints, warrants closer assessment. `UNVERIFIED — the MET threshold.`
> **· Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)** — combines ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, diabetes on insulin, renal impairment and high-risk surgery. `UNVERIFIED — components and interpretation.`
> **· FRAILTY — a better predictor of postoperative outcome than chronological age**, and increasingly used to inform whether to operate at all. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.2.

> [!danger] Do not stop dual antiplatelet therapy after a recent coronary stent
> **Premature cessation of dual antiplatelet therapy after coronary stenting causes STENT THROMBOSIS, which carries very high mortality.**
> **There is a mandatory minimum duration of DAPT after stenting, which differs by stent type and indication, and elective surgery should be DEFERRED until it is complete.**
> **If surgery cannot wait, the decision requires explicit discussion with cardiology and the surgical and anaesthetic teams** — it is never a decision for a junior doctor or a pre-admission clinic to make unilaterally.
> **Similarly, elective surgery is deferred for a defined period after myocardial infarction.** `UNVERIFIED — the DAPT durations by stent type, and the interval after infarction.`

> [!warning] Preoperative anaemia should be found and treated weeks in advance
> **Anaemia before elective surgery is common, is an INDEPENDENT risk factor for morbidity, mortality and transfusion, and is frequently ignored until the day.**
> **Patient Blood Management principles: identify anaemia EARLY (weeks before), determine the cause, and treat it — most often with INTRAVENOUS IRON for iron deficiency, which works within weeks.**
> **Australia has a National Blood Authority Patient Blood Management framework**, and this is one of the clearest examples of a preoperative intervention that measurably improves outcomes.
> **The practical failure: the haemoglobin is checked on admission the day before surgery, when it is too late to do anything but transfuse.** Cross-refer [[A9]] and [[J1]]. `UNVERIFIED — the PBM thresholds and iron regimens.`

> [!tip] The other optimisable factors
> **· SMOKING — cessation reduces respiratory and wound complications, and the benefit increases with the duration of abstinence. Even short-term cessation before surgery helps, and the preoperative period is a highly effective teachable moment** — patients are more likely to succeed at quitting around surgery than at almost any other time. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended cessation interval.`
> **· ALCOHOL — reduction reduces complications, and withdrawal must be anticipated in dependent patients.**
> **· DIABETES — glycaemic optimisation, and identification of unrecognised diabetes.**
> **· OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNOEA — screen with STOP-BANG; it predicts postoperative respiratory complications and changes monitoring.** Cross-refer [[RESP-X]] 0.5.
> **· Nutrition, exercise ("prehabilitation"), and management of heart failure, COPD and asthma.**

> [!danger] Do not order routine preoperative investigations in well patients
> **Routine blood tests, ECGs and chest radiographs in asymptomatic patients undergoing low-risk surgery do not improve outcomes.** They generate incidental abnormalities that cause delay, further testing, and anxiety without benefit.
> **Investigations should be targeted by the PATIENT (comorbidity, medications, age) and the PROCEDURE (magnitude, expected blood loss).**
> **What is usually justified: FBC where anaemia or bleeding is plausible or blood loss expected · UEC in renal disease, diuretics, ACE inhibitors or major surgery · glucose or HbA1c in diabetes · ECG in cardiac disease or above an age threshold for major surgery · group and hold or crossmatch where transfusion is possible · pregnancy test where relevant.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific indications and age thresholds.`

**Ix:** As above — **targeted rather than routine.** **Group and hold or crossmatch** (*why:* preparation for transfusion, and antibody screening takes time; *what:* group and antibodies). **Echocardiography only where it will change management** — new or worsening heart failure, an undiagnosed murmur with symptoms, or suspected severe valvular disease (*why:* **severe aortic stenosis substantially changes anaesthetic conduct and risk**, whereas an echo that confirms known stable disease changes nothing; *what:* valve disease, ventricular function). Respiratory function tests in significant lung disease undergoing thoracic or major surgery (*why:* predicts postoperative respiratory failure; *what:* FEV1, DLCO). **Cervical spine imaging in rheumatoid arthritis** (*why:* atlantoaxial subluxation risks cord injury on intubation; *what:* instability — cross-refer [[L2]] 0.2).

## 0.2 Airway Assessment and Anaesthetic Technique
`CF-PAIR §1.12` **This block and Part 1 §1.12 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!warning] Predictors of a difficult airway
> **· Previous difficult intubation — the single most useful predictor, and the reason to ASK and to CHECK old anaesthetic records.**
> **· Mallampati class, limited MOUTH OPENING, short THYROMENTAL distance, reduced NECK EXTENSION, prominent incisors, receding mandible.**
> **· Obesity, particularly with a large neck circumference · obstructive sleep apnoea · pregnancy · beard.**
> **· Pathology: head and neck cancer, previous neck RADIOTHERAPY or surgery, burns contractures, airway tumours, and infection.**
> **· RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS — atlantoaxial instability.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.2.
> **· ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS — a fixed flexed cervical spine, which makes both intubation and neuraxial access difficult.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.3.
> **DOCUMENT a difficult airway prominently in the record AND give the patient written documentation to carry** — a difficult airway discovered a second time, unwarned, is a preventable catastrophe.

> [!tip] Regional anaesthesia and analgesia — the advantages worth knowing
> **Reduced opioid requirement, superior analgesia, less postoperative nausea and vomiting, earlier mobilisation, and — importantly in older patients — LESS DELIRIUM.**
> **The fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block in hip fracture is a specific, high-value example** that reduces opioid use and delirium and is under-used. Cross-refer [[L7]] 0.4.

> [!danger] New back pain with neurological deficit after neuraxial anaesthesia is an emergency
> **EPIDURAL HAEMATOMA or ABSCESS causes permanent paraplegia if not decompressed quickly.**
> **The warning features: new or worsening back pain · a motor or sensory block that is DENSER, HIGHER or LASTING LONGER than expected · a block that recurs after wearing off · new bladder dysfunction · and, with abscess, fever and raised inflammatory markers.**
> **A patient whose epidural block should have worn off and has not, or who develops new leg weakness, needs URGENT MRI and urgent neurosurgical discussion — not observation overnight.**
> **This is why anticoagulant timing around neuraxial procedures is strictly protocolised**, and why those intervals must be checked rather than estimated. Cross-refer [[A9]] 0.4 and [[L4]] 0.2. `UNVERIFIED — all anticoagulant timing intervals for neuraxial procedures.`

> [!warning] Post-dural puncture headache
> **A POSTURAL headache — markedly worse on sitting or standing, relieved by lying flat** — following dural puncture, typically appearing within a day or two. Often frontal or occipital, sometimes with neck stiffness, photophobia, tinnitus or diplopia.
> **The postural nature is the diagnostic feature**, and it distinguishes it from other postoperative headaches.
> **Managed conservatively at first, with an EPIDURAL BLOOD PATCH for persistent or severe symptoms, which is often dramatically effective.** Cross-refer [[D1]] 0.4.

> [!danger] Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity — know that lipid emulsion exists and where it is
> **Occurs after inadvertent intravascular injection or excessive dose, particularly with large-volume blocks.**
> **The progression: perioral tingling, TINNITUS, a METALLIC TASTE, visual disturbance, agitation and confusion → SEIZURES → cardiovascular collapse and arrhythmia refractory to standard resuscitation.**
> **Management: STOP the injection · call for help · A–B–C with 100% oxygen · control seizures · and give INTRAVENOUS LIPID EMULSION ("Intralipid"), which is the specific antidote.**
> **Prolonged resuscitation may be required, and recovery is possible after extended arrest — do not stop early.**
> **Every area where regional blocks are performed should have a LAST kit and a copy of the protocol. Know where yours is.** `UNVERIFIED — the lipid emulsion regimen; follow the ANZCA/ASRA protocol on the wall.`

> [!tip] The anaesthetic drug problems worth recognising
> **· MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA — triggered by volatile agents and suxamethonium. A rising END-TIDAL CO₂ is the earliest sign, before temperature rises. Dantrolene is the antidote, and the family requires screening.** Cross-refer [[A6]] 0.3.
> **· SUXAMETHONIUM — causes a transient rise in potassium, which becomes DANGEROUS HYPERKALAEMIA in burns beyond about 24–48 hours, denervation injury, spinal cord injury, and prolonged immobility, through upregulation of acetylcholine receptors.** **Also: suxamethonium apnoea in pseudocholinesterase deficiency — prolonged paralysis requiring continued ventilation, which is inherited and should be documented and communicated to the family.**
> **· Residual neuromuscular blockade — a cause of postoperative respiratory compromise and aspiration**, and a reason for careful monitoring in recovery.
> **· AWARENESS under anaesthesia — rare, deeply distressing, and requires acknowledgement, explanation and psychological follow-up rather than dismissal.**

## Skin lesion morphology — reference terms
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!info] Flat, non-palpable changes in skin colour
> - **Macule:** flat, non-palpable change in skin colour, <0.5cm diameter. Freckles are pigmented macules.
> - **Patch:** flat, non-palpable change in skin colour, >0.5cm diameter.

> [!info] Elevation due to fluid in a cavity
> - **Vesicle/Blister:** fluid within the upper layers of skin, <0.5cm diameter.
> - **Bulla:** large, fluid-filled lesion below the epidermis, >1.0cm diameter.
> - **Pustule:** collection of pus in the subcutis, >0.5cm diameter.

> [!info] Elevation due to solid masses
> - **Papule:** raised area, <0.5cm diameter.
> - **Plaque:** raised area, >2cm diameter.
> - **Nodule:** mass/lump, >0.5cm diameter.
> - **Wheal:** dermal oedema.

> [!info] Loss of skin
> - **Erosion:** partial epidermal loss, heals without scars.
> - **Ulcer:** full-thickness skin loss.
> - **Fissure:** linear crack.
> - **Atrophy:** thinning of the epidermis.

> [!info] Vascular changes
> - **Telangiectasia:** easily visible superficial blood vessels.
> - **Spider naevus:** single telangiectatic arteriole in the skin.
> - **Purpura:** rash caused by blood in the skin, often multiple petechiae.
> - **Petechiae:** micro-haemorrhage, 1–2mm diameter.

## 0.1 Describing a Rash
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] The vocabulary — because an accurate description gets you a diagnosis over the phone
> **PRIMARY LESIONS:**
> **· MACULE — flat, small, colour change only. PATCH — flat, larger.**
> **· PAPULE — raised, small. PLAQUE — raised, larger, with a flat top. NODULE — raised and DEEPER.**
> **· VESICLE — fluid-filled, small. BULLA — fluid-filled, larger. PUSTULE — pus-filled.**
> **· WHEAL — a transient, oedematous, itchy swelling that moves and resolves within hours (the defining feature of urticaria).**
> **· PURPURA — NON-BLANCHING. PETECHIAE are small; ECCHYMOSES are large.**
> **SECONDARY CHANGES: scale · crust · excoriation · LICHENIFICATION (thickened skin with accentuated markings, from chronic rubbing) · EROSION (loss of epidermis only — heals WITHOUT scarring) · ULCER (extends into dermis — heals WITH a scar) · fissure · atrophy.**
> **CONFIGURATION: annular · TARGETOID · linear · GROUPED/herpetiform · reticulate (net-like) · serpiginous · DERMATOMAL.**
> **DISTRIBUTION: flexural · extensor · PHOTODISTRIBUTED (face, V of neck, dorsal hands, sparing under the chin and behind the ears — the shadowed sites are the clue) · acral · truncal · symmetrical or asymmetrical.**

> [!danger] Two things to do with every rash
> **1. PRESS ON IT. BLANCHING VERSUS NON-BLANCHING is the single most important bedside test in dermatology**, and it separates a benign exanthem from purpura requiring urgent assessment. Use a glass. Cross-refer [[G3]].
> **2. EXAMINE THE WHOLE SKIN, UNDRESSED — plus the MUCOSAE (mouth, eyes, genitals), the NAILS, the SCALP, and the PALMS AND SOLES.**
> **Mucosal involvement changes the diagnosis completely** — it is what distinguishes a straightforward drug rash from Stevens-Johnson syndrome. **A rash examined through a gap in a gown is a rash half seen.**

> [!danger] Erythema is under-recognised in darker skin — and it causes delayed diagnosis
> **Erythema in richly pigmented skin may appear VIOLACEOUS, BROWN, GREY or simply darker rather than red**, and the classic textbook descriptions ("bright red", "salmon pink") are written from white skin.
> **The consequences are documented and serious: DELAYED DIAGNOSIS OF CELLULITIS · under-recognition of erythroderma · missed CYANOSIS and missed pallor · and jaundice detected late.**
> **What to do instead: PALPATE — warmth, induration, tenderness and swelling do not depend on colour · COMPARE with the patient's own unaffected skin, ideally the contralateral side · use good natural light · examine the mucosae, conjunctivae, palms and soles where colour change is easier to appreciate · and ASK the patient what has changed, since they know their own skin.**
> **Also: post-inflammatory HYPER- and HYPOpigmentation are more prominent and more persistent in darker skin, and are a significant cause of distress that should be acknowledged rather than dismissed as cosmetic.**
> **Photograph lesions with consent** — for monitoring, for referral, and because dermatology advice by phone is far better with an image.

## 0.1 Assessing a Pigmented Lesion
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!danger] The single most important feature is CHANGE
> **Australia has the highest incidence of melanoma in the world, and melanoma is a leading cause of cancer death in young Australian adults** — which sets the threshold for how seriously a changing lesion should be taken.
> **ABCDE: ASYMMETRY · BORDER irregularity · COLOUR variation within the lesion · DIAMETER (conventionally above about 6 mm) · EVOLVING.**
> **"E" IS THE MOST IMPORTANT. A lesion that is CHANGING — in size, shape, colour, elevation, or by bleeding, itching or ulcerating — warrants assessment regardless of how it scores on the other letters.**
> **A patient who says "this one has changed" is providing better information than any single morphological feature.**

> [!tip] The ugly duckling sign — often more useful than ABCDE
> **Most people's naevi RESEMBLE EACH OTHER — they share a family likeness.**
> **THE UGLY DUCKLING is the lesion that looks DIFFERENT from the patient's others.**
> **This works because it uses the patient as their own control, and it detects the lesion that ABCDE misses in someone with many atypical naevi (where every lesion scores badly on ABCDE) or someone with very few (where a small early melanoma may not yet meet the criteria).**
> **Stand back and look at the whole back at once, rather than examining lesions one at a time through a lens.**

> [!warning] The history that stratifies risk
> **· PERSONAL history of melanoma or keratinocyte cancer — the strongest single risk factor for another.**
> **· FAMILY history of melanoma, particularly in a first-degree relative or at a young age.**
> **· SKIN TYPE — fair skin, red or blond hair, blue eyes, freckling, poor tanning.**
> **· SUN EXPOSURE HISTORY, and specifically the number of episodes of BLISTERING SUNBURN, particularly in CHILDHOOD.** Intermittent intense exposure relates to melanoma; cumulative chronic exposure relates to SCC.
> **· Number of naevi, and the presence of ATYPICAL naevi.**
> **· IMMUNOSUPPRESSION — see 0.3, where the transplant population is discussed. This dramatically changes risk.**
> **· Previous solaria use, outdoor occupation, and prior phototherapy.**

> [!danger] Examine the whole skin, and if in doubt, excise
> **A "check this mole" consultation should include a FULL SKIN EXAMINATION — including the SCALP (part the hair), BEHIND THE EARS, BETWEEN THE TOES, the SOLES, the NAILS, and the genital and perianal skin where appropriate and consented.**
> **Melanomas occur at sites that never see the sun, and acral and subungual melanomas are diagnosed late precisely because nobody looks.**
> **AND THE OPERATING RULE: IF YOU ARE NOT SURE, EXCISE IT OR REFER IT. DO NOT "WATCH" A SUSPICIOUS LESION.**
> **"Review in three months" applied to a lesion that concerns you is how thin, curable melanomas become thick ones — and the patient frequently does not return.**
> **Serial photography has a role for monitoring multiple naevi in high-risk patients under specialist follow-up; it is not a substitute for excising a lesion you are worried about.**

## 0.5 Nails
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] Nail signs of systemic disease
> **· CLUBBING — cross-refer [[A4]] 0.1.**
> **· KOILONYCHIA (spoon-shaped) — IRON DEFICIENCY.**
> **· BEAU LINES — transverse grooves across all nails, caused by temporary arrest of nail growth during a severe systemic illness.** **They are useful because they DATE THE INSULT: knowing that nails grow at a roughly predictable rate, the distance from the cuticle indicates how long ago the illness occurred.**
> **· LEUKONYCHIA (white nails) — hypoalbuminaemia, from liver disease or nephrotic syndrome.**
> **· TERRY NAILS (proximal white nail with a distal band) — cirrhosis. HALF-AND-HALF nails — chronic kidney disease.**
> **· SPLINTER HAEMORRHAGES — TRAUMA is by far the commonest cause, but they are a recognised sign of INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS and vasculitis.** Cross-refer [[B5]] 0.1.
> **· PERIUNGUAL TELANGIECTASIA AND ABNORMAL NAILFOLD CAPILLARIES — a genuinely valuable bedside sign of CONNECTIVE TISSUE DISEASE, particularly systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis.** **Look at the nailfolds with a dermatoscope or even an ophthalmoscope in anyone with Raynaud phenomenon — dilated, dropped-out capillaries indicate a secondary rather than primary Raynaud and change the workup entirely.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4 and [[L3]] 0.6.
> **· Nail PITTING, ONYCHOLYSIS and oil-drop change — PSORIASIS.** Cross-refer [[G4]] 0.2.
> **· Yellow nail syndrome — with lymphoedema and pleural effusions.**

> [!danger] Confirm onychomycosis before treating it
> **A substantial proportion of dystrophic nails that look fungal are NOT — the differential includes PSORIASIS, TRAUMA (particularly the great toenails in runners and in people with foot deformity), lichen planus, and — critically — SUBUNGUAL MELANOMA.**
> **SYSTEMIC ANTIFUNGALS ARE NOT TRIVIAL: they require months of treatment, carry HEPATOTOXICITY, and have significant DRUG INTERACTIONS (terbinafine and the azoles).**
> **TAKE NAIL CLIPPINGS AND SUBUNGUAL DEBRIS FOR MICROSCOPY AND CULTURE BEFORE PRESCRIBING** — and take an adequate sample from the affected proximal part, not just a snip of the distal edge.
> **Treating a psoriatic or traumatic nail with months of terbinafine achieves nothing and risks harm.**
> **PARONYCHIA: ACUTE — usually staphylococcal, painful, red, may need incision and drainage. CHRONIC — associated with WET WORK and irritant exposure, with loss of the cuticle and secondary candida; the treatment is AVOIDING MOISTURE AND IRRITANTS plus a topical steroid, and antifungals alone do not work.**

## 0.1 Triple Assessment
`CF-PAIR §1.17` **This block and Part 1 §1.17 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!danger] Triple assessment means all three components, and they must be CONCORDANT
> **1. CLINICAL assessment — history and examination.**
> **2. IMAGING — ultrasound and/or mammography, chosen by age.**
> **3. PATHOLOGY — core biopsy.**
> **The critical principle is CONCORDANCE. If any one component is suspicious and the others are not, the case is NOT closed.**
> **A benign biopsy result in a clinically or radiologically suspicious lump means the biopsy may have missed the lesion — it does not mean the lump is benign.** **Escalate: repeat sampling, excision biopsy, or multidisciplinary discussion.**
> **Discordance is the mechanism by which cancers are missed after a "normal" biopsy**, and recognising it is the single most important safety concept in breast assessment.

> [!tip] Imaging is chosen by age, because breast density changes
> **· Younger women (broadly under 35–40) — ULTRASOUND first.** Dense glandular tissue makes mammography insensitive, and the yield of malignancy is low.
> **· Older women — MAMMOGRAPHY plus ULTRASOUND.**
> **· MRI — for high-risk screening (BRCA carriers, chest irradiation), assessing disease extent particularly in LOBULAR carcinoma, and for implant assessment.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the age thresholds.`
> **CORE BIOPSY is preferred over fine needle aspiration**, because it provides tissue architecture (distinguishing invasive from in situ disease), grade, and **receptor status (ER, PR, HER2)** — all of which determine treatment. **FNA retains a role for cyst aspiration and for lymph node sampling.**

> [!warning] Take the family history properly — it changes the pathway
> **The features that suggest a hereditary predisposition:**
> **· Multiple affected relatives on the SAME side of the family.**
> **· YOUNG age at diagnosis (particularly under 50).**
> **· BILATERAL breast cancer.**
> **· MALE breast cancer — a strong indicator, particularly of BRCA2.**
> **· OVARIAN cancer in the family, and breast plus ovarian in the same person or family.**
> **· Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.**
> **· Known familial mutation.**
> **These warrant referral to a familial cancer service**, because the implications extend to surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, treatment choice (PARP inhibitors) and cascade testing of relatives.

> [!tip] Risk factors — dominated by age and oestrogen exposure
> **· AGE — the dominant risk factor by a wide margin.**
> **· Family history and germline mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2 and others).**
> **· Lifetime oestrogen exposure — early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, later first pregnancy, and menopausal hormone therapy.**
> **· Previous breast biopsy showing ATYPIA or lobular carcinoma in situ.**
> **· Previous CHEST RADIOTHERAPY, particularly for lymphoma in adolescence — these women need early, intensive surveillance and it is frequently overlooked.**
> **· Dense breast tissue · ALCOHOL · obesity after menopause · physical inactivity.**
> **Protective: parity, BREASTFEEDING, and physical activity.**

## 0.2 Drugs for Eye Examinations and Procedures
`CF-PAIR §1.18` **This block and Part 1 §1.18 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

- **Mydriatics and cycloplegics:** **tropicamide** (short-acting mydriatic, the standard for fundoscopy), **cyclopentolate** (longer, cycloplegic — used for refraction in children and in uveitis to relieve ciliary spasm), **atropine** (very long-acting — used therapeutically in uveitis and amblyopia, not for examination), **phenylephrine** (a sympathomimetic mydriatic without cycloplegia, often combined with tropicamide).
- **Topical anaesthetics:** **oxybuprocaine, proxymetacaine, tetracaine** — for tonometry, foreign body removal, and to allow examination of a photophobic eye (see `NEW_Drugs_02_Anaesthetics.md` 0.1.4).
- **Diagnostic dyes:** **fluorescein** — the essential bedside dye, revealing epithelial defects, abrasions, ulcers and the **dendritic ulcer of herpes simplex keratitis** under blue light, and used in **Seidel's test** for aqueous leak after penetrating injury; **lissamine green** and **rose bengal** for ocular surface staining. **Intravenous fluorescein angiography** is a separate retinal investigation, and **anaphylaxis is a recognised risk** requiring resuscitation facilities.
- **Miotics:** **pilocarpine** — used to reverse mydriasis and in acute angle-closure glaucoma.
- **Intraoperative and periprocedural agents:** viscoelastics, intracameral antibiotics and mydriatics, and **anti-VEGF agents** (see 0.5).

> [!danger] **NEVER give a topical anaesthetic to a patient to take home** — repeated use causes corneal epithelial toxicity, delayed healing, ulceration and permanent visual loss, and it removes the pain that would bring them back. Single clinical use only.

> [!danger] **Dilating an eye: the real risks and the false one.**
> - **Precipitating acute angle-closure glaucoma is uncommon but real** in a predisposed (shallow anterior chamber, hypermetropic, older, Asian) eye. **Warn the patient to return immediately with a painful red eye, haloes, headache or vomiting** — and know that this presentation is a **sight-threatening emergency** needing same-day ophthalmology.
> - **Do not dilate when the pupil is being used for neurological monitoring** — in head injury or reduced conscious state, a pharmacologically fixed dilated pupil destroys the most important clinical sign. **Document clearly on the chart and at the bedside if you dilate such a patient.**
> - **The patient cannot drive** until the mydriasis wears off (hours with tropicamide, up to a day or more with cyclopentolate and much longer with atropine), and should be warned about glare and blurred near vision.
> - **Systemic anticholinergic effects** — flushing, tachycardia, dry mouth and **delirium**, particularly with cyclopentolate and atropine in **children and the elderly**; and **phenylephrine can raise blood pressure**. Per **CLAUDE.md rule 5**, note that **paediatric ophthalmic drop concentrations differ from adult ones** — atropine and cyclopentolate in infants have caused systemic toxicity, and the strength must come from a paediatric source.

## 0.1 Examining the Ear and Assessing Hearing
`CF-PAIR §1.19` **This block and Part 1 §1.19 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] Otoscopy — and the part of the drum everyone forgets
> **· PULL THE PINNA UP AND BACK IN ADULTS, DOWN AND BACK IN INFANTS AND YOUNG CHILDREN** — the infant canal runs in a different direction, and pulling the wrong way obscures the view.
> **· Use the LARGEST speculum that comfortably fits (it gives more light and a wider view), hold the otoscope like a pen, and rest your hand against the patient's head so that a sudden movement moves the scope with them.**
> **· EXAMINE THE UNAFFECTED EAR FIRST** — it establishes what normal looks like in that patient and it is less painful.
> **· Be systematic: the pinna and post-auricular area · the CANAL (wax, debris, oedema, discharge, granulation) · then the DRUM — colour, translucency, the light reflex, position (bulging or retracted), mobility, perforation, and any fluid or bubbles behind it.**
> **AND LOOK AT THE ATTIC — THE PARS FLACCIDA — EVERY TIME.**
> **The pars flaccida sits above the lateral process of the malleus, it is small, it is easy to skip, and it is WHERE CHOLESTEATOMA HIDES.** **A retraction pocket or crust in the attic is the finding that matters, and it is missed by clinicians who look only at the pars tensa.** See 0.3.

> [!danger] Tuning fork tests — and the false negative Rinne
> **Use a 512 Hz fork.**
> **RINNE — compare air conduction (beside the ear) with bone conduction (on the mastoid):**
> **· AC > BC ("Rinne positive") = NORMAL or SENSORINEURAL loss.**
> **· BC > AC ("Rinne negative") = CONDUCTIVE loss in that ear.**
> **WEBER — fork on the vertex or forehead, ask where the sound is heard:**
> **· Lateralises TOWARDS a CONDUCTIVE loss.**
> **· Lateralises AWAY FROM a SENSORINEURAL loss.**
> **THE TRAP — THE FALSE NEGATIVE RINNE: in SEVERE UNILATERAL SENSORINEURAL loss, the bone-conducted sound crosses the skull and is heard by the OPPOSITE, normal cochlea — so the test appears to show BC > AC, mimicking a conductive loss.**
> **THE WEBER RESOLVES IT: in a true conductive loss the Weber lateralises TO the affected ear; in a dead ear with a false negative Rinne it lateralises AWAY from it.**
> **Always do both tests together, and interpret them together.** **This matters most in sudden hearing loss — see 0.5.**

> [!tip] The other assessments, and how to talk to a hearing-impaired patient
> **· WHISPERED VOICE TEST — a reasonable bedside screen: mask the other ear, stand behind the patient at arm's length, and whisper a combination of numbers and letters after a full exhalation.**
> **· PURE TONE AUDIOMETRY with air and bone conduction — the formal test, distinguishing conductive from sensorineural and quantifying the loss.**
> **· TYMPANOMETRY — measures middle ear compliance. A FLAT (type B) trace indicates middle ear fluid or a perforation; a negative-pressure (type C) trace indicates Eustachian tube dysfunction.** `UNVERIFIED — the classification and normal values.`
> **· Always also examine the NOSE, THROAT, NECK for lymphadenopathy, and the CRANIAL NERVES — particularly the facial nerve.**
> **COMMUNICATING WITH A HEARING-IMPAIRED PATIENT — practical and consistently done badly:**
> **· FACE THEM, at their level, with your face in good light so they can lip-read. Do not talk while looking at the notes or the screen, and do not talk from behind or while wearing a mask if it can be avoided.**
> **· SPEAK CLEARLY AND SLIGHTLY MORE SLOWLY — do NOT SHOUT, which distorts speech and looks aggressive.**
> **· REDUCE BACKGROUND NOISE — turn off the television, close the door, move away from the nurses' station.**
> **· CHECK THE HEARING AID IS IN, TURNED ON, AND HAS A WORKING BATTERY.** **Hearing aids left in the locker are a routine and preventable cause of "confusion" in hospitalised older people, and they contribute directly to delirium.** Cross-refer [[D2]] 0.2.
> **· WRITE DOWN key information, and confirm understanding.**

## 0.2 Acute Vestibular Syndrome and the HINTS Examination
`CF-PAIR §1.21` **This block and Part 1 §1.21 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

**D:** Acute-onset **continuous** vertigo lasting more than 24 hours, with nausea and vomiting, nystagmus, head-motion intolerance and gait unsteadiness.

**A/P:** The differential is essentially two conditions with opposite implications: **vestibular neuritis** (benign, self-limiting, from inflammation of the vestibular nerve, often post-viral) and **posterior circulation stroke** — cerebellar or brainstem infarction. **A meaningful proportion of patients presenting with isolated acute vertigo have a stroke**, and they are the group most likely to be discharged home.

> [!danger] HINTS is more sensitive than early MRI for posterior circulation stroke
> **Early diffusion-weighted MRI has a false negative rate in the first 24–48 hours for small posterior fossa infarcts.** A properly performed HINTS examination by a trained examiner outperforms it.
> **This is the single most important fact in this file**, because it inverts the instinct to reassure on the basis of a negative scan.

> [!info] HINTS — the three components
> **Only valid in a patient with CONTINUOUS, ONGOING vertigo and nystagmus at the time of examination.** It is **not** applicable to episodic vertigo, and it is **not** applicable to a currently asymptomatic patient — using it there produces dangerously misleading results.
>
> **1. Head Impulse test** — rapid small-amplitude head turn while the patient fixates on your nose.
> **· ABNORMAL (a corrective catch-up saccade) = PERIPHERAL** — the vestibulo-ocular reflex is broken on that side.
> **· NORMAL (eyes stay locked on target) = CENTRAL.** This is counterintuitive: **a normal test is the worrying one.**
>
> **2. Nystagmus** —
> **· Unidirectional, horizontal (with a torsional component), fast phase always in the same direction = PERIPHERAL.**
> **· Direction-changing on gaze to either side, or PURELY VERTICAL, or purely torsional = CENTRAL.**
>
> **3. Test of Skew** — alternate cover test.
> **· Any vertical corrective movement (skew deviation) = CENTRAL.**
>
> **The mnemonic for the central pattern is "INFARCT": Impulse Normal, Fast-phase Alternating, Refixation on Cover Test.**
> **Any ONE central finding makes it central.** All three must be peripheral to call it peripheral.

> [!warning] HINTS-plus, and why hearing loss does not mean peripheral
> Adding **new unilateral hearing loss** to the examination improves sensitivity.
> **New hearing loss with acute vertigo is NOT reassuring** — an **anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) infarct supplies the labyrinth as well as the brainstem and cerebellum, so it can cause hearing loss with vertigo.** The historical teaching that hearing loss localises to the ear is not safe in this setting.

> [!danger] Other features that make it central
> **· Inability to stand or walk unaided.** **A patient with acute vertigo who cannot walk without falling has a central lesion until proven otherwise** — patients with vestibular neuritis veer toward the affected side but can generally walk.
> **· Severe headache or neck pain** — consider **vertebral artery dissection**, especially in a younger patient.
> **· Any other brainstem or cerebellar sign** — diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial numbness, limb ataxia, Horner syndrome, hemisensory loss.
> **· Sudden onset**, vascular risk factors, or age.
> **· Deterioration over hours** — cerebellar stroke swells and compresses the brainstem. Cross-refer [[D3]] 0.2.

> [!tip] Vestibular neuritis — treat, but do not over-treat
> Vestibular suppressants (prochlorperazine, promethazine, benzodiazepines) relieve acute symptoms.
> **Limit them to the first few days.** **Prolonged use PREVENTS central vestibular compensation and produces a patient who remains dizzy for months.** This is a common and avoidable iatrogenic problem.
> **Early vestibular rehabilitation — movement rather than rest — accelerates compensation**, and telling the patient to lie still in a dark room is the wrong advice beyond the first day or two. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended duration limit for suppressants, and the evidence for corticosteroids, which is contested.`

**S/Smx:** Continuous vertigo, nausea and vomiting, nystagmus, unsteadiness, and — where central — additional neurological signs.

**Ix:** **HINTS examination in the patient with ongoing nystagmus** (*why:* more sensitive than early MRI, immediate, and free; *what:* the INFARCT pattern versus a peripheral pattern). **Gait assessment — can the patient stand and walk unaided?** (*why:* inability to walk is a strong central indicator and takes seconds to test; *what:* truncal ataxia, falling). **MRI brain with diffusion, including posterior fossa** where central features are present or suspicion persists (*why:* CT is very poor for the posterior fossa; *what:* infarct — noting the false negative rate in the first 48 hours, so **a negative early MRI in a patient with a central HINTS pattern does not exclude stroke and should be repeated**). CT angiography of the vertebral and basilar arteries where dissection or vertebrobasilar disease is suspected (*why:* dissection is a leading cause of posterior circulation stroke in the young; *what:* dissection, stenosis). Audiometry where hearing loss (*why:* characterises the loss and distinguishes labyrinthine from retrocochlear; *what:* pattern and side). ECG and cardiac assessment (*why:* embolic source, and arrhythmia as an alternative cause of "dizziness"; *what:* AF, arrhythmia). Postural blood pressure (*why:* excludes orthostatic hypotension; *what:* postural drop).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
**HINTS and gait assessment.** Antiemetics and vestibular suppressants short-term. **Urgent imaging and stroke pathway if any central feature.** Admit and observe if the patient cannot mobilise safely.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Stroke management per [[D3]], or supportive care with early vestibular rehabilitation for neuritis.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Vestibular rehabilitation** for persistent symptoms, which is effective and under-referred. Secondary prevention after stroke. Driving advice.

## Brachial Plexus Injury — Erb's Palsy vs Klumpke's Palsy

> [!note] Gap-filled — genuinely absent as a dedicated entry despite Erb's palsy being mentioned in passing elsewhere in this project (as a neonatal birth injury, see [[16_08-09_Antenatal_and_Perinatal_Problems]], not repeated here) without ever explaining the classic clinical picture or contrasting it with Klumpke's palsy — a natural extension of the C5–T1 nerve root content immediately above.

**The brachial plexus (C5–T1) has an upper and lower portion, and injury to each produces a genuinely distinct, classically-tested clinical picture:**

| | Erb's (Erb–Duchenne) palsy | Klumpke's palsy |
|---|---|---|
| **Roots involved** | Upper plexus — C5–C6 (± C7) | Lower plexus — C8–T1 (± C7) |
| **Mechanism** | Excessive **increase** in the angle between shoulder and neck — forced lateral neck flexion away from the shoulder, or shoulder depression | Excessive **arm abduction** ("hyperabduction traction") — e.g. reaching overhead and being pulled, or breech extraction in obstetric cases |
| **Classic causes** | Shoulder dystocia/difficult vaginal delivery (most common obstetric cause — genuinely the **more common** of the two obstetric brachial plexus injuries, with the better spontaneous-recovery prognosis); motorcycle accidents; traction injuries ("stinger"/"burner" in contact sports) | Rare in obstetric palsy specifically (usually from arm-first/breech presentation with subsequent traction); adult traumatic hyperabduction injuries |
| **Classic posture/deformity** | **"Waiter's tip"** — arm adducted, internally rotated, elbow extended, wrist and fingers flexed (biceps/deltoid/brachialis weakness — "can't lift the arm to wave") | **"Claw hand"** — the intrinsic hand muscles are affected (same appearance as the ulnar/median nerve claw-hand pattern described above, since these lower plexus roots feed those same distal nerves) |
| **Reflex** | Absent biceps reflex (C5–C6) | Reflexes largely preserved (the affected roots don't carry major upper-limb reflexes) |
| **A genuinely important associated finding** | — | **Horner's syndrome** may co-occur if the injury is proximal enough to involve the T1 sympathetic fibres — the same anatomical link already established in the T1 nerve root entry above, not repeated here |

**Ix:** clinical diagnosis based on the pattern of weakness/deformity above; nerve conduction studies/EMG and MRI of the plexus for more detailed localisation or when surgical planning is being considered.

**Mx:** many cases (particularly obstetric Erb's palsy) **recover spontaneously with time** — physiotherapy/occupational therapy to maintain range of motion while awaiting recovery is the mainstay of initial management; surgical exploration and repair (nerve grafting, tendon transfers) is considered for severe injuries or those failing to show adequate recovery by around 3–6 months, given delayed surgical intervention is associated with poorer functional outcomes.

## 0.1 Localising the Lesion
`CF-PAIR` **Pairs with `## Brain Lesion Localisation` below — the same task from the Corpus A and Corpus B sides. Both kept in full, not reconciled.**

**D:** The systematic determination of which level of the nervous system is affected, from cortex to muscle.

> [!tip] The eight levels, and how each presents
> **Brain (cortex, internal capsule)** — UMN pattern, contralateral, often with cortical features (aphasia, neglect, visual field loss).
> **Spinal cord** — UMN below the lesion, LMN at the lesion, **a sensory level**, and sphincter involvement.
> **Anterior horn cell** — **mixed UMN and LMN signs with NO sensory involvement** (motor neurone disease).
> **Nerve root** — myotomal weakness, dermatomal sensory loss, loss of the reflex for that root, and **radicular pain shooting down the limb.**
> **Plexus** — deficits crossing multiple root and nerve territories, confined to one limb.
> **Peripheral nerve** — a single nerve territory (mononeuropathy), a symmetrical length-dependent pattern (polyneuropathy), or multiple discrete nerves (mononeuritis multiplex).
> **Neuromuscular junction** — **fatigable** weakness, ptosis and diplopia, bulbar involvement, **no sensory loss, normal reflexes.**
> **Muscle** — **proximal, symmetrical weakness, no sensory loss, reflexes preserved until late, raised CK.**

> [!info] Upper versus lower motor neurone
> | | **UMN** | **LMN** |
> |---|---|---|
> | Tone | **Increased** (spastic, clasp-knife) | **Reduced** or normal |
> | Reflexes | **Brisk**, clonus | **Reduced or absent** |
> | Plantar | **Upgoing (Babinski)** | Downgoing |
> | Wasting | Absent (except disuse, late) | **Present** |
> | Fasciculation | Absent | **Present** |
> | Pattern | **Pyramidal** — upper limb extensors and lower limb flexors weakest | Territory of the affected root or nerve |
> **Acute UMN lesions can initially cause FLACCID weakness with absent reflexes ("spinal shock"), with spasticity developing over days to weeks.** So a flaccid areflexic patient early after a cord injury is not evidence against a UMN lesion — this is a classic trap.

> [!warning] Two combinations that point to specific diagnoses
> **Mixed UMN and LMN signs with no sensory involvement** → **motor neurone disease**, until proven otherwise. Wasting and fasciculation with brisk reflexes in the same limb is a striking and specific finding.
> **Absent ankle reflexes with upgoing plantars** → a lesion affecting both peripheral nerves and the cord: **subacute combined degeneration from B12 deficiency**, or coexisting cervical myelopathy with a peripheral neuropathy (common in diabetics), or tabes dorsalis, or Friedreich ataxia.

**Ix:** **A careful examination is the investigation.** Nerve conduction studies and EMG confirm and refine, but they answer the question you pose, so **localise clinically first** — the request "?neuropathy" without a clinical question gets a less useful study.

## 0.1 Psychiatric Assessment and the Mental State Examination
`CF-PAIR §1.22` **This block and Part 1 §1.22 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] The MSE is an EXAMINATION, not a history
> **It describes what you observe and elicit in the room, at this moment** — in the same way a cardiovascular examination describes what you find on this occasion.
> **What the patient reports about last week belongs in the history. What they say and do now belongs in the MSE.**
> **Write it descriptively, so another clinician could picture the encounter** — "sat with head lowered, made brief eye contact when addressed, spoke in short sentences with long pauses" tells the reader more than "flat affect".

> [!info] The components
> **· APPEARANCE AND BEHAVIOUR** — grooming, dress, nutritional state, psychomotor activity (retardation or agitation), eye contact, rapport, cooperation, and **abnormal movements: extrapyramidal side effects, tardive dyskinesia, tremor, catatonic features.**
> **· SPEECH** — rate, rhythm, volume, quantity, tone. **Pressured** speech, **poverty** of speech, latency of response, and dysarthria or dysphasia, which point to an organic cause.
> **· MOOD AND AFFECT** — **MOOD is the patient's SUBJECTIVE report, ideally in their own words. AFFECT is your OBJECTIVE observation** — range (full, restricted, blunted, flat), reactivity, and congruence with stated mood and with content.
> **· THOUGHT FORM** — how thinking is organised: **circumstantiality, tangentiality, flight of ideas, loosening of associations, thought blocking, neologisms, perseveration.**
> **· THOUGHT CONTENT** — **delusions** (persecutory, grandiose, nihilistic, somatic, of reference, of control; **mood-congruent or incongruent**), overvalued ideas, obsessions, ruminations, and **suicidal or homicidal ideation, which must be actively asked about and documented.**
> **· THOUGHT POSSESSION** — insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting.
> **· PERCEPTION** — **hallucinations, specifying the modality.** **Auditory hallucinations in the second person ("you are worthless") or third person (voices discussing the patient) are more typical of primary psychiatric illness; VISUAL, olfactory and tactile hallucinations should raise an ORGANIC cause.** Also illusions, depersonalisation and derealisation.
> **· COGNITION** — orientation, attention and concentration, memory; formal testing where indicated.
> **· INSIGHT AND JUDGEMENT** — **not binary.** Describe: does the person recognise something is wrong, do they attribute it to illness, do they see a need for treatment, and can they weigh options? These can be present in one respect and absent in another.

> [!danger] Collateral history is not optional, and neither is the physical examination
> **Collateral from family, carers, the GP, the community team, previous records and ambulance staff frequently changes the assessment entirely** — particularly regarding timeline, baseline function, substance use and risk.
> **Obtain it, with consent where possible — and in an emergency, obtaining information necessary to assess risk is generally justified without it.** Document what you did and why.
> **EVERY psychiatric presentation requires observations, a physical examination and a targeted medical assessment.** See 0.6.
> **Attributing new symptoms to a known psychiatric diagnosis without examining the patient is DIAGNOSTIC OVERSHADOWING**, and it is a documented cause of missed serious illness and premature death in people with mental illness. Cross-refer [[AU1]] 0.5.

> **· Get down to the child's level, introduce yourself to THEM, and explain what you are doing in words they understand.**
> **· Do the least distressing parts of the examination first and the ears and throat last.**
> **· Use DISTRACTION — a parent's phone, bubbles, a toy, counting — which measurably reduces distress and improves cooperation.**
> **· Keep the parent close, and use them: a child examined on a parent's lap tolerates far more than one on a couch.**
> **· Be honest about whether something will hurt.** A child who is told "this won't hurt" and then hurt does not trust the next clinician, and that has consequences for years.
> **· Involve older children and adolescents directly in the conversation rather than talking over them.** Cross-refer [[M7]].
> **· Explain to parents what you are looking for, what the plan is, and — critically — WHAT SHOULD MAKE THEM RETURN.** **Safety-netting is part of the treatment**: give specific, concrete return criteria rather than "come back if you're worried".

## 0.1 Lachman Test

**D:** With the patient supine and the knee flexed to **20–30°**, stabilise the femur with one hand and pull the tibia **anteriorly** with the other, feeling for excessive anterior translation and, critically, for the quality of the **end point**.

**Tests for:** **Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture.**

**Positive:** Increased anterior translation compared with the other side, with a **soft or absent end point**. The end point is the more reliable finding — translation alone is subjective.

> [!info] **Performance — and this is the test to learn.** Sources place the Lachman as **the most sensitive** of the ACL tests: pooled sensitivity around **85%** with specificity around **94%**, though a more recent meta-analysis argues its sensitivity has been **historically overestimated** and is closer to that of the other tests. In acute injury, pooled sensitivity falls to roughly **0.79**. **The consistent recommendation across sources is: if you perform one test for a suspected ACL rupture, perform the Lachman.**

> [!warning] **Why it beats the anterior drawer.** At 20–30° of flexion the **hamstrings are relaxed** and the **menisci do not block** anterior translation. At 90° (the anterior drawer position), hamstring spasm in an acutely painful knee can hold the tibia and mask a complete rupture.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A large acute haemarthrosis after a twisting injury with a "pop" is an ACL rupture until proven otherwise** — the differential also includes patellar dislocation, osteochondral fracture and peripheral meniscal tear.
> - **Always examine the other knee first** to establish that patient's normal, and to gain their confidence.
> - **Pain and guarding make the acute examination unreliable in both directions.** Re-examine after analgesia, or a few days later once the effusion and spasm settle.
> - **Exclude a fracture before stress-testing ligaments** — apply the Ottawa knee rules and image if indicated.
> - See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

**Alt:** Anterior drawer (0.2), pivot shift (0.3), lever sign; **MRI** — the imaging standard for cruciate and meniscal injury; instrumented laxity measurement; examination under anaesthesia; arthroscopy.

## 0.2 Anterior Drawer Test

**D:** **Two distinct tests share this name, and they must not be confused.**
- **Knee:** patient supine, hip flexed 45°, **knee flexed to 90°**, foot stabilised (often by the examiner sitting on it); grasp the proximal tibia and pull **anteriorly**.
- **Ankle:** patient seated with the ankle in slight plantarflexion; stabilise the distal tibia with one hand and pull the **heel forward** with the other.

**Tests for:** **Knee** — ACL rupture. **Ankle** — **anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)** injury in a lateral ankle sprain.

**Positive:** Excessive anterior translation with a soft end point (knee); anterior translation of the talus, sometimes with a visible dimple or "suction sign" over the anterolateral ankle (ankle).

> [!info] **Performance at the knee — and the acute/chronic split is the whole story.** Sources report the anterior drawer performs **well in chronic ACL deficiency** (sensitivity ~92%, specificity ~91%) **but not in acute injury**, where hamstring spasm and effusion defeat it — one meta-analysis puts acute sensitivity at about **0.78** with specificity **0.91**. **In an acute knee, use the Lachman.**

> [!warning] **At the ankle, timing matters.** The test is unreliable immediately after injury because of pain and swelling; **re-examination at about 4–5 days**, once swelling settles, is far more informative, and this delayed reassessment is standard practice for grading lateral ligament injury.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A false-negative anterior drawer at the knee occurs with a "locked" bucket-handle meniscal tear**, where the displaced meniscus physically blocks anterior translation — so a normal test in a knee that will not fully extend is not reassuring.
> - **A posterior cruciate rupture causes a false-positive anterior drawer**: the tibia starts sagged posteriorly, so pulling it forward feels like excess translation. **Always look for posterior sag first** (0.4) — this is the classic trap, and it makes a PCL injury look like an ACL injury.
> - **Apply the Ottawa ankle rules** before stress-testing an injured ankle — exclude fracture, and specifically palpate the **proximal fibula** (Maisonneuve) and the **base of the fifth metatarsal**.
> - See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

**Alt:** Lachman (0.1) and pivot shift (0.3) at the knee; talar tilt and the **squeeze test** for syndesmotic injury at the ankle; **MRI**; ultrasound for ligament assessment; stress radiographs.

## 0.3 Pivot Shift Test

**D:** With the knee in extension, apply a **valgus force and internal rotation** to the tibia while slowly **flexing** the knee. In an ACL-deficient knee the anteriorly subluxed lateral tibial plateau **reduces with a palpable clunk at around 20–40° of flexion** as the iliotibial band changes its line of pull.

**Tests for:** ACL rupture, demonstrating the **rotational instability** that causes the patient's symptom of giving way.

**Positive:** A palpable or visible **clunk or jerk** as the tibia reduces.

> [!info] **Performance — the most specific ACL test, and the least sensitive.** Sources agree: specificity around **98%**, sensitivity only around **24%** overall (a meta-analysis of acute injuries gives sensitivity ~0.55, specificity ~0.96). **A positive pivot shift essentially confirms an ACL rupture; a negative one excludes nothing.**

> [!danger] **It requires a relaxed patient, which means it is close to useless in an acute painful knee** — guarding abolishes the shift. It is most reliably elicited **under anaesthesia**, which is where it is often formally documented. Do not conclude the ACL is intact because you could not produce a pivot shift in the emergency department.

> [!warning] **It reproduces the patient's instability, which is unpleasant.** Warn them, examine the uninjured side first, and stop if they cannot relax — repeated failed attempts guarantee guarding and make every subsequent test uninterpretable.

> [!info] **Why it matters clinically:** the pivot shift correlates with **functional instability and with the risk of subsequent meniscal and chondral damage**, which is part of the argument for reconstruction in an active patient. A high-grade pivot shift is not merely a diagnostic finding.

**Alt:** Lachman (0.1) — the screening test; anterior drawer (0.2); the Jerk test and other pivot-shift variants; **MRI**; examination under anaesthesia; arthroscopy. See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

## 0.4 Posterior Drawer Test

**D:** Patient supine, hip flexed 45°, **knee flexed to 90°**, foot stabilised; push the proximal tibia **posteriorly**. **Before doing anything else, look at the knee from the side** with both knees flexed to 90° and compare the position of the tibial tuberosities.

**Tests for:** **Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)** injury.

**Positive:** Excessive posterior translation with a soft end point, and — the more important sign — a visible **posterior sag** of the tibia at rest.

> [!danger] **The posterior sag sign is the finding that prevents the classic misdiagnosis.**
> A tibia that starts sagged posteriorly gives a **falsely positive anterior drawer**, because the first part of the anterior pull is simply reducing the tibia from its sagged position. **A PCL injury misread as an ACL injury is a recognised and consequential error.** Always inspect for sag before testing anteriorly.

> [!info] **Mechanism directs the examination.** PCL injuries follow a **direct blow to the front of the flexed tibia** — the **dashboard injury** in a motor vehicle crash, or a fall onto a flexed knee with the foot plantarflexed. That history should prompt a deliberate PCL examination, because it is otherwise easily missed and PCL injuries are often less dramatic acutely than ACL ruptures.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A knee dislocation is a limb-threatening emergency.** Multiligament injury — and particularly a knee that has clearly dislocated and spontaneously reduced — carries a high risk of **popliteal artery injury and common peroneal nerve palsy**. **Document distal pulses and neurology, and involve senior orthopaedic and vascular teams urgently**; a normal pulse does not exclude an intimal injury, and vascular imaging is usually required. See [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]].
> - Assess for associated **posterolateral corner** injury (dial test, external rotation asymmetry) — isolated PCL injury is much less common than combined injury, and missing the corner leads to failed reconstruction.
> - **MRI** is required for a suspected multiligament injury.

**Alt:** Posterior sag / godfrey test; quadriceps active test; dial test for posterolateral corner; **MRI**; stress radiographs; vascular assessment with ankle-brachial index and CT angiography where dislocation is suspected.

## 0.5 Thompson Test (Simmonds-Thompson, Calf Squeeze)

**D:** With the patient **prone and the feet hanging over the end of the bed**, squeeze the calf (the gastrocnemius-soleus mass) and observe the foot. Compare with the other side.

**Tests for:** **Complete Achilles tendon rupture.**

**Positive:** **Absent plantarflexion** of the foot on squeezing — the tendon is not transmitting the contraction.

> [!info] **Performance.** Sources describe it as the standard initial test for suspected Achilles rupture, quoting sensitivity around **96%** and specificity around **93%**. It was described by **Simmonds in 1957**, which is why the eponym is sometimes doubled.

> [!danger] **A patient with a completely ruptured Achilles tendon CAN STILL PLANTARFLEX and CAN OFTEN STILL WALK.**
> The long flexors and tibialis posterior plantarflex the foot, so asking the patient to "point your toes" or watching them walk **does not exclude the diagnosis**. This is why the Thompson test exists and why **Achilles rupture is one of the most commonly missed injuries in emergency departments**. Sources also note the test cannot distinguish a partial from a complete tear.

> [!warning] **The examination that finds it**
> - **A palpable gap** in the tendon, usually 2–6 cm above the insertion, though it fills with haematoma within hours and becomes harder to feel.
> - **Loss of the normal resting tension** — with the patient prone, the affected foot hangs in more dorsiflexion than the other.
> - **The history is characteristic**: sudden pain "like being kicked or shot in the back of the heel", often in a middle-aged intermittent athlete, often with an audible snap.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroids** (systemic or local injection) are recognised risk factors for tendon rupture — ask.
> - **Missed rupture leads to chronic weakness, a poorer functional result and a harder reconstruction.** If in doubt, **immobilise in equinus and refer** — that costs little and protects the tendon.
> - **Ultrasound is a quick, accessible confirmatory test** and can assess gap size in plantarflexion, which influences management; MRI is reserved for complex or chronic cases.
> - See [[11_03_Ortho_-_Hand_and_Foot]].

**Alt:** Palpation for a gap; Matles test (prone knee flexion, observing resting foot position); **ultrasound**; MRI; the O'Brien needle test (rarely used).

## 0.6 Finkelstein's Test

**D:** The **true Finkelstein test**: the examiner grasps the patient's **thumb** and applies longitudinal traction and **ulnar deviation**, reproducing pain over the **first dorsal compartment** at the radial styloid. Distinguished from **Eichhoff's manoeuvre** — the patient tucks the thumb into the palm, closes the fingers over it, and the examiner ulnar-deviates the wrist.

**Tests for:** **De Quervain tenosynovitis** — stenosing tenosynovitis of **abductor pollicis longus** and **extensor pollicis brevis**.

**Positive:** Sharp pain over the radial styloid and first dorsal compartment, reproducing the patient's symptoms.

> [!danger] **The manoeuvre everyone is taught as "Finkelstein's" is usually Eichhoff's, and it produces false positives.**
> Sources are explicit: **Eichhoff's test is commonly but incorrectly taught as Finkelstein's**, is very provocative, and generates false-positive pain by stressing adjacent radial wrist structures — the scaphoid, the first CMC joint, the radiocarpal joint. **A properly performed Finkelstein test is much less likely to be falsely positive.** Knowing the distinction is the point of this entry: a "positive Finkelstein" in a patient whose pain is actually from thumb-base arthritis leads to the wrong treatment.

> [!warning] **The differential at the radial side of the wrist is the reason for the false positives**
> - **Thumb CMC (basal joint) osteoarthritis** — pain more distal, at the thumb base; test with the grind test (0.7). **The two conditions coexist commonly in the same age group.**
> - **Scaphoid fracture** — anatomical snuffbox tenderness after a fall on the outstretched hand; a completely different and time-critical problem.
> - **Intersection syndrome** — pain and crepitus more proximally, about 4–6 cm proximal to the wrist.
> - **Wartenberg syndrome** — superficial radial nerve entrapment, causing numbness rather than pain on resisted movement.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **De Quervain tenosynovitis is strongly associated with the postpartum period and with infant care** ("mother's thumb") — the repetitive lifting of a baby with an abducted thumb. Ask, because it changes the counselling and the prognosis.
> - **Do not miss a scaphoid fracture.** Snuffbox tenderness after a fall means scaphoid views and, if radiographs are normal but suspicion persists, immobilisation and re-imaging or MRI — not "wrist sprain".
> - Management is splinting, activity modification, NSAIDs and **corticosteroid injection**, which is effective; surgical release for refractory cases. **Repeated injections risk tendon rupture and subcutaneous fat atrophy and depigmentation**, which is disfiguring and permanent — warn the patient.
> - See [[11_03_Ortho_-_Hand_and_Foot]].

**Alt:** Direct palpation of the first dorsal compartment; the **grind test** (0.7) to identify coexisting CMC arthritis; ultrasound (which can also show a septated compartment, a cause of injection failure); radiographs to exclude arthritis and fracture.

## 0.7 Grind Test (Thumb Carpometacarpal)

**D:** Grasp the patient's **thumb metacarpal**, apply **axial compression** into the trapezium and simultaneously **rotate**, grinding the joint surfaces against each other.

**Tests for:** **Osteoarthritis of the first carpometacarpal (trapeziometacarpal, "basal") joint.**

**Positive:** Pain, and often **palpable crepitus**, at the base of the thumb.

> [!info] **Performance — specific rather than sensitive.** Sources report sensitivity of **42–53%** depending on examiner, with specificity of **80–93%**. Later work found a **pressure-shear test** substantially more sensitive (reported at 99–100%) than the grind test. **A positive grind test supports the diagnosis; a negative one does not exclude it**, and inter-examiner variation is significant.

> [!warning] **Thumb-base osteoarthritis is common, disabling and under-recognised** — particularly in **postmenopausal women**. It causes pain on pinch and grip (keys, jars, taps, pegs), and later an **adduction deformity of the metacarpal with compensatory MCP hyperextension** (the "zigzag" or shoulder sign). It is frequently dismissed as "arthritis in the hands" without a specific diagnosis, which forgoes effective treatment.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **It coexists with de Quervain tenosynovitis and with carpal tunnel syndrome** in the same patients; one diagnosis does not exclude the others, and treating only the one you tested for leaves the patient no better.
> - **Radiographic severity correlates poorly with symptoms** — treat the patient, not the film.
> - **Non-operative treatment is genuinely effective**: a thumb spica or CMC splint, hand therapy, joint protection education and adaptive aids, NSAIDs, and intra-articular injection. **Refer to hand therapy before considering surgery.**
> - **Rapidly progressive, hot, or inflammatory-sounding joint pain is not osteoarthritis** — consider inflammatory arthritis, gout and infection.

**Alt:** Pressure-shear test (reported as more sensitive); crank/traction-shift tests; palpation and pinch strength measurement; **radiographs** (thumb views, including a stress view) for staging; ultrasound; diagnostic injection.

## 0.8 FABER Test (Patrick's Test)

**D:** **F**lexion, **AB**duction, **E**xternal **R**otation. Patient supine; place the ankle of the tested leg on the opposite knee (figure-4), then stabilise the opposite anterior superior iliac spine and press the tested knee **downwards**.

**Tests for:** Hip joint pathology and **sacroiliac joint** pathology — and, importantly, **it helps distinguish between them by where the pain is felt**.

**Positive:** Pain, and restricted downward movement of the knee compared with the other side.

> [!info] **Location of the pain is the discriminator, and this is what makes the test useful**
> - **Pain in the GROIN** → **hip joint** pathology (osteoarthritis, femoroacetabular impingement, labral tear, avascular necrosis).
> - **Pain in the BUTTOCK over the sacroiliac joint** → **sacroiliac** pathology.
> - Anterior thigh or knee pain may reflect referred hip pain — remember hip pathology commonly presents as knee pain, especially in children.

> [!warning] **Neither sensitive nor specific on its own.** FABER is one of a **cluster** of sacroiliac provocation tests (with distraction, compression, thigh thrust and Gaenslen), and the accepted approach is that **multiple positive tests in a cluster** carry weight while any single test does not. Used alone it will mislead.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Inflammatory back pain** — pain and stiffness worse in the morning and with rest, better with exercise, waking the patient in the second half of the night, in a person under 45 — should prompt assessment for **axial spondyloarthritis**, including the modified Schober test (0.13), HLA-B27, inflammatory markers and sacroiliac imaging. Diagnostic delay in ankylosing spondylitis is measured in years. See [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]].
> - **A child or adolescent with hip pain, a limp, or referred knee pain** needs a specific differential — septic arthritis, transient synovitis, Perthes, SUFE — and septic arthritis must be excluded urgently. See [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]].
> - **Do not force the manoeuvre** in a patient with a suspected fracture, a hip prosthesis (risk of dislocation), or severe pain.
> - See [[11_04_Ortho_-_Hip]].

**Alt:** Full hip range of motion with **log roll** (the most specific test for intra-articular hip pathology); FADIR/impingement test; Thomas test for fixed flexion; the sacroiliac provocation cluster; Trendelenburg test (0.9); radiographs and MRI.

## 0.9 Trendelenburg Test
`CF-PAIR §1.26` **Part 1 §1.26 Trendelenburg's Sign and Gait covers the same test. Both kept in full, NOT reconciled.**

**D:** Ask the patient to **stand on one leg** while you observe the pelvis from behind, ideally with your hands resting on both iliac crests. Repeat on the other side.

**Tests for:** Function of the **hip abductors — gluteus medius and minimus** — and of the superior gluteal nerve that supplies them.

**Positive:** **The pelvis drops on the side of the LIFTED leg** — which means the weakness is in the **STANDING** (weight-bearing) leg. Sources state this explicitly, and getting the side right is the entire content of the test.

> [!danger] **The side is the exam question and the clinical point: the weak side is the side the patient is STANDING on.**
> The abductors of the stance leg must contract to hold the unsupported hemipelvis level. If they cannot, that side of the pelvis fails and the **opposite** side sags. Reporting the wrong side sends the investigation to the wrong hip.

> [!info] **What causes it**
> - **Pain** — the commonest reason in practice: any painful hip (osteoarthritis, fracture, infection) inhibits abduction. This is an **antalgic** rather than a truly weak Trendelenburg.
> - **Muscle weakness** — gluteal tendinopathy or tear (common and under-diagnosed in older women), disuse, myopathy.
> - **Superior gluteal nerve injury** — a recognised complication of hip surgery and of badly sited intramuscular gluteal injections.
> - **Abnormal joint mechanics** — developmental dysplasia, a shortened femoral neck after fracture, failed arthroplasty.
> - **A compensated gait** produces the **Trendelenburg lurch**: the patient leans their trunk **over** the affected stance leg to move the centre of gravity and reduce the abductor demand — a bilateral version gives a waddling gait.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Bilateral positive tests** suggest a **proximal myopathy** or bilateral hip disease — check for other proximal weakness, ask about steroids and statins, and consider CK, TFTs and inflammatory myopathy.
> - **In a child, a positive Trendelenburg with a limp** raises developmental dysplasia of the hip, Perthes disease and SUFE.
> - **Balance, not just strength, limits the test** — a patient may fail because they cannot balance. Allow light fingertip support and interpret accordingly.
> - **Lateral hip pain with a positive Trendelenburg in an older woman is usually gluteal tendinopathy**, not "trochanteric bursitis", and it responds to load-based physiotherapy rather than repeated injection.

**Alt:** Formal abductor power testing; observed gait (looking for the lurch); single-leg stance timing; hip radiographs and **MRI or ultrasound** for gluteal tendon pathology; EMG/NCS where nerve injury is suspected.

## 0.10 Adam's Test (Forward Bend Test)
`CF-PAIR §1.25` **Part 1 §1.25 Scoliosis Screening (Adam's Forward Bend Test) covers the same test. Both kept in full, NOT reconciled.**

**D:** The patient stands and **bends forward from the waist with knees straight and arms hanging**, feet together, while the examiner views the back **tangentially from behind** looking for asymmetry — a **rib hump** or paravertebral prominence. Quantified with a **scoliometer**, which measures the **angle of trunk rotation (ATR)**.

**Tests for:** **Scoliosis** — specifically, the **rotational deformity** that distinguishes a structural curve from a postural one.

**Positive:** A visible or measurable asymmetry of the trunk on forward flexion.

> [!info] **Structural versus postural — what the test actually determines**
> A **structural** scoliosis has a **rotational component**, so the deformity **persists or becomes more obvious** on forward bending. A **postural** curve — from leg length discrepancy, muscle spasm or posture — **disappears** on forward flexion. That distinction, not the presence of a curve, is what the test is for.

> [!info] **Referral thresholds.** Sources describe **5° and 7° of angle of trunk rotation** as the criteria proposed for referral in school screening settings. They also report a meaningful discrepancy: in one screening study of 954 children, **123 children at >5° and 13 at >7° were abnormal on the scoliometer but appeared normal on the Adams test alone** — the visual test alone under-detects.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A painful scoliosis, a rapidly progressive curve, a LEFT thoracic curve, or any curve with neurological signs is not adolescent idiopathic scoliosis** until proven otherwise. Those features suggest an underlying cause — syrinx, tethered cord, tumour, osteoid osteoma, neuromuscular disease — and warrant **MRI** and specialist referral. Idiopathic scoliosis is typically painless and right-thoracic.
> - **Curves progress fastest during the adolescent growth spurt**, so **skeletal maturity determines urgency**. A moderate curve in a premenarchal girl with open growth plates is far more concerning than the same curve in a skeletally mature patient. Assess growth status.
> - **The Cobb angle on a standing full-spine radiograph is what defines and grades scoliosis** — the forward bend test screens, radiography diagnoses.
> - **Early referral preserves options.** Bracing works in the growing child with a moderate curve; once the curve is severe or growth has finished, surgery is the only option left.
> - See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]].

**Normal/abnormal:** Angle of trunk rotation in degrees; referral at the threshold used by your local service (5° or 7° per the sources).

**Alt:** **Scoliometer** (should accompany the visual test); standing full-spine **radiograph with Cobb angle**; assessment of skeletal maturity (Risser sign, menarchal status); **MRI** for atypical features; leg length measurement to identify a postural cause.

## 0.11 Straight Leg Raise (Lasègue's Sign)

**D:** With the patient **supine**, raise the straight leg passively with the knee extended. Note the angle at which the patient's **radicular** pain is reproduced. **Sciatic nerve tension increases from about 30° to 70°.** The **crossed (contralateral) SLR** raises the *unaffected* leg and is positive if it reproduces pain **in the affected leg**.

**Tests for:** **Lumbosacral nerve root irritation** — most often an **L5 or S1** radiculopathy from disc herniation.

**Positive:** Reproduction of **radicular pain radiating below the knee** at 30–70° of elevation. **Pain confined to the back or hamstring is not a positive test.**

> [!info] **Performance — the ipsilateral and crossed tests are mirror images of each other**
> - **Ipsilateral SLR: sensitive, not specific** — sources give sensitivity **72–97%** and specificity **11–66%**. Useful for **ruling out**.
> - **Crossed SLR: specific, not sensitive** — sensitivity **23–42%**, specificity **85–100%**. **A positive crossed SLR is strong evidence of significant nerve root compression**, often from a large central or paracentral disc, and it is the finding that should raise your concern most.
> - **Sensitivity falls markedly with age** — one source reports sensitivity of only **33%** in patients over 60, with a two-thirds false-negative rate. **A negative SLR in an older patient means very little**, and in that age group the differential (spinal stenosis, malignancy, fracture, vascular claudication) is different anyway.

> [!danger] **CAUDA EQUINA SYNDROME must be actively excluded in every patient with severe or bilateral sciatica.**
> Ask about and document: **urinary retention or incontinence, faecal incontinence, saddle (perineal) anaesthesia, bilateral leg symptoms, and progressive or profound motor weakness.** **Examine perianal sensation and anal tone if there is any suspicion.** This is an **emergency requiring urgent MRI and same-day neurosurgical or spinal referral** — delay causes permanent incontinence and sexual dysfunction, and "arrange an outpatient scan" is not an acceptable plan. See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]].

> [!warning] **False positives are common.** Hamstring tightness, hip pathology, sacroiliac pain and simple back pain all limit the raise. **Sensitising manoeuvres** — **ankle dorsiflexion** or **neck flexion** at the point of pain — increase neural tension without changing muscle length, and worsening pain on those manoeuvres supports a genuinely neural origin. Reproducing back pain alone is not a positive test.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore the other red flags for back pain**: age over 50 with new pain, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, fever or intravenous drug use, night pain, trauma, prolonged corticosteroids, or any progressive neurological deficit. These change the pathway from conservative management to urgent imaging. See also `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.8.

**Alt:** **Slump test** (reported as more sensitive, less specific); crossed SLR; **femoral stretch test** (0.12) for upper lumbar roots; neurological examination by myotome, dermatome and reflex (see [[11_07a_Ortho_-_Dermatomes_and_Myotomes_Reference]]); **MRI lumbar spine** — urgently where red flags or cauda equina features are present.

## 0.12 Femoral Stretch Test (Reverse Lasègue / Prone Knee Bend)

**D:** With the patient **prone** (or lying on the unaffected side), **flex the knee** and then **extend the hip**, stretching the femoral nerve and the upper lumbar roots.

**Tests for:** Irritation of the **upper lumbar nerve roots — L2, L3 and L4** — and of the femoral nerve. **It is the upper-lumbar counterpart of the straight leg raise**, which tests L5 and S1.

**Positive:** Reproduction of pain in the **anterior thigh** (and sometimes the medial lower leg via the saphenous branch), not merely tightness in the quadriceps.

> [!info] **Why you need both tests.** Most lumbar disc herniations are at L4/5 and L5/S1, which is why the SLR is the test everyone knows. **Upper lumbar herniations are much less common and are correspondingly more often missed** — and they present differently: **anterior thigh pain, quadriceps weakness, a reduced or absent knee jerk**, rather than posterior leg pain and an absent ankle jerk. If the symptoms are anterior, the SLR is the wrong test.

> [!warning] **Published diagnostic accuracy for this test is sparse** — the sources retrieved on straight leg raising did not provide comparable sensitivity and specificity figures for the femoral stretch test, and **none are quoted here** rather than borrowing SLR figures that do not apply. Interpret it as a supportive sign within the neurological examination, not as a stand-alone test.

> [!danger] **Anterior thigh pain with quadriceps weakness has a differential beyond disc disease, and some of it is urgent**
> - **Diabetic amyotrophy (lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy)** — severe anterior thigh pain with wasting and weakness, often with weight loss, in a person with diabetes.
> - **Retroperitoneal or psoas haematoma** compressing the femoral nerve — think of this in anyone **anticoagulated**, and it is a diagnosis that is missed until the deficit is fixed.
> - **Psoas abscess** — fever, back or flank pain, a flexed hip that resists extension.
> - **Malignancy or infection in the upper lumbar spine or retroperitoneum.**
> - **A femoral nerve palsy after hip surgery, prolonged lithotomy positioning, or a groin procedure.**

> [!danger] **Do not ignore** — as with the SLR, **exclude cauda equina syndrome**, and remember that **a positive stretch test localises the level but never establishes the cause**. Imaging and the rest of the neurological examination do that.

**Alt:** Straight leg raise (0.11) for the lower roots; knee jerk and quadriceps power; sensory testing over the anterior thigh and medial calf; **MRI lumbar spine**; CT abdomen/pelvis where a retroperitoneal cause is suspected; EMG/NCS.

## 0.13 Modified Schober Test

**D:** With the patient standing, mark the **lumbosacral junction** (at the level of the posterior superior iliac spines / the dimples of Venus), then mark **10 cm above** and **5 cm below** it. Ask the patient to **bend forward maximally** with the knees straight, and re-measure the distance between the outer marks.

**Tests for:** **Lumbar spine flexion** — as an objective measure of the spinal restriction of **axial spondyloarthritis / ankylosing spondylitis**, and to track it over time.

**Positive (abnormal):** An **increase of less than 5 cm** on the 15 cm baseline indicates reduced lumbar flexion; sources describe an increase of **more than 5 cm** as a healthy range of motion.

> [!warning] **Know which version you are performing, and record it.** The **original Schober** used 10 cm above the lumbosacral junction only; the **modified** version adds the 5 cm below (baseline 15 cm); the **modified-modified (Macrae-Wright)** version uses different landmarks again. **They give different numbers and are not interchangeable** — serial measurements must use the same method and, ideally, the same examiner.

> [!danger] **A single measurement is nearly useless; the trend is the point.** Its value is in **monitoring** an individual patient over years and in composite disease activity indices, not in diagnosing anyone. Sources include a study concluding that **the Schober test is not a valid assessment tool for lumbar mobility** when validated against radiographic measurement — so treat it as a crude, reproducible, cheap surrogate rather than a measurement of what it claims to measure.

> [!danger] **The diagnosis is not made by this test, and delay is the real problem.** Suspect **axial spondyloarthritis** from the history: **inflammatory back pain** — insidious onset **before age 45**, **worse with rest and better with exercise**, **morning stiffness over 30 minutes**, **night pain waking the patient in the second half of the night** — with peripheral and extra-articular features: **enthesitis** (Achilles, plantar fascia), **dactylitis**, **acute anterior uveitis**, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, and a family history. **Diagnostic delay in ankylosing spondylitis is typically measured in years**, largely because inflammatory back pain is treated as mechanical.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Acute anterior uveitis is an ophthalmic emergency** — a painful red eye with photophobia in a patient with back pain needs same-day ophthalmology, and it is often the presentation that reveals the underlying spondyloarthritis.
> - **The ankylosed spine fractures easily and unstably.** Minor trauma in a patient with advanced ankylosing spondylitis can cause a **three-column cervical fracture** with a high risk of cord injury; the threshold for CT is very low, and plain films are inadequate.
> - **Investigate with** inflammatory markers, HLA-B27 (supportive, not diagnostic — it is common in the general population), **sacroiliac joint imaging** (X-ray, and **MRI** which detects inflammation before radiographic change), and rheumatology referral.
> - **Exercise and physiotherapy are first-line treatment**, alongside NSAIDs and, where indicated, biologic therapy. Measure chest expansion and occiput-to-wall distance as well.
> - See [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]].

**Alt:** Occiput-to-wall and tragus-to-wall distance; chest expansion; lateral lumbar flexion; cervical rotation; composite metrology and disease activity indices used in rheumatology; sacroiliac joint **MRI**.

## 0.14 Compression Test (Spurling's Test)

**D:** **Spurling's manoeuvre**: the patient's neck is **extended and laterally flexed (and often rotated) towards the symptomatic side**, and the examiner applies gentle **axial compression** to the top of the head, narrowing the neural foramen on that side.

**Tests for:** **Cervical radiculopathy** — nerve root compression, usually from a disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte.

**Positive:** Reproduction of the patient's **radicular** arm pain or paraesthesia in a dermatomal distribution. **Local neck pain alone is NOT a positive test.**

> [!info] **Performance — specific, not sensitive, so it confirms rather than excludes.** Sources report sensitivity around **30%** with specificity around **93%** in acute radiculopathy; a later study in patients with unilateral radiculopathy of at least 4 weeks reported much higher figures (sensitivity 95%, specificity 94%) — **the sources genuinely conflict**, and the honest summary is that Spurling's test shows **low-to-moderate sensitivity and high specificity**. **A positive test is clinically useful for confirming radiculopathy; a negative test does not rule it out.**

> [!danger] **Do not perform this test if there is any suspicion of cervical instability, fracture, or myelopathy.**
> Axial compression on a potentially unstable cervical spine is dangerous. **Contraindications include acute trauma, known instability (rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome), suspected fracture, and signs of cord compression.** Stop immediately if the test produces bilateral symptoms, lower limb symptoms or any suggestion of cord involvement.

> [!danger] **Distinguish radiculopathy from MYELOPATHY — this is the point that changes management.**
> **Cervical myelopathy** is cord compression, and it is progressive, often painless, and easily missed: **clumsy hands** (difficulty with buttons and coins), **deteriorating handwriting**, a **broad-based unsteady gait**, **bladder disturbance**, **upper motor neurone signs** (hyperreflexia below the level, Hoffmann sign, Babinski, clonus), and **Lhermitte phenomenon**. It requires **urgent MRI and spinal surgical referral** — decompression prevents further deterioration but does not reliably reverse established damage, so time matters.

> [!warning] **Do not ignore**
> - **Red flags in neck pain**: trauma, fever, malignancy history, weight loss, night pain, progressive neurological deficit, **Horner syndrome**, and vertebrobasilar symptoms (dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, drop attacks) — any of which redirects the work-up entirely.
> - **Not every arm pain is cervical**: consider brachial plexopathy, thoracic outlet syndrome, peripheral entrapment (carpal or cubital tunnel), shoulder pathology, and **Pancoast tumour** (apical lung cancer with C8/T1 symptoms and Horner syndrome — a classic miss in a smoker).
> - Most cervical radiculopathy improves with conservative management; surgery is for persistent or progressive deficit.
> - See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] and [[04_Neurology]].

**Alt:** **Cervical distraction test** (0.15); upper limb neurodynamic (tension) tests; **shoulder abduction relief sign**; full neurological examination by dermatome, myotome and reflex; **MRI cervical spine**; EMG/NCS where the level or the peripheral/central distinction is unclear.

## 0.15 Distraction Test (Cervical Distraction)

**D:** With the patient supine, the examiner grasps the head under the **occiput and chin** and applies gentle **axial traction**, unloading the cervical spine and widening the neural foramina.

**Tests for:** **Cervical radiculopathy** — it is the **relief** counterpart to Spurling's compression test.

**Positive:** **Relief or reduction** of the patient's radicular arm symptoms during traction. Unusually among provocative tests, **the positive finding is that the patient feels better.**

> [!info] **Performance and the limitation to know.** Sources report **sensitivity around 44%** with **high specificity** — the same pattern as Spurling's. Critically, they also note that **manual distraction relieves cervical facetogenic and discogenic pain as well as radicular pain**, so a positive test does not prove that the pain is radicular in origin. It is used **in combination** with Spurling's, upper limb neurodynamic testing and cervical rotation as a **cluster**, which performs far better than any single test.

> [!warning] **The same contraindications apply as for the compression test** — do not distract a spine that might be unstable or fractured, and stop for any bilateral, lower limb or cord symptoms. Apply traction gently and gradually; a sudden pull is both frightening and unsafe.

> [!danger] **The clinical value is that it points towards a treatment.** A patient whose arm pain is relieved by manual distraction is one in whom **traction-based physiotherapy and postural management** may help — and it gives the patient the reassuring information that a position exists in which the pain is less. **The corollary matters too:** a patient made *worse* by distraction, or one with cord signs, is not a candidate for traction.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore** — as with 0.14, **exclude myelopathy** (clumsy hands, gait disturbance, upper motor neurone signs, bladder symptoms) before treating anything as simple radiculopathy, and re-check for red flags at every review. Progressive weakness or worsening deficit is a surgical referral, not another physiotherapy appointment.

**Alt:** Spurling's compression test (0.14); shoulder abduction (relief) sign; upper limb neurodynamic tests; the validated cervical radiculopathy test cluster; **MRI cervical spine**; EMG/NCS.
