## 0.1 Airway Compromise — Recognition and Localisation

**D:** Partial or complete obstruction to airflow anywhere from the nose to the terminal bronchioles, threatening oxygenation and ventilation.

**A/P:** Airflow through a narrowing becomes turbulent → turbulence generates sound → **the character and timing of the sound localises the level of obstruction**, because extrathoracic and intrathoracic airways behave differently across the respiratory cycle. Extrathoracic airways (above the thoracic inlet) collapse further during inspiration, when intraluminal pressure falls below atmospheric → inspiratory noise. Intrathoracic airways are compressed during expiration by rising pleural pressure → expiratory noise.

> [!tip] The noise tells you the level
> | Noise | Level | Timing | Typical causes |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Stertor** (snoring, coarse) | Above the larynx — nose, pharynx | Inspiratory | Reduced consciousness, tonsillar hypertrophy, OSA, soft tissue swelling |
> | **Stridor** (harsh, high-pitched) | Larynx, subglottis, trachea | Inspiratory if extrathoracic; expiratory or biphasic if intrathoracic or fixed | Croup, epiglottitis, foreign body, anaphylaxis, tumour |
> | **Wheeze** (musical, polyphonic) | Lower airways | Expiratory | Asthma, COPD, bronchiolitis |
> | **Grunting** | Not obstruction — expiration against a partly closed glottis to generate auto-PEEP | Expiratory | Any significant respiratory distress in a child; a serious sign |

> [!danger] Red flags demanding immediate senior and anaesthetic involvement
> Drooling and inability to swallow saliva · tripod or sniffing posture · refusal to lie flat · voice change or muffled voice · agitation or exhaustion · **a silent chest or diminishing stridor with worsening effort** — the noise disappears when airflow becomes insufficient to generate it, so a quietening patient is deteriorating, not improving · cyanosis, a late sign.

> [!danger] Do not distress the patient with suspected supraglottic obstruction
> In suspected epiglottitis or severe croup, particularly in a child: **no throat examination, no cannulation, no forced positioning, no separation from the parent.** Distress increases oxygen demand and can precipitate complete obstruction in an airway held open by muscle tone alone. Keep the child upright on the parent's lap, give oxygen if tolerated, and get anaesthetics and ENT to the bedside for examination in a controlled environment — theatre, not the resus bay.

**S/Smx:** Noisy breathing as above, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, intercostal and subcostal recession, nasal flaring, restlessness then drowsiness, and a rising then falling respiratory rate as fatigue develops.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment, prioritised over any test (*why:* obstruction progresses faster than investigations return, and a patient sent to radiology can obstruct in the scanner; *what:* level, severity, trajectory). Oxygen saturation (*why:* useful but reassuring for too long — it is preserved until obstruction is severe; *what:* hypoxia as a late sign). Nasendoscopy by ENT (*why:* directly visualises supraglottic and glottic pathology and is the definitive assessment of the upper airway; *what:* oedema, mass, cord movement, level of narrowing). Lateral soft tissue neck radiograph **only in a stable patient** (*why:* supportive for epiglottitis and retropharyngeal abscess, but must never delay airway management or move an unstable patient away from help; *what:* thumbprint sign, widened prevertebral soft tissue, steeple sign). CT neck with contrast in the stable patient (*why:* defines deep neck space collections, masses and tracheal narrowing; *what:* collection, mass, luminal calibre). ABG, reserved for the deteriorating (*why:* rising CO₂ indicates ventilatory failure, though obtaining it should not delay definitive airway management; *what:* hypercapnia).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen. Position of comfort — do not force supine. **Call for anaesthetic and ENT help at the moment of recognition.** Prepare for a difficult airway including a surgical airway. Treat the reversible cause: adrenaline for anaphylaxis, nebulised adrenaline and steroid for croup, removal of a foreign body.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
Definitive airway secured in a controlled setting — usually inhalational induction in theatre with ENT scrubbed and ready for a surgical airway. Cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.6.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Treat the underlying cause and document the airway difficulty encountered.

## 0.2 Acute Upper Airway Obstruction

**D:** Sudden obstruction at or above the level of the trachea, threatening complete airway loss.

**R/Causes:** Foreign body · anaphylaxis · angioedema, including the bradykinin-mediated form caused by ACE inhibitors and hereditary angioedema · epiglottitis and supraglottitis · deep neck space infection including Ludwig's angina and retropharyngeal abscess · **expanding post-operative neck haematoma** after thyroidectomy or carotid endarterectomy · inhalation and thermal airway injury · laryngeal trauma · bilateral vocal cord palsy · tumour with acute decompensation · reduced conscious state with loss of tone.

**A/P:** Oedema, mass or blood narrows the airway → resistance to flow rises steeply as radius falls, since flow varies with the fourth power of the radius → **a small further reduction in an already narrowed airway produces a disproportionate and often sudden deterioration.** This non-linearity is why patients appear to compensate and then collapse abruptly rather than declining smoothly.

> [!danger] Expanding post-operative neck haematoma
> After thyroidectomy or carotid endarterectomy, an expanding haematoma compresses the airway and obstructs venous drainage, causing laryngeal oedema that persists even once the clot is removed. **This is one of the few situations where a junior doctor should open the wound at the bedside** — remove the clips or sutures, evacuate the clot, and call for help simultaneously. Waiting for theatre has killed patients. Know where the clip removers are kept on your surgical ward before you need them.

> [!warning] ACE inhibitor angioedema does not behave like anaphylaxis
> It is **bradykinin-mediated, not histamine-mediated**, so adrenaline, antihistamines and steroids are largely ineffective — though adrenaline is still given where anaphylaxis cannot be excluded. It characteristically involves the lips, tongue and larynx without urticaria, can occur after years of uneventful use, and progresses over hours. The management is airway protection and cessation of the drug. `UNVERIFIED — the role and availability of specific bradykinin-pathway agents (icatibant, C1 esterase inhibitor) in Australian practice requires verification; cross-refer [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Hereditary angioedema.`

> [!danger] Inhalation injury — intubate early, not later
> Facial burns, singed nasal hair or eyebrows, carbonaceous sputum, soot in the oropharynx, hoarse voice, stridor, or burns sustained in an enclosed space. Airway oedema develops progressively over hours and an airway that is manageable now may be impossible in two hours. **Early elective intubation is the correct decision**, and deferring it is the recognised error. Cross-refer [[A7]] Burns.

**S/Smx:** Stridor, voice change or loss, drooling, dysphagia and odynophagia, tripod posture, agitation, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, and neck swelling or asymmetry.

**Ix:** Assessment at the bedside with anaesthetics and ENT present (*why:* the airway decision is clinical and urgent, and investigations that move the patient away from help are dangerous; *what:* level and severity). Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* directly visualises the supraglottis and cords, distinguishing oedema from mass from cord palsy, and is safe in a cooperative adult; *what:* supraglottic oedema, mass, cord movement). Wound and neck inspection with drain output review in the post-operative patient (*why:* identifies the expanding haematoma requiring immediate decompression; *what:* tense swelling, high drain output, then a drain that suddenly stops — an ominous sign of clot). Serum tryptase if anaphylaxis is possible (*why:* retrospective confirmation; *what:* acute rise — cross-refer [[F0.1]] 0.9). CT neck with contrast in the stable patient (*why:* defines deep neck space infection requiring drainage; *what:* rim-enhancing collection, airway deviation). C1 esterase inhibitor level and function where recurrent angioedema without urticaria (*why:* identifies hereditary angioedema, which requires entirely different treatment; *what:* low level or function).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, position of comfort, immediate anaesthetic and ENT involvement, and preparation for a surgical airway. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Open the wound for post-operative neck haematoma. Nebulised adrenaline may temporise laryngeal oedema. `UNVERIFIED — all doses from ASCIA and eTG.`

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Definitive airway in theatre. Drainage of collections. Cessation of the causative drug. Surgical exploration for haematoma.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the cause and the airway difficulty. Permanent medical alert and allergy documentation. ACE inhibitor angioedema is a class contraindication for life — document it prominently, since re-prescription is a recognised harm.

## 0.3 Chronic Upper Airway Obstruction

**D:** Progressive narrowing of the upper airway developing over weeks to years, often presenting only when a further insult tips it into acute failure.

**R/Causes:** Laryngeal, hypopharyngeal or thyroid malignancy · large or retrosternal goitre · **post-intubation or post-tracheostomy tracheal stenosis** · bilateral vocal cord palsy, most often after thyroid surgery · obstructive sleep apnoea · granulomatosis with polyangiitis · in children, laryngomalacia, subglottic stenosis and vascular rings.

**A/P:** Gradual narrowing permits physiological adaptation → the patient reduces activity, alters their breathing pattern and normalises their symptoms → **presentation is late and is often precipitated by a viral illness, secretions or sedation** that removes the remaining margin. This is why an apparently sudden presentation may have a long history behind it.

> [!warning] Tracheal stenosis is under-recognised
> A patient treated repeatedly for "asthma" that does not respond to bronchodilators, with **biphasic stridor or a fixed monophonic wheeze** and a history of previous intubation or tracheostomy, has tracheal stenosis until proven otherwise. Flow-volume loops distinguish fixed extrathoracic, variable extrathoracic and intrathoracic obstruction, and are useful precisely when the diagnosis is being missed.

> [!tip] Bilateral vocal cord palsy after thyroid surgery
> Unilateral palsy gives hoarseness. **Bilateral palsy gives a near-normal voice with stridor**, because the cords sit in the midline — which is exactly why it is missed. New stridor after thyroidectomy with a preserved voice is bilateral recurrent laryngeal nerve injury until excluded.

**S/Smx:** Progressive exertional dyspnoea, stridor initially only on exertion, voice change, dysphagia, cough, reduced exercise tolerance, weight loss and neck mass in malignancy, and snoring with witnessed apnoeas in OSA.

**Ix:** Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* the definitive assessment of the larynx and cord movement, and it is an outpatient procedure; *what:* mass, cord palsy, narrowing level). CT neck and chest (*why:* defines the level, length and cause of narrowing and stages malignancy; *what:* stenosis, mass, extrinsic compression). Flow-volume loops (*why:* characterises fixed versus variable and extrathoracic versus intrathoracic obstruction, which no other pulmonary function measure does, and identifies the "asthma" that is not asthma; *what:* flattened inspiratory or expiratory limb). Thyroid function and ultrasound where goitre is present (*why:* determines whether the mass is functional and whether it extends retrosternally; *what:* nodule, retrosternal extension). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* confirms and grades OSA and determines CPAP need; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index — cross-refer [[13_05b_ENT_-_Stridor__Croup__Epiglottitis__Laryngomalacia__OSA]]). Biopsy of any mass (*why:* directs oncological management; *what:* histology).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
If presenting acutely decompensated, manage as per 0.2. Otherwise oxygen, avoid sedatives, and refer.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific: tumour resection or oncological management, tracheal dilatation or resection, cord lateralisation or tracheostomy for bilateral palsy, thyroidectomy for compressive goitre, CPAP for OSA.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Flag the difficult airway prominently in the medical record**, since these patients present for unrelated surgery and an unanticipated difficult airway in that setting is dangerous.

## 0.4 Lower Airway Obstruction

**D:** Obstruction distal to the trachea, at bronchial or bronchiolar level.

**R/Causes:** Asthma and COPD (the great majority) · bronchiolitis in infants · **inhaled foreign body** · anaphylaxis · endobronchial tumour · mucus plugging · aspiration.

**A/P:** Narrowing of small airways → expiratory flow limitation, since intrathoracic airways are compressed during expiration → air trapping and hyperinflation → the diaphragm flattens and works at mechanical disadvantage → increased work of breathing and V/Q mismatch.

> [!danger] Inhaled foreign body
> **Unilateral wheeze or reduced air entry in a child is a foreign body until proven otherwise**, particularly with a history of choking while eating — though the choking episode is unwitnessed or forgotten in a substantial proportion. Peanuts and small toy parts are typical. The classic radiographic finding is **unilateral hyperinflation on an expiratory film**, from a ball-valve effect that lets air in but not out; the inspiratory film may be entirely normal. A normal chest radiograph does not exclude it, and bronchoscopy is both the diagnostic and therapeutic step.

> [!warning] Not all wheeze is asthma
> Monophonic wheeze localised to one area suggests fixed obstruction — foreign body or tumour — rather than the polyphonic widespread wheeze of asthma. Wheeze that has never responded to a bronchodilator deserves a different diagnosis rather than a higher dose.

**S/Smx:** Expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiratory phase, cough, dyspnoea, hyperinflated chest, accessory muscle use. In infants: recession, grunting, poor feeding, apnoea.

**Ix:** Auscultation for the pattern and symmetry of wheeze (*why:* asymmetry is the finding that redirects you from asthma toward foreign body or tumour; *what:* unilateral versus widespread). CXR, with inspiratory and expiratory films where foreign body is suspected (*why:* the expiratory film reveals the air trapping that the inspiratory film conceals, and most inhaled objects are radiolucent; *what:* unilateral hyperinflation, mediastinal shift, collapse). Oxygen saturation and, in severe cases, blood gas (*why:* severity and rising CO₂; *what:* hypoxia, hypercapnia). Peak flow or spirometry where feasible (*why:* objectively grades reversible obstruction; *what:* reduced values with bronchodilator response). Flexible or rigid bronchoscopy (*why:* diagnostic and therapeutic for foreign body and endobronchial lesions; *what:* object, tumour, plug). Nasopharyngeal aspirate for viral PCR in infants (*why:* confirms bronchiolitis and supports cohorting; *what:* RSV and other viruses).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Bronchodilators and steroids for asthma and COPD — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.1 and 0.2. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Urgent ENT or thoracic referral for suspected foreign body — **do not attempt blind finger sweeps or postural drainage in a stable patient with a partial obstruction**, as this can convert partial to complete obstruction.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Bronchoscopic removal of foreign body. Disease-specific management otherwise.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Preventer optimisation, inhaler technique, action plan, and choking prevention advice for families of young children.

## 0.5 Acute Stridor

**D:** New harsh inspiratory or biphasic noise indicating laryngeal, subglottic or tracheal narrowing.

> [!tip] Differential by age — the highest-yield framing
> **Neonate:** laryngomalacia (usually chronic, positional, improves prone), subglottic stenosis, vocal cord palsy, vascular ring, laryngeal web.
> **6 months – 3 years:** **croup** is the commonest by a wide margin · inhaled foreign body · bacterial tracheitis · retropharyngeal abscess · anaphylaxis.
> **Any child:** epiglottitis — now rare in immunised populations following Hib vaccination, but consider it in the unimmunised · anaphylaxis · burns and inhalation injury.
> **Adult:** anaphylaxis and angioedema · **adult supraglottitis, which is now more common than paediatric epiglottitis** · tumour · post-extubation oedema · vocal cord dysfunction · deep neck space infection.

> [!info] Croup versus epiglottitis
> | | Croup | Epiglottitis |
> |---|---|---|
> | Onset | Gradual over 1–2 days, worse at night | Rapid, hours |
> | Prodrome | Coryza | Little or none |
> | Cough | **Barking, seal-like** | Absent or minimal |
> | Voice | Hoarse | **Muffled, "hot potato"** |
> | Drooling | No | **Yes** |
> | Posture | Any | **Tripod, sitting forward** |
> | Appearance | Usually well between spasms | Toxic, still, frightened |
> | Action | Steroid, nebulised adrenaline if severe | **Do not examine; theatre with ENT and anaesthetics** |

> [!warning] Vocal cord dysfunction
> Inspiratory stridor with normal oxygen saturation, no response to bronchodilators, and often a history of "asthma" that never improves. Frequently exercise- or stress-related, and diagnosed on nasendoscopy during an episode. It is a genuine and under-diagnosed cause, and treating it as asthma leads to years of unnecessary steroids.

**S/Smx:** Inspiratory stridor if extrathoracic, biphasic if fixed or subglottic. Associated features per the table. Severity is graded on stridor at rest versus on exertion, recession, agitation, and oxygen saturation.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment without distressing the patient (*why:* examination can precipitate complete obstruction in supraglottic disease; *what:* severity grade, level). Oxygen saturation obtained non-invasively if tolerated (*why:* preserved until late, so its normality is not reassurance; *what:* hypoxia). Flexible nasendoscopy in a controlled setting (*why:* directly visualises the level and cause; *what:* epiglottic swelling, cord movement, mass). Lateral neck radiograph **only in a stable patient and never in place of airway management** (*why:* supportive for epiglottitis and retropharyngeal abscess; *what:* thumbprint sign, widened prevertebral tissue). Inspiratory and expiratory CXR where foreign body is suspected (*why:* reveals air trapping; *what:* unilateral hyperinflation). Blood cultures where bacterial tracheitis or epiglottitis is suspected, **taken after the airway is secured** (*why:* organism identification, but venepuncture distresses a child and can precipitate obstruction; *what:* organism). Immunisation history (*why:* Hib status materially changes the probability of epiglottitis; *what:* completed schedule or not).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Keep the patient calm and upright with a parent if a child. Oxygen if tolerated without distress. **Corticosteroid for croup**, with nebulised adrenaline for moderate to severe. Adrenaline for anaphylaxis. For suspected epiglettitis, bacterial tracheitis or severe obstruction: senior anaesthetic and ENT to the bedside, and airway secured in theatre. `UNVERIFIED — dexamethasone and prednisolone dosing for croup, nebulised adrenaline dose and repeat interval, and severity grading criteria are omitted; obtain from RCH and local paediatric guidelines.`

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific. Antibiotics for bacterial causes. Bronchoscopy for foreign body. Drainage of abscess.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Croup is recurrent in some children and parents should have written advice on recognition and when to return. Immunisation catch-up where incomplete. Speech pathology referral for vocal cord dysfunction.

## Acute Stridor

> **Note on tier and overlap:** the bare topic **Stridor** is Tier 1 and was **skipped** on a header match (see the skip table). **Acute Stridor** is a separate Tier 2 list topic that matched no header, so it is built here. If your review finds the existing corpus stridor entries do not carry the airway-emergency content, this entry is the material to merge.

- **D:** a harsh, high-pitched noise from turbulent airflow through a **narrowed upper airway**. **Stridor means the airway is obstructed, and the volume of the noise is not the measure of severity — a quietening stridor in a tiring patient is deterioration, not improvement.**
- **The localising value of the sound:** **inspiratory stridor** suggests obstruction above or at the vocal cords (supraglottic/glottic); **biphasic** suggests obstruction at the glottis or subglottis; **expiratory stridor or wheeze** suggests a lower tracheal or bronchial cause.
- **DDx:**
  - **Children:** **croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)** — barking cough, coryzal prodrome, worse at night, the commonest cause; **epiglottitis/supraglottitis** — rapid onset, high fever, drooling, muffled voice, tripod posture, minimal cough, and **now uncommon where Hib immunisation is high but not eliminated, and still occurring in unimmunised children and in adults**; **bacterial tracheitis**; **inhaled foreign body** — sudden onset in a previously well toddler; **anaphylaxis**; **retropharyngeal abscess**; **laryngomalacia** (chronic, positional, in infants); subglottic stenosis after previous intubation; **burns and inhalation injury**.
  - **Adults:** **anaphylaxis and angioedema — including ACE inhibitor–induced**; **epiglottitis**; **laryngeal tumour**; **bilateral vocal cord palsy** (often after thyroid surgery); **post-extubation oedema**; deep neck space infection including **Ludwig's angina**; **inhalation and thermal airway injury**; foreign body; external compression by a large goitre or mediastinal mass; **vocal cord dysfunction** (a mimic, and a diagnosis of exclusion).
- **Focused Hx — kept brief, because assessment must not delay airway management:** speed of onset; **fever**; **drooling and inability to swallow saliva**; voice change; **choking episode**; **allergen exposure, sting, food, or new drug**; **ACE inhibitor use**; burns, smoke or steam exposure; **immunisation status**; previous intubation or neck surgery; known airway abnormality; and previous similar episodes.
- **Examination:** **this is where the greatest harm is done, so the first rule is what not to do.**
  - **Do not distress the patient — particularly a child.** **Do not examine the throat with a tongue depressor, do not lie them flat, do not attempt cannulation or venepuncture, and do not separate a child from their parent**, if epiglottitis or severe obstruction is possible: distress can precipitate complete obstruction.
  - **Observe from a distance:** work of breathing, respiratory rate, **tracheal tug, intercostal and subcostal recession, nasal flaring**, position adopted (**tripoding, sniffing position**), drooling, colour, conscious level, ability to speak or cry.
  - **SpO₂ if it can be obtained without distress — and remember it is a late sign.**
  - **Assess the character of the stridor and whether it is present at rest** (stridor at rest indicates more severe obstruction than stridor only on exertion or crying).
  - Look for **urticaria, angioedema, lip and tongue swelling**, and for **facial or oral burns, soot in the nostrils or mouth, and singed nasal hairs**.
  - **Full examination happens after the airway is secured or the patient is stabilised**, not before.
- **First-line Ix:** **stridor is a clinical diagnosis and the immediate priority is the airway, not investigation.** Nothing should delay senior, anaesthetic and ENT involvement. Once safe: **lateral soft-tissue neck X-ray** (the "thumbprint" sign of epiglottitis, the "steeple" sign of croup) — **of limited use, frequently non-diagnostic, and never to be obtained by sending an unstable patient to radiology**; **flexible nasendoscopy** by ENT; **CT neck** where a mass or deep space infection is suspected and the airway is secure; **FBC, CRP, blood cultures** once the airway is safe; **arterial blood gas** where ventilatory failure is suspected — **but a normal gas does not reassure in upper airway obstruction**.

> [!danger] **The single most important principle: in suspected epiglottitis or severe upper airway obstruction, call for the people who can secure an airway — senior emergency, anaesthetics and ENT — before doing anything else, and keep the patient calm and upright with a parent if a child.** Definitive airway management is planned for theatre or a controlled environment, not attempted on the ward.
> **Drug doses are deliberately omitted throughout** — corticosteroid and nebulised adrenaline regimens for croup, and adrenaline for anaphylaxis, are all protocol- and weight-specific, all differ between Australian services, and all are egress-blocked here. **Use your local paediatric or adult airway protocol; do not reconstruct a weight-based dose from memory in this presentation.**

## 0.6 Chronic Stridor

**D:** Persistent or recurrent stridor over weeks to months.

**R/Causes:** In infants, **laryngomalacia is by far the commonest** — inspiratory stridor from birth or the first weeks, worse when supine, feeding, crying or with intercurrent illness, and better prone. Also: subglottic stenosis (particularly following neonatal intubation), vocal cord palsy, vascular ring or sling, subglottic haemangioma (which may enlarge over the first months), laryngeal cleft or web, recurrent respiratory papillomatosis. In adults: tumour, tracheal stenosis, bilateral cord palsy, external compression by goitre or mediastinal mass.

**A/P:** A structurally narrowed or dynamically collapsing airway produces turbulent flow at rest or on exertion. In laryngomalacia, immature and floppy supraglottic tissues are drawn inward during inspiration → intermittent obstruction that improves as the cartilage stiffens with growth.

> [!warning] Which infant with stridor needs investigation rather than reassurance
> Laryngomalacia is common and usually self-limiting, but reassurance is only safe when the pattern fits. **Investigate if there is: poor weight gain or feeding difficulty, cyanotic episodes, apnoea, biphasic or expiratory stridor rather than inspiratory, stridor that is worsening rather than plateauing, a weak or absent cry, or onset after the neonatal period.** Any of these warrants ENT referral and nasendoscopy rather than watchful waiting.

**S/Smx:** Persistent stridor with the positional and feeding pattern described, feeding difficulty, failure to thrive in significant cases, and in adults progressive exertional dyspnoea and voice change.

**Ix:** Flexible nasendoscopy (*why:* the diagnostic test — visualises the supraglottis dynamically and confirms laryngomalacia or identifies an alternative; *what:* omega-shaped epiglottis with inspiratory collapse, or a structural lesion). Microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy under anaesthesia (*why:* assesses below the cords, which nasendoscopy cannot, and identifies subglottic and tracheal pathology; *what:* stenosis, haemangioma, cleft). Growth chart plotting (*why:* faltering growth converts a benign-sounding diagnosis into one requiring intervention; *what:* weight trajectory crossing centiles). Sleep study or overnight oximetry (*why:* quantifies nocturnal obstruction and desaturation; *what:* desaturation episodes). Echocardiography and CT or MR angiography where vascular ring is suspected (*why:* identifies a surgically correctable extrinsic compression; *what:* vascular ring or sling). CT neck and chest in adults (*why:* identifies tumour or compression; *what:* mass, luminal narrowing).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Rarely needed. Manage intercurrent illness that decompensates the airway.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Observation for uncomplicated laryngomalacia, which typically resolves over the first one to two years. Supraglottoplasty for severe cases. Surgical or endoscopic management of structural lesions.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Growth and feeding monitoring, parental education on when to return, and ENT follow-up.

## 0.7 Noisy Breathing — The Discriminating Approach

**D:** An undifferentiated presentation, common in general practice and paediatrics, where the diagnostic task is to identify which noise is present before considering causes.

> [!tip] Work through it in this order
> **1. When in the cycle?** Inspiratory, expiratory or both.
> **2. What quality?** Coarse and snoring (stertor), harsh and high-pitched (stridor), musical (wheeze), or a short expiratory grunt.
> **3. Where is it loudest?** Over the neck suggests upper airway; over the chest suggests lower.
> **4. Is it positional?** Improved prone suggests laryngomalacia; worse supine suggests pharyngeal collapse or OSA.
> **5. Is the patient distressed?** A noisy but comfortable, feeding, growing child is a very different problem from a noisy child with recession and poor feeding.

> [!warning] The two errors this framework prevents
> **Calling stridor "wheeze"** and treating an upper airway obstruction with bronchodilators, which delays the correct diagnosis and can be fatal in an evolving obstruction.
> **Dismissing grunting as noise.** Grunting is not an airway sound at all — it is expiration against a partially closed glottis to generate auto-PEEP, and in a child it signals significant respiratory distress or sepsis. A grunting infant needs urgent assessment.

**S/Smx:** As above, with the associated features determining urgency: feeding, growth, sleep disruption, exercise tolerance, voice, and the presence of recession or distress.

**Ix:** Structured history including a recording of the noise on the parent's phone (*why:* the noise is frequently absent in clinic, and a recording is genuinely diagnostic in distinguishing stertor from stridor from wheeze — this is a practical and underused step; *what:* character and timing). Growth chart (*why:* faltering growth signals a significant airway problem regardless of how benign the noise sounds; *what:* trajectory). Oxygen saturation, awake and ideally during sleep (*why:* identifies the nocturnal desaturation that daytime assessment misses; *what:* desaturation). Flexible nasendoscopy where stridor or stertor is persistent (*why:* the definitive upper airway assessment; *what:* level and cause). CXR where lower airway pathology is suspected (*why:* foreign body, consolidation, cardiomegaly; *what:* findings). Sleep study where OSA is suspected (*why:* confirms and grades; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Only where distress or hypoxia is present — then manage per the level identified.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific per 0.3 to 0.6.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Reassurance with clear return criteria where benign, and referral where the warning features in 0.6 are present.

## 0.8 Tracheostomy and Laryngectomy Emergency

**D:** Airway emergency in a patient with a surgical airway — obstruction, displacement, bleeding, or failure to ventilate.

> [!danger] The single most important question: is there a patent upper airway?
> **Tracheostomy** — the larynx is intact and the upper airway is patent. The patient **can** be oxygenated via the face and **can** usually be intubated orally.
> **Laryngectomy** — the larynx has been removed and the trachea is brought to the skin as an end stoma. **There is no connection between the mouth and the trachea.** Oxygen applied to the face achieves nothing, oral intubation is impossible, and all airway management must be via the stoma.
> Getting this wrong is fatal. Australian and UK hospitals use **bed-head signs** stating which the patient has, along with tube type and size — look for it, and if there is no sign, treat the stoma as the airway while checking.

> [!info] The emergency sequence
> Based on the National Tracheostomy Safety Project algorithms, which are the standard resource and should be the version you follow. `UNVERIFIED — obtain the current NTSP tracheostomy and laryngectomy emergency algorithms and your hospital's version; the sequence below is the principle, not a substitute for the algorithm.`
> **1.** Call for help — anaesthetics, ENT, ICU.
> **2.** Apply oxygen to **both** the face and the stoma in a tracheostomy patient; to the stoma only in a laryngectomy patient.
> **3.** Assess patency — look, listen, feel, and use waveform capnography, which is the most reliable indicator of a patent and correctly positioned tube.
> **4.** **Remove the inner cannula** — blockage by dried secretions is the commonest cause and this alone resolves many events.
> **5.** Pass a suction catheter. If it passes, suction. If it will not pass, the tube is blocked or displaced.
> **6.** Deflate the cuff — this may allow the patient to breathe around the tube if the upper airway is patent.
> **7.** If still obstructed, **remove the tube.** Then oxygenate and ventilate via the face for a tracheostomy patient, or via the stoma for a laryngectomy patient.

> [!warning] Bleeding from a tracheostomy
> Minor bleeding is common. **Significant or sentinel bleeding may herald a tracheo-innominate artery fistula** — a rare, catastrophic and time-critical event, most likely between roughly one and six weeks post-insertion. Any brisk bleed from a tracheostomy demands immediate senior surgical and ENT involvement, not observation. `UNVERIFIED — the emergency manoeuvres described for tracheo-innominate fistula, including cuff overinflation and digital compression, require verification against a current surgical source.`

> [!danger] A displaced tube in a fresh tracheostomy
> A tracheostomy less than about a week old has an immature tract that has not yet formed a stable channel. **Blind reinsertion can create a false passage into the pretracheal tissues**, which is often fatal and looks initially like a successful placement. In a fresh tracheostomy, reinsertion should be attempted only by someone competent to do so, ideally over a guide, and oxygenation via the upper airway is the safer immediate route where anatomy permits.

**S/Smx:** Respiratory distress, inability to pass a suction catheter, absent or abnormal capnography trace, visible tube displacement, surgical emphysema, bleeding, agitation, desaturation, and reduced or absent air movement at the tube.

**Ix:** Waveform capnography (*why:* the most reliable indicator that the tube is patent and in the trachea, and it distinguishes a blocked tube from a displaced one faster than any other test; *what:* trace present or absent). Attempted passage of a suction catheter (*why:* a simple bedside test of tube patency that also clears secretions; *what:* passes freely, or obstructs). Direct inspection and removal of the inner cannula (*why:* identifies the commonest and most easily reversible cause; *what:* secretions, crusting). Fibreoptic scope through the tube where available (*why:* confirms position within the trachea and excludes a false passage; *what:* tracheal rings and carina visible). CXR after any tube change or reinsertion (*why:* confirms position and excludes pneumothorax and surgical emphysema; *what:* tube position, pneumothorax). Bed-head sign and operation note (*why:* establishes tracheostomy versus laryngectomy, tube type, size and whether the tract is mature — the information every subsequent decision depends on; *what:* airway type and history).

### 0.8.1 Mx – Immediate
Follow the NTSP algorithm. Call for help first. Oxygen to the right places. Inner cannula out, suction catheter, cuff down, tube out if needed.

### 0.8.2 Mx – Definitive
Tube replacement by a competent operator, or an alternative airway. Surgical management of bleeding.

### 0.8.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Ensure the bed-head sign, emergency box and appropriate spare tubes are at the bedside at all times — checking this on your ward round is a genuinely useful intern habit. Tracheostomy care education for patient and carers. Cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.6.

## 0.1 Respiratory Failure — Type 1 versus Type 2

**D:** Failure of the respiratory system to maintain adequate gas exchange, classified by whether carbon dioxide is normal or elevated.

**A/P:** The lung performs two separable jobs. **Oxygenation** depends on matching ventilation to perfusion across the alveolar-capillary membrane. **Ventilation** — the clearance of CO₂ — depends almost entirely on minute volume, since CO₂ diffuses far more readily than oxygen. The two fail independently, and which one has failed determines the treatment.

> [!tip] The classification
> **Type 1 — hypoxaemic.** Low PaO₂ with a normal or **low** PaCO₂. The lung's gas exchange surface has failed but the pump is working, often working harder than normal, which is why CO₂ is blown off. Causes: pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, ARDS, pneumothorax, asthma early.
> **Type 2 — hypercapnic.** Low PaO₂ with a **raised** PaCO₂. The pump has failed — inadequate minute ventilation regardless of how good the lung is. Causes: COPD, severe asthma late, opioid and sedative toxicity, neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease, obesity hypoventilation, exhaustion from any cause.
> `UNVERIFIED — the specific PaO₂ and PaCO₂ threshold values defining each type require verification against a current source; no numbers are stated here.`

> [!warning] Type 1 becomes Type 2 when the patient tires
> This is not a fixed category. A patient with pneumonia hyperventilating to maintain oxygenation has Type 1 failure with a low CO₂. When they exhaust, the CO₂ rises. **The transition is the deterioration**, and it is why a "normalising" CO₂ in a distressed patient is a warning rather than reassurance — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.1.

> [!info] Acute versus chronic — the bicarbonate answers it
> Renal compensation for hypercapnia takes days. So a patient with a high CO₂ and a **high bicarbonate with a near-normal pH** has chronic retention. A high CO₂ with a **normal bicarbonate and a low pH** is acute. Acute-on-chronic shows a raised bicarbonate that is nonetheless insufficient, leaving an acidotic pH. This single comparison determines whether the patient needs NIV now.

> [!tip] Five mechanisms of hypoxaemia — and the one that matters at the bedside
> **V/Q mismatch** — commonest, improves with supplemental oxygen.
> **Shunt** — blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely (consolidation, collapse, ARDS, intracardiac). **Does not correct with supplemental oxygen**, and this refractoriness is the discriminating clinical observation.
> **Hypoventilation** — raised CO₂ with a **normal alveolar-arterial gradient**; the lung is fine, the pump is not.
> **Diffusion impairment** — interstitial disease, usually exertional.
> **Low inspired oxygen** — altitude, or a disconnected oxygen supply, which is worth excluding before anything clever.
> `UNVERIFIED — the alveolar gas equation, the A-a gradient formula and its age-adjusted normal range require verification.`

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, cyanosis (late and unreliable). Hypercapnia specifically: drowsiness, confusion, headache, warm peripheries with a bounding pulse, and asterixis.

**Ix:** ABG (*why:* the only test that separates Type 1 from Type 2 and acute from chronic, since oximetry cannot detect CO₂ at all; *what:* PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH, bicarbonate). VBG where arterial sampling is difficult (*why:* adequate for pH, bicarbonate and trending CO₂, though unreliable for PaO₂; *what:* pH, bicarbonate). Oxygen saturation with the inspired oxygen concentration documented alongside it (*why:* a saturation figure is meaningless without knowing what oxygen the patient is on — 94% on room air and 94% on 15 litres are entirely different patients, and this is routinely omitted from documentation; *what:* saturation with FiO₂). CXR (*why:* separates the parenchymal causes of Type 1 failure from the clear-lunged causes such as PE and pump failure; *what:* consolidation, oedema, pneumothorax, effusion, hyperinflation). ECG (*why:* right heart strain, arrhythmia, ischaemia; *what:* strain pattern). FBC (*why:* anaemia reduces oxygen content even with a normal saturation; *what:* haemoglobin). Serial FVC where neuromuscular cause is suspected (*why:* gases are normal until abrupt decompensation in this group; *what:* declining FVC — cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.5).

### 0.1.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen titrated to a target appropriate to the patient. Identify which type of failure and treat accordingly: oxygen and treatment of the lung pathology in Type 1, **ventilatory support** in Type 2.

### 0.1.2 Mx – Definitive
NIV for Type 2 failure with acidosis. Invasive ventilation where NIV fails or is inappropriate. Cause-specific treatment.

### 0.1.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Assessment for home NIV or long-term oxygen therapy where failure persists — see 0.3.

## 0.2 Acute Respiratory Failure

**D:** Respiratory failure developing over minutes to hours, without time for metabolic compensation.

> [!tip] Cause by anatomical level — the framework that avoids missing things
> **Airway:** obstruction, foreign body, anaphylaxis, epiglottitis — cross-refer [[A2]].
> **Lung:** pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, pneumothorax, ARDS, asthma, COPD, haemorrhage.
> **Pump — chest wall and muscles:** flail chest, neuromuscular disease, diaphragmatic weakness, massive obesity, tense abdomen.
> **Drive — central:** opioids, sedatives, raised intracranial pressure, brainstem lesion, high cervical cord injury.
> **Circulation:** shock of any cause with inadequate oxygen delivery despite normal lungs.

**A/P:** Any of the above reduces alveolar ventilation, impairs gas exchange, or increases the work of breathing beyond what the respiratory muscles can sustain → **the common final pathway is muscle fatigue**, and fatigue converts a compensating patient into an arresting one over a short interval.

> [!danger] The signs of impending arrest
> Exhaustion. Paradoxical abdominal movement — the abdomen moving inward on inspiration as the fatigued diaphragm is drawn up. Falling respiratory rate in a previously tachypnoeic patient. Reduced conscious state. A silent chest. Rising CO₂. **Any of these means the airway should be secured now rather than after another trial of therapy.**

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, intercostal recession, inability to speak in sentences, agitation then drowsiness, sweating, cyanosis.

**Ix:** ABG (*why:* classifies the failure and identifies the ventilatory failure that oximetry conceals; *what:* PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH). CXR (*why:* the highest-yield single imaging test for the parenchymal causes, and it identifies the pneumothorax that requires immediate decompression; *what:* consolidation, oedema, pneumothorax, collapse). ECG (*why:* arrhythmia, ischaemia and right heart strain; *what:* findings). Bedside lung ultrasound where available (*why:* faster than radiography at the bedside and distinguishes pneumothorax, effusion, consolidation and interstitial oedema without moving the patient; *what:* absent lung sliding, B-lines, consolidation). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* separates cardiogenic from non-cardiogenic causes and identifies RV strain from PE; *what:* LV function, RV dilatation). FBC, UEC, CRP, lactate (*why:* infection, organ dysfunction and perfusion; *what:* leucocytosis, raised lactate). CTPA where PE is suspected and the patient is stable enough (*why:* the diagnostic test; *what:* filling defect). Toxicology and bedside glucose (*why:* opioid toxicity and hypoglycaemia are rapidly reversible causes; *what:* pinpoint pupils, low glucose).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
A–E. Oxygen to target. Sit upright unless contraindicated. Treat the identified cause. **Decompress a tension pneumothorax on clinical grounds without waiting for imaging.** NIV or invasive ventilation as indicated. Escalate early.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific, with ICU involvement where support is required.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Rehabilitation, and reassessment of chronic disease management where the failure was an exacerbation of established disease.

## 0.3 Chronic and Acute-on-Chronic Respiratory Failure

**D:** Persistent inadequate gas exchange, with the acute-on-chronic form being an acute deterioration superimposed on established compensation.

**R/Causes:** COPD · obesity hypoventilation syndrome · neuromuscular disease including motor neurone disease and muscular dystrophy · kyphoscoliosis and chest wall restriction · interstitial lung disease · bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis · central hypoventilation.

**A/P:** Chronic hypercapnia → renal bicarbonate retention over days → pH returns toward normal while CO₂ stays high → the patient functions with a compensated respiratory acidosis and a chronically raised bicarbonate. **A superimposed insult — infection, sedation, excessive oxygen, fluid overload — raises CO₂ faster than compensation can follow**, and the pH falls. Chronic hypoxaemia additionally drives pulmonary vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → right ventricular hypertrophy and failure, which is cor pulmonale.

> [!tip] Recognising acute-on-chronic on a single gas
> A raised CO₂ with a **raised bicarbonate and an acidotic pH** is acute-on-chronic — the bicarbonate proves chronicity, the low pH proves an acute change. This is the patient who benefits most from NIV.

> [!warning] Long-term oxygen therapy has strict criteria and is not symptom relief
> LTOT improves survival in chronic hypoxaemia and is prescribed on measured criteria after a period of stability, not during an exacerbation. **It is not given for breathlessness in a non-hypoxaemic patient**, where it does not help. Smoking while on home oxygen is both a safety hazard and a contraindication. `UNVERIFIED — PaO₂ criteria, the required period of stability, and the daily hours of use required for benefit all need verification against current Australian respiratory guidance and the funding criteria in South Australia.`

> [!info] Home NIV
> Considered in chronic hypercapnic failure from neuromuscular disease, chest wall disease, obesity hypoventilation and selected COPD. Assessment is by a respiratory or sleep service.

**S/Smx:** Chronic exertional dyspnoea, morning headache and daytime somnolence from nocturnal hypercapnia, poor sleep quality, ankle oedema and raised JVP in cor pulmonale, weight loss in advanced COPD, cyanosis.

**Ix:** ABG with bicarbonate (*why:* establishes chronicity and the baseline against which acute changes are judged; *what:* raised CO₂ with raised bicarbonate). Overnight oximetry or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring (*why:* nocturnal hypoventilation precedes daytime hypercapnia and is the point at which home NIV is considered; *what:* nocturnal desaturation, rising CO₂). Spirometry with lung volumes and diffusing capacity (*why:* characterises obstructive versus restrictive physiology and quantifies severity; *what:* FEV1/FVC ratio, reduced volumes, reduced DLCO). Echocardiography (*why:* identifies pulmonary hypertension and right heart strain; *what:* RV dilatation, estimated pulmonary artery pressure). FBC (*why:* secondary polycythaemia from chronic hypoxaemia; *what:* raised haemoglobin and haematocrit). BMI and, where relevant, sleep study (*why:* obesity hypoventilation is common, under-diagnosed and treatable; *what:* apnoea-hypopnoea index, nocturnal hypoventilation). CXR and CT chest (*why:* defines the underlying structural disease; *what:* hyperinflation, fibrosis, bronchiectasis).

### 0.3.1 Mx – Immediate
For acute-on-chronic deterioration: controlled oxygen to target with a repeat gas after any change, bronchodilators, treatment of the precipitant, and **early NIV for hypercapnic acidosis.** Cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.2.

### 0.3.2 Mx – Definitive
Optimise the underlying disease. Assess for home NIV and LTOT once stable.

### 0.3.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, nutritional support, and — importantly — **advance care planning.** Patients with severe chronic respiratory failure will face decisions about intubation, and having that conversation in clinic is far better than having it at three in the morning during an exacerbation.

## 0.4 Respiratory Arrest

**D:** Cessation of effective breathing with a pulse still present.

**A/P:** Ventilation stops → PaO₂ falls and PaCO₂ rises → profound hypoxaemia and acidosis → **bradycardia, then pulseless electrical activity, then asystole.** The interval between respiratory arrest and cardiac arrest is short but real, and intervention within it has a far better outcome than intervention after cardiac arrest has occurred. **This is why respiratory arrest is the most rewarding arrest to recognise** — and why paediatric outcomes hinge on it, since most paediatric cardiac arrest is asphyxial in origin.

> [!tip] The rapidly reversible causes — check these while ventilating
> **Opioid toxicity** — pinpoint pupils, low rate before the arrest. **Hypoglycaemia.** **Airway obstruction** — foreign body, oedema, vomit. **Tension pneumothorax.** **Seizure** with post-ictal apnoea. **High cervical cord injury.** **Severe bronchospasm.** Each has a specific and fast intervention.

> [!danger] Effective bag-mask ventilation is the intervention
> Not intubation, which takes time and skill and can wait. **A two-person bag-mask technique with an oropharyngeal airway, good seal and jaw thrust ventilates most patients**, and the commonest reasons it fails are a poor seal and inadequate airway positioning rather than anything requiring advanced equipment. Practise the two-person technique; it is the skill that matters most here.

**S/Smx:** Absent or agonal breathing with a palpable pulse. Cyanosis. Unresponsiveness. Bradycardia developing.

**Ix:** None before intervention. Once ventilating: bedside glucose (*why:* rapidly reversible; *what:* hypoglycaemia). Waveform capnography (*why:* confirms effective ventilation and airway position; *what:* trace). ABG or VBG (*why:* quantifies the hypoxia and acidosis and guides ongoing support; *what:* pH, pCO₂, pO₂). ECG (*why:* bradycardia progressing toward PEA; *what:* rhythm). CXR after airway placement (*why:* tube position, pneumothorax; *what:* findings). Toxicology and pupil examination (*why:* opioid toxicity is the commonest reversible cause on a ward; *what:* pinpoint pupils).

### 0.4.1 Mx – Immediate
Call for help. Open the airway, bag-mask ventilate with oxygen, insert an airway adjunct. Check pulse — if absent, this is cardiac arrest and follow [[A1]] 0.4. Bedside glucose. Naloxone for suspected opioid toxicity — cross-refer [[F0.1]] 0.5. Needle decompression for tension pneumothorax.

### 0.4.2 Mx – Definitive
Secure the airway if ventilation cannot be sustained. Treat the cause. ICU.

### 0.4.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Root cause review — most in-hospital respiratory arrests follow recognisable deterioration or a prescribing error, and both are preventable. Cross-refer [[A1]] 0.1.

## 0.5 Acute Bronchospasm

**D:** Acute reversible narrowing of the lower airways from smooth muscle constriction.

**R/Causes:** Asthma and COPD exacerbation · **anaphylaxis** · drug-induced — beta-blockers including ophthalmic drops, NSAIDs in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, adenosine, contrast media · aspiration · irritant or smoke inhalation · **peri-anaesthetic bronchospasm** on induction or intubation · cold air and exercise.

**A/P:** Trigger → mast cell degranulation or direct irritant receptor stimulation → bronchial smooth muscle contraction with mucosal oedema and mucus secretion → expiratory flow limitation → air trapping and increased work of breathing.

> [!warning] Bronchospasm is a sign, not a diagnosis
> **Wheeze in a patient with no history of airways disease is not asthma until you have excluded anaphylaxis, pulmonary oedema and foreign body.** "Cardiac asthma" — wheeze from pulmonary oedema — is common in older patients and responds to nitrates and NIV, not to salbutamol. Treating it as asthma delays the correct treatment.

> [!danger] Peri-anaesthetic bronchospasm
> Bronchospasm on induction or shortly after intubation should raise anaphylaxis to a neuromuscular blocking agent or antibiotic, alongside the mechanical causes — tube in a bronchus, tube against the carina, inadequate depth of anaesthesia. It is an anaesthetic emergency and the differential is worth knowing because it appears in exams and in theatre.

> [!warning] Beta-blockers
> Non-selective beta-blockers can precipitate severe bronchospasm in asthma. **Ophthalmic beta-blocker drops for glaucoma are systemically absorbed** and are a classic and frequently overlooked cause — check the eye drops, not just the tablets.

**S/Smx:** Widespread polyphonic expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiration, dyspnoea, chest tightness, cough, accessory muscle use. A silent chest indicates severe obstruction.

**Ix:** Clinical assessment of severity (*why:* determines treatment intensity and disposition faster than any test; *what:* speech, work of breathing, saturation, conscious state). Oxygen saturation (*why:* rapid severity marker, preserved until late; *what:* hypoxia). ABG in severe or deteriorating cases (*why:* identifies the rising CO₂ that signals exhaustion; *what:* normalising or raised pCO₂). CXR where the cause is uncertain or complications suspected (*why:* distinguishes pulmonary oedema, pneumothorax, consolidation and foreign body from primary bronchospasm; *what:* findings). ECG and BNP where cardiac cause is plausible (*why:* separates cardiac asthma from true bronchospasm; *what:* ischaemia, elevated BNP). Serum tryptase where anaphylaxis is possible (*why:* retrospective confirmation of a mast cell event; *what:* acute rise). Medication and eye drop review (*why:* identifies the beta-blocker or NSAID trigger that will otherwise recur; *what:* offending agent).

### 0.5.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen. Inhaled short-acting beta-agonist, spacer or nebuliser. Ipratropium in moderate to severe. Systemic corticosteroid. **Adrenaline if anaphylaxis is possible** — cross-refer [[F0.1]] 0.9. Escalate to magnesium in severe cases. `UNVERIFIED — all doses from eTG and the Australian Asthma Handbook.`

### 0.5.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the underlying cause and remove the trigger. Cross-refer [[F0.5]] 0.1–0.2.

### 0.5.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Document the trigger as an allergy or contraindication where drug-induced. Preventer optimisation and action plan.

## 0.6 Hypoxia

**D:** Inadequate oxygen delivery to tissues. Distinct from **hypoxaemia**, which is low oxygen in arterial blood — a patient can be hypoxic with a normal PaO₂, and this distinction is where the diagnostic value lies.

> [!tip] Four categories of tissue hypoxia
> **Hypoxaemic** — low arterial oxygen. The familiar one.
> **Anaemic** — normal PaO₂ and normal saturation, but insufficient haemoglobin to carry oxygen. A severely anaemic patient can have a saturation of 100% and be profoundly tissue-hypoxic. **Carbon monoxide poisoning belongs here** — the haemoglobin is present but occupied.
> **Stagnant or circulatory** — adequate oxygen content but inadequate flow. Shock, cardiac failure, regional ischaemia.
> **Histotoxic** — oxygen delivered but the cell cannot use it. **Cyanide poisoning**, which classically presents with a high lactate, a high mixed venous oxygen saturation and a normal PaO₂.

> [!danger] Pulse oximetry lies in specific and important situations
> **Carbon monoxide poisoning** — carboxyhaemoglobin absorbs similarly to oxyhaemoglobin, so the oximeter reads **normal or high** while the patient is severely hypoxic. Requires a co-oximeter or specific carboxyhaemoglobin measurement.
> **Methaemoglobinaemia** — the reading tends toward a fixed value in the mid-eighties regardless of actual oxygenation, and the blood is characteristically chocolate-brown and does not turn red on exposure to air.
> **Poor peripheral perfusion, shock, hypothermia, vasopressors** — unreliable or absent trace.
> **Nail polish, artificial nails, dyes** — false readings.
> **Skin pigmentation** — pulse oximeters have been shown to overestimate oxygen saturation in patients with darker skin, meaning occult hypoxaemia can be missed. `UNVERIFIED — confirm the current evidence and any device or practice guidance on this, as it is an active area and the magnitude of the effect varies between devices.`

**A/P:** Oxygen delivery is the product of cardiac output and arterial oxygen content, and arterial oxygen content is dominated by **haemoglobin concentration and its saturation**, with dissolved oxygen contributing very little. This is why transfusing an anaemic patient can improve oxygen delivery far more than increasing FiO₂, and why a saturation number alone is an incomplete picture.

**S/Smx:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, tachycardia, restlessness and confusion — **agitation is an early sign of hypoxia and is frequently mistaken for anxiety or non-compliance.** Cyanosis is late, requires a certain absolute quantity of deoxygenated haemoglobin, and therefore **may never appear in a severely anaemic patient** while appearing readily in a polycythaemic one.

**Ix:** ABG with co-oximetry (*why:* measures true oxygen content, carboxyhaemoglobin and methaemoglobin, all of which standard oximetry misses; *what:* PaO₂, COHb, MetHb). FBC (*why:* anaemic hypoxia is invisible on a saturation reading; *what:* haemoglobin). Lactate (*why:* the marker of tissue-level oxygen failure regardless of which category caused it; *what:* elevation, clearance). Bedside echocardiography (*why:* identifies the stagnant category — inadequate flow with adequate content; *what:* contractility, filling). CXR (*why:* parenchymal causes; *what:* consolidation, oedema). ECG (*why:* ischaemia both causes and results from hypoxia; *what:* ischaemic change). Carboxyhaemoglobin specifically where there is any suggestion of smoke or fume exposure, or multiple household members with headache (*why:* it is undetectable on standard oximetry and easily missed; *what:* elevated level).

### 0.6.1 Mx – Immediate
Oxygen, titrated to a target — recognising that supplemental oxygen does not correct anaemic, stagnant or histotoxic hypoxia, and that **failure to respond to oxygen should redirect the diagnosis rather than prompt more oxygen.** High-flow oxygen is nonetheless correct in carbon monoxide poisoning, where it accelerates carboxyhaemoglobin clearance.

### 0.6.2 Mx – Definitive
Category-specific: treat the lung, transfuse, restore circulation, or give the specific antidote.

### 0.6.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Address the underlying cause and reassess oxygen requirement once stable.

## 0.7 Tachypnoea

**D:** Respiratory rate above the normal range for age.

> [!danger] The most predictive and least reliably recorded observation
> Respiratory rate rises earlier than heart rate, blood pressure or saturation in almost every form of deterioration, and it is the observation most often estimated rather than counted. **Count it for a full minute**, ideally while appearing to take the pulse, since patients who know they are being watched breathe differently. An accurately recorded respiratory rate is one of the most useful things an intern contributes.

**A/P:** Chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors drive respiratory rate in response to hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, and afferent input from lung stretch and irritant receptors. **A raised respiratory rate is therefore not a respiratory sign — it is a systemic one**, and the commonest non-respiratory causes are metabolic acidosis and sepsis.

> [!tip] Differential — think beyond the lungs
> **Respiratory:** pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, asthma, COPD, pneumothorax, effusion.
> **Metabolic:** any metabolic acidosis — DKA, lactic acidosis, renal failure, salicylate. Deep sighing **Kussmaul** breathing is characteristic. Cross-refer [[F0.2]] 0.1.
> **Circulatory:** sepsis, shock, anaemia, heart failure.
> **Neurological:** pain, anxiety, brainstem lesion, raised intracranial pressure.
> **Other:** fever, pregnancy, thyrotoxicosis, drugs including salicylate and stimulants.

> [!warning] Do not diagnose anxiety-related hyperventilation as a first move
> It is a diagnosis of exclusion. PE, DKA, sepsis and salicylate toxicity all present with tachypnoea in a distressed patient, and all have been missed by attributing the breathing to panic.

**S/Smx:** Increased rate, with the pattern often informative — deep and sighing in metabolic acidosis, shallow and rapid in restrictive or painful conditions, laboured with accessory muscle use in obstruction.

**Ix:** Counted respiratory rate over a full minute (*why:* the estimate is unreliable and the trend across the observation chart is the most predictive information available; *what:* rate and trend). VBG or ABG with lactate (*why:* distinguishes respiratory from metabolic drive, which is the first branch in the differential; *what:* acidosis with a low CO₂ indicates compensation for a metabolic problem). Bedside glucose and ketones (*why:* DKA presents as tachypnoea, sometimes without any respiratory symptoms at all; *what:* hyperglycaemia with ketosis). Oxygen saturation on documented FiO₂ (*why:* separates hypoxaemic from non-hypoxaemic causes; *what:* saturation with inspired oxygen). CXR (*why:* parenchymal and pleural causes; *what:* findings). FBC, UEC, CRP (*why:* anaemia, renal failure, infection; *what:* haemoglobin, creatinine, inflammatory markers). D-dimer or CTPA where PE is plausible (*why:* PE frequently presents with isolated tachypnoea and a clear chest radiograph; *what:* filling defect). Salicylate level where the picture is a mixed acid-base disorder (*why:* easily missed; *what:* level — cross-refer [[F0.2]] 0.8).

### 0.7.1 Mx – Immediate
Assess for the causes above rather than treating the number. Oxygen if hypoxaemic. Escalate if the rate is rising.

### 0.7.2 Mx – Definitive
Cause-specific.

### 0.7.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Where the cause is genuinely anxiety or dysfunctional breathing, breathing retraining and psychological therapy — after organic causes have been excluded.

## 0.8 Bradypnoea

**D:** Respiratory rate below the normal range for age.

**R/Causes:** **Opioids and sedatives** including benzodiazepines and gabapentinoids · raised intracranial pressure · brainstem lesion or stroke · hypothyroidism and myxoedema · hypothermia · severe hypercapnia with CO₂ narcosis · high cervical cord injury · neuromuscular disease · post-ictal state · **exhaustion in a previously tachypnoeic patient.**

> [!danger] A falling respiratory rate in a distressed patient is exhaustion, not improvement
> The asthmatic whose rate falls from 40 to 20 while remaining distressed and drowsy is not getting better. **This is the single most dangerous misreading of an observation chart**, because the number moves toward normal while the patient moves toward arrest. Interpret respiratory rate alongside work of breathing, conscious state and CO₂ — never in isolation.

> [!warning] Bradypnoea on a ward is opioid toxicity until proven otherwise
> Check the medication chart, look at the pupils, and remember that **saturation on supplemental oxygen can remain normal in a patient who is dangerously hypercapnic.** Sedation score and respiratory rate are the monitoring parameters. Cross-refer [[F0.1]] 0.5.

**A/P:** Reduced respiratory drive or an inability to execute it → falling minute ventilation → hypercapnia → CO₂ narcosis further reduces drive → a self-reinforcing loop terminating in apnoea.

**S/Smx:** Slow shallow breathing, drowsiness, small pupils in opioid toxicity, warm flushed peripheries and bounding pulse in hypercapnia, asterixis, and eventually apnoea.

**Ix:** ABG or VBG (*why:* the definitive test — quantifies hypercapnia, which oximetry cannot detect and which supplemental oxygen conceals; *what:* raised pCO₂ with respiratory acidosis). Bedside glucose (*why:* reversible mimic; *what:* hypoglycaemia). Pupil examination and medication chart review (*why:* opioid toxicity is the commonest ward cause and the answer is usually written on the chart; *what:* pinpoint pupils, cumulative opioid and sedative dose). Non-contrast CT brain where there is any focal sign or unexplained persistence (*why:* raised intracranial pressure and brainstem lesions cause bradypnoea and are not survivable to miss; *what:* haemorrhage, mass, hydrocephalus). TSH and core temperature (*why:* myxoedema coma and hypothermia both cause bradypnoea and both are reversible; *what:* raised TSH, low temperature). UEC and calcium (*why:* metabolic causes of reduced conscious state; *what:* derangement).

### 0.8.1 Mx – Immediate
Airway positioning, oxygen, and **bag-mask ventilation if ventilation is inadequate — ventilate before reaching for a reversal agent.** Bedside glucose. Naloxone titrated to adequate respiration where opioid toxicity is likely, with the caveats in [[F0.1]] 0.5. Escalate.

### 0.8.2 Mx – Definitive
Treat the cause. Where hypercapnia is the driver, ventilatory support rather than reversal.

### 0.8.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
Review and adjust sedative and opioid prescribing. Document the event. Cross-refer [[F0.4]] 0.9 for opioid stewardship.
