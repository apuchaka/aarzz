## 0.1 Lachman Test

**D:** With the patient supine and the knee flexed to **20–30°**, stabilise the femur with one hand and pull the tibia **anteriorly** with the other, feeling for excessive anterior translation and, critically, for the quality of the **end point**.

**Tests for:** **Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture.**

**Positive:** Increased anterior translation compared with the other side, with a **soft or absent end point**. The end point is the more reliable finding — translation alone is subjective.

> [!info] **Performance — and this is the test to learn.** Sources place the Lachman as **the most sensitive** of the ACL tests: pooled sensitivity around **85%** with specificity around **94%**, though a more recent meta-analysis argues its sensitivity has been **historically overestimated** and is closer to that of the other tests. In acute injury, pooled sensitivity falls to roughly **0.79**. **The consistent recommendation across sources is: if you perform one test for a suspected ACL rupture, perform the Lachman.**

> [!warning] **Why it beats the anterior drawer.** At 20–30° of flexion the **hamstrings are relaxed** and the **menisci do not block** anterior translation. At 90° (the anterior drawer position), hamstring spasm in an acutely painful knee can hold the tibia and mask a complete rupture.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A large acute haemarthrosis after a twisting injury with a "pop" is an ACL rupture until proven otherwise** — the differential also includes patellar dislocation, osteochondral fracture and peripheral meniscal tear.
> - **Always examine the other knee first** to establish that patient's normal, and to gain their confidence.
> - **Pain and guarding make the acute examination unreliable in both directions.** Re-examine after analgesia, or a few days later once the effusion and spasm settle.
> - **Exclude a fracture before stress-testing ligaments** — apply the Ottawa knee rules and image if indicated.
> - See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

**Alt:** Anterior drawer (0.2), pivot shift (0.3), lever sign; **MRI** — the imaging standard for cruciate and meniscal injury; instrumented laxity measurement; examination under anaesthesia; arthroscopy.

## 0.2 Anterior Drawer Test

**D:** **Two distinct tests share this name, and they must not be confused.**
- **Knee:** patient supine, hip flexed 45°, **knee flexed to 90°**, foot stabilised (often by the examiner sitting on it); grasp the proximal tibia and pull **anteriorly**.
- **Ankle:** patient seated with the ankle in slight plantarflexion; stabilise the distal tibia with one hand and pull the **heel forward** with the other.

**Tests for:** **Knee** — ACL rupture. **Ankle** — **anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL)** injury in a lateral ankle sprain.

**Positive:** Excessive anterior translation with a soft end point (knee); anterior translation of the talus, sometimes with a visible dimple or "suction sign" over the anterolateral ankle (ankle).

> [!info] **Performance at the knee — and the acute/chronic split is the whole story.** Sources report the anterior drawer performs **well in chronic ACL deficiency** (sensitivity ~92%, specificity ~91%) **but not in acute injury**, where hamstring spasm and effusion defeat it — one meta-analysis puts acute sensitivity at about **0.78** with specificity **0.91**. **In an acute knee, use the Lachman.**

> [!warning] **At the ankle, timing matters.** The test is unreliable immediately after injury because of pain and swelling; **re-examination at about 4–5 days**, once swelling settles, is far more informative, and this delayed reassessment is standard practice for grading lateral ligament injury.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A false-negative anterior drawer at the knee occurs with a "locked" bucket-handle meniscal tear**, where the displaced meniscus physically blocks anterior translation — so a normal test in a knee that will not fully extend is not reassuring.
> - **A posterior cruciate rupture causes a false-positive anterior drawer**: the tibia starts sagged posteriorly, so pulling it forward feels like excess translation. **Always look for posterior sag first** (0.4) — this is the classic trap, and it makes a PCL injury look like an ACL injury.
> - **Apply the Ottawa ankle rules** before stress-testing an injured ankle — exclude fracture, and specifically palpate the **proximal fibula** (Maisonneuve) and the **base of the fifth metatarsal**.
> - See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

**Alt:** Lachman (0.1) and pivot shift (0.3) at the knee; talar tilt and the **squeeze test** for syndesmotic injury at the ankle; **MRI**; ultrasound for ligament assessment; stress radiographs.

## 0.3 Pivot Shift Test

**D:** With the knee in extension, apply a **valgus force and internal rotation** to the tibia while slowly **flexing** the knee. In an ACL-deficient knee the anteriorly subluxed lateral tibial plateau **reduces with a palpable clunk at around 20–40° of flexion** as the iliotibial band changes its line of pull.

**Tests for:** ACL rupture, demonstrating the **rotational instability** that causes the patient's symptom of giving way.

**Positive:** A palpable or visible **clunk or jerk** as the tibia reduces.

> [!info] **Performance — the most specific ACL test, and the least sensitive.** Sources agree: specificity around **98%**, sensitivity only around **24%** overall (a meta-analysis of acute injuries gives sensitivity ~0.55, specificity ~0.96). **A positive pivot shift essentially confirms an ACL rupture; a negative one excludes nothing.**

> [!danger] **It requires a relaxed patient, which means it is close to useless in an acute painful knee** — guarding abolishes the shift. It is most reliably elicited **under anaesthesia**, which is where it is often formally documented. Do not conclude the ACL is intact because you could not produce a pivot shift in the emergency department.

> [!warning] **It reproduces the patient's instability, which is unpleasant.** Warn them, examine the uninjured side first, and stop if they cannot relax — repeated failed attempts guarantee guarding and make every subsequent test uninterpretable.

> [!info] **Why it matters clinically:** the pivot shift correlates with **functional instability and with the risk of subsequent meniscal and chondral damage**, which is part of the argument for reconstruction in an active patient. A high-grade pivot shift is not merely a diagnostic finding.

**Alt:** Lachman (0.1) — the screening test; anterior drawer (0.2); the Jerk test and other pivot-shift variants; **MRI**; examination under anaesthesia; arthroscopy. See [[11_05_Ortho_-_Knee_and_Ankle]].

## 0.4 Posterior Drawer Test

**D:** Patient supine, hip flexed 45°, **knee flexed to 90°**, foot stabilised; push the proximal tibia **posteriorly**. **Before doing anything else, look at the knee from the side** with both knees flexed to 90° and compare the position of the tibial tuberosities.

**Tests for:** **Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)** injury.

**Positive:** Excessive posterior translation with a soft end point, and — the more important sign — a visible **posterior sag** of the tibia at rest.

> [!danger] **The posterior sag sign is the finding that prevents the classic misdiagnosis.**
> A tibia that starts sagged posteriorly gives a **falsely positive anterior drawer**, because the first part of the anterior pull is simply reducing the tibia from its sagged position. **A PCL injury misread as an ACL injury is a recognised and consequential error.** Always inspect for sag before testing anteriorly.

> [!info] **Mechanism directs the examination.** PCL injuries follow a **direct blow to the front of the flexed tibia** — the **dashboard injury** in a motor vehicle crash, or a fall onto a flexed knee with the foot plantarflexed. That history should prompt a deliberate PCL examination, because it is otherwise easily missed and PCL injuries are often less dramatic acutely than ACL ruptures.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A knee dislocation is a limb-threatening emergency.** Multiligament injury — and particularly a knee that has clearly dislocated and spontaneously reduced — carries a high risk of **popliteal artery injury and common peroneal nerve palsy**. **Document distal pulses and neurology, and involve senior orthopaedic and vascular teams urgently**; a normal pulse does not exclude an intimal injury, and vascular imaging is usually required. See [[11_01_Ortho_-_Orthopaedic_Emergencies]].
> - Assess for associated **posterolateral corner** injury (dial test, external rotation asymmetry) — isolated PCL injury is much less common than combined injury, and missing the corner leads to failed reconstruction.
> - **MRI** is required for a suspected multiligament injury.

**Alt:** Posterior sag / godfrey test; quadriceps active test; dial test for posterolateral corner; **MRI**; stress radiographs; vascular assessment with ankle-brachial index and CT angiography where dislocation is suspected.

## 0.5 Thompson Test (Simmonds-Thompson, Calf Squeeze)

**D:** With the patient **prone and the feet hanging over the end of the bed**, squeeze the calf (the gastrocnemius-soleus mass) and observe the foot. Compare with the other side.

**Tests for:** **Complete Achilles tendon rupture.**

**Positive:** **Absent plantarflexion** of the foot on squeezing — the tendon is not transmitting the contraction.

> [!info] **Performance.** Sources describe it as the standard initial test for suspected Achilles rupture, quoting sensitivity around **96%** and specificity around **93%**. It was described by **Simmonds in 1957**, which is why the eponym is sometimes doubled.

> [!danger] **A patient with a completely ruptured Achilles tendon CAN STILL PLANTARFLEX and CAN OFTEN STILL WALK.**
> The long flexors and tibialis posterior plantarflex the foot, so asking the patient to "point your toes" or watching them walk **does not exclude the diagnosis**. This is why the Thompson test exists and why **Achilles rupture is one of the most commonly missed injuries in emergency departments**. Sources also note the test cannot distinguish a partial from a complete tear.

> [!warning] **The examination that finds it**
> - **A palpable gap** in the tendon, usually 2–6 cm above the insertion, though it fills with haematoma within hours and becomes harder to feel.
> - **Loss of the normal resting tension** — with the patient prone, the affected foot hangs in more dorsiflexion than the other.
> - **The history is characteristic**: sudden pain "like being kicked or shot in the back of the heel", often in a middle-aged intermittent athlete, often with an audible snap.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroids** (systemic or local injection) are recognised risk factors for tendon rupture — ask.
> - **Missed rupture leads to chronic weakness, a poorer functional result and a harder reconstruction.** If in doubt, **immobilise in equinus and refer** — that costs little and protects the tendon.
> - **Ultrasound is a quick, accessible confirmatory test** and can assess gap size in plantarflexion, which influences management; MRI is reserved for complex or chronic cases.
> - See [[11_03_Ortho_-_Hand_and_Foot]].

**Alt:** Palpation for a gap; Matles test (prone knee flexion, observing resting foot position); **ultrasound**; MRI; the O'Brien needle test (rarely used).

## 0.6 Finkelstein's Test

**D:** The **true Finkelstein test**: the examiner grasps the patient's **thumb** and applies longitudinal traction and **ulnar deviation**, reproducing pain over the **first dorsal compartment** at the radial styloid. Distinguished from **Eichhoff's manoeuvre** — the patient tucks the thumb into the palm, closes the fingers over it, and the examiner ulnar-deviates the wrist.

**Tests for:** **De Quervain tenosynovitis** — stenosing tenosynovitis of **abductor pollicis longus** and **extensor pollicis brevis**.

**Positive:** Sharp pain over the radial styloid and first dorsal compartment, reproducing the patient's symptoms.

> [!danger] **The manoeuvre everyone is taught as "Finkelstein's" is usually Eichhoff's, and it produces false positives.**
> Sources are explicit: **Eichhoff's test is commonly but incorrectly taught as Finkelstein's**, is very provocative, and generates false-positive pain by stressing adjacent radial wrist structures — the scaphoid, the first CMC joint, the radiocarpal joint. **A properly performed Finkelstein test is much less likely to be falsely positive.** Knowing the distinction is the point of this entry: a "positive Finkelstein" in a patient whose pain is actually from thumb-base arthritis leads to the wrong treatment.

> [!warning] **The differential at the radial side of the wrist is the reason for the false positives**
> - **Thumb CMC (basal joint) osteoarthritis** — pain more distal, at the thumb base; test with the grind test (0.7). **The two conditions coexist commonly in the same age group.**
> - **Scaphoid fracture** — anatomical snuffbox tenderness after a fall on the outstretched hand; a completely different and time-critical problem.
> - **Intersection syndrome** — pain and crepitus more proximally, about 4–6 cm proximal to the wrist.
> - **Wartenberg syndrome** — superficial radial nerve entrapment, causing numbness rather than pain on resisted movement.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **De Quervain tenosynovitis is strongly associated with the postpartum period and with infant care** ("mother's thumb") — the repetitive lifting of a baby with an abducted thumb. Ask, because it changes the counselling and the prognosis.
> - **Do not miss a scaphoid fracture.** Snuffbox tenderness after a fall means scaphoid views and, if radiographs are normal but suspicion persists, immobilisation and re-imaging or MRI — not "wrist sprain".
> - Management is splinting, activity modification, NSAIDs and **corticosteroid injection**, which is effective; surgical release for refractory cases. **Repeated injections risk tendon rupture and subcutaneous fat atrophy and depigmentation**, which is disfiguring and permanent — warn the patient.
> - See [[11_03_Ortho_-_Hand_and_Foot]].

**Alt:** Direct palpation of the first dorsal compartment; the **grind test** (0.7) to identify coexisting CMC arthritis; ultrasound (which can also show a septated compartment, a cause of injection failure); radiographs to exclude arthritis and fracture.

## 0.7 Grind Test (Thumb Carpometacarpal)

**D:** Grasp the patient's **thumb metacarpal**, apply **axial compression** into the trapezium and simultaneously **rotate**, grinding the joint surfaces against each other.

**Tests for:** **Osteoarthritis of the first carpometacarpal (trapeziometacarpal, "basal") joint.**

**Positive:** Pain, and often **palpable crepitus**, at the base of the thumb.

> [!info] **Performance — specific rather than sensitive.** Sources report sensitivity of **42–53%** depending on examiner, with specificity of **80–93%**. Later work found a **pressure-shear test** substantially more sensitive (reported at 99–100%) than the grind test. **A positive grind test supports the diagnosis; a negative one does not exclude it**, and inter-examiner variation is significant.

> [!warning] **Thumb-base osteoarthritis is common, disabling and under-recognised** — particularly in **postmenopausal women**. It causes pain on pinch and grip (keys, jars, taps, pegs), and later an **adduction deformity of the metacarpal with compensatory MCP hyperextension** (the "zigzag" or shoulder sign). It is frequently dismissed as "arthritis in the hands" without a specific diagnosis, which forgoes effective treatment.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **It coexists with de Quervain tenosynovitis and with carpal tunnel syndrome** in the same patients; one diagnosis does not exclude the others, and treating only the one you tested for leaves the patient no better.
> - **Radiographic severity correlates poorly with symptoms** — treat the patient, not the film.
> - **Non-operative treatment is genuinely effective**: a thumb spica or CMC splint, hand therapy, joint protection education and adaptive aids, NSAIDs, and intra-articular injection. **Refer to hand therapy before considering surgery.**
> - **Rapidly progressive, hot, or inflammatory-sounding joint pain is not osteoarthritis** — consider inflammatory arthritis, gout and infection.

**Alt:** Pressure-shear test (reported as more sensitive); crank/traction-shift tests; palpation and pinch strength measurement; **radiographs** (thumb views, including a stress view) for staging; ultrasound; diagnostic injection.

## 0.8 FABER Test (Patrick's Test)

**D:** **F**lexion, **AB**duction, **E**xternal **R**otation. Patient supine; place the ankle of the tested leg on the opposite knee (figure-4), then stabilise the opposite anterior superior iliac spine and press the tested knee **downwards**.

**Tests for:** Hip joint pathology and **sacroiliac joint** pathology — and, importantly, **it helps distinguish between them by where the pain is felt**.

**Positive:** Pain, and restricted downward movement of the knee compared with the other side.

> [!info] **Location of the pain is the discriminator, and this is what makes the test useful**
> - **Pain in the GROIN** → **hip joint** pathology (osteoarthritis, femoroacetabular impingement, labral tear, avascular necrosis).
> - **Pain in the BUTTOCK over the sacroiliac joint** → **sacroiliac** pathology.
> - Anterior thigh or knee pain may reflect referred hip pain — remember hip pathology commonly presents as knee pain, especially in children.

> [!warning] **Neither sensitive nor specific on its own.** FABER is one of a **cluster** of sacroiliac provocation tests (with distraction, compression, thigh thrust and Gaenslen), and the accepted approach is that **multiple positive tests in a cluster** carry weight while any single test does not. Used alone it will mislead.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Inflammatory back pain** — pain and stiffness worse in the morning and with rest, better with exercise, waking the patient in the second half of the night, in a person under 45 — should prompt assessment for **axial spondyloarthritis**, including the modified Schober test (0.13), HLA-B27, inflammatory markers and sacroiliac imaging. Diagnostic delay in ankylosing spondylitis is measured in years. See [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]].
> - **A child or adolescent with hip pain, a limp, or referred knee pain** needs a specific differential — septic arthritis, transient synovitis, Perthes, SUFE — and septic arthritis must be excluded urgently. See [[11_10_Ortho_-_Paediatric_Orthopaedics]].
> - **Do not force the manoeuvre** in a patient with a suspected fracture, a hip prosthesis (risk of dislocation), or severe pain.
> - See [[11_04_Ortho_-_Hip]].

**Alt:** Full hip range of motion with **log roll** (the most specific test for intra-articular hip pathology); FADIR/impingement test; Thomas test for fixed flexion; the sacroiliac provocation cluster; Trendelenburg test (0.9); radiographs and MRI.

## 0.9 Trendelenburg Test
`CF-PAIR §1.26` **Part 1 §1.26 Trendelenburg's Sign and Gait covers the same test. Both kept in full, NOT reconciled.**

**D:** Ask the patient to **stand on one leg** while you observe the pelvis from behind, ideally with your hands resting on both iliac crests. Repeat on the other side.

**Tests for:** Function of the **hip abductors — gluteus medius and minimus** — and of the superior gluteal nerve that supplies them.

**Positive:** **The pelvis drops on the side of the LIFTED leg** — which means the weakness is in the **STANDING** (weight-bearing) leg. Sources state this explicitly, and getting the side right is the entire content of the test.

> [!danger] **The side is the exam question and the clinical point: the weak side is the side the patient is STANDING on.**
> The abductors of the stance leg must contract to hold the unsupported hemipelvis level. If they cannot, that side of the pelvis fails and the **opposite** side sags. Reporting the wrong side sends the investigation to the wrong hip.

> [!info] **What causes it**
> - **Pain** — the commonest reason in practice: any painful hip (osteoarthritis, fracture, infection) inhibits abduction. This is an **antalgic** rather than a truly weak Trendelenburg.
> - **Muscle weakness** — gluteal tendinopathy or tear (common and under-diagnosed in older women), disuse, myopathy.
> - **Superior gluteal nerve injury** — a recognised complication of hip surgery and of badly sited intramuscular gluteal injections.
> - **Abnormal joint mechanics** — developmental dysplasia, a shortened femoral neck after fracture, failed arthroplasty.
> - **A compensated gait** produces the **Trendelenburg lurch**: the patient leans their trunk **over** the affected stance leg to move the centre of gravity and reduce the abductor demand — a bilateral version gives a waddling gait.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Bilateral positive tests** suggest a **proximal myopathy** or bilateral hip disease — check for other proximal weakness, ask about steroids and statins, and consider CK, TFTs and inflammatory myopathy.
> - **In a child, a positive Trendelenburg with a limp** raises developmental dysplasia of the hip, Perthes disease and SUFE.
> - **Balance, not just strength, limits the test** — a patient may fail because they cannot balance. Allow light fingertip support and interpret accordingly.
> - **Lateral hip pain with a positive Trendelenburg in an older woman is usually gluteal tendinopathy**, not "trochanteric bursitis", and it responds to load-based physiotherapy rather than repeated injection.

**Alt:** Formal abductor power testing; observed gait (looking for the lurch); single-leg stance timing; hip radiographs and **MRI or ultrasound** for gluteal tendon pathology; EMG/NCS where nerve injury is suspected.

## 0.10 Adam's Test (Forward Bend Test)
`CF-PAIR §1.25` **Part 1 §1.25 Scoliosis Screening (Adam's Forward Bend Test) covers the same test. Both kept in full, NOT reconciled.**

**D:** The patient stands and **bends forward from the waist with knees straight and arms hanging**, feet together, while the examiner views the back **tangentially from behind** looking for asymmetry — a **rib hump** or paravertebral prominence. Quantified with a **scoliometer**, which measures the **angle of trunk rotation (ATR)**.

**Tests for:** **Scoliosis** — specifically, the **rotational deformity** that distinguishes a structural curve from a postural one.

**Positive:** A visible or measurable asymmetry of the trunk on forward flexion.

> [!info] **Structural versus postural — what the test actually determines**
> A **structural** scoliosis has a **rotational component**, so the deformity **persists or becomes more obvious** on forward bending. A **postural** curve — from leg length discrepancy, muscle spasm or posture — **disappears** on forward flexion. That distinction, not the presence of a curve, is what the test is for.

> [!info] **Referral thresholds.** Sources describe **5° and 7° of angle of trunk rotation** as the criteria proposed for referral in school screening settings. They also report a meaningful discrepancy: in one screening study of 954 children, **123 children at >5° and 13 at >7° were abnormal on the scoliometer but appeared normal on the Adams test alone** — the visual test alone under-detects.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **A painful scoliosis, a rapidly progressive curve, a LEFT thoracic curve, or any curve with neurological signs is not adolescent idiopathic scoliosis** until proven otherwise. Those features suggest an underlying cause — syrinx, tethered cord, tumour, osteoid osteoma, neuromuscular disease — and warrant **MRI** and specialist referral. Idiopathic scoliosis is typically painless and right-thoracic.
> - **Curves progress fastest during the adolescent growth spurt**, so **skeletal maturity determines urgency**. A moderate curve in a premenarchal girl with open growth plates is far more concerning than the same curve in a skeletally mature patient. Assess growth status.
> - **The Cobb angle on a standing full-spine radiograph is what defines and grades scoliosis** — the forward bend test screens, radiography diagnoses.
> - **Early referral preserves options.** Bracing works in the growing child with a moderate curve; once the curve is severe or growth has finished, surgery is the only option left.
> - See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]].

**Normal/abnormal:** Angle of trunk rotation in degrees; referral at the threshold used by your local service (5° or 7° per the sources).

**Alt:** **Scoliometer** (should accompany the visual test); standing full-spine **radiograph with Cobb angle**; assessment of skeletal maturity (Risser sign, menarchal status); **MRI** for atypical features; leg length measurement to identify a postural cause.

## 0.11 Straight Leg Raise (Lasègue's Sign)

**D:** With the patient **supine**, raise the straight leg passively with the knee extended. Note the angle at which the patient's **radicular** pain is reproduced. **Sciatic nerve tension increases from about 30° to 70°.** The **crossed (contralateral) SLR** raises the *unaffected* leg and is positive if it reproduces pain **in the affected leg**.

**Tests for:** **Lumbosacral nerve root irritation** — most often an **L5 or S1** radiculopathy from disc herniation.

**Positive:** Reproduction of **radicular pain radiating below the knee** at 30–70° of elevation. **Pain confined to the back or hamstring is not a positive test.**

> [!info] **Performance — the ipsilateral and crossed tests are mirror images of each other**
> - **Ipsilateral SLR: sensitive, not specific** — sources give sensitivity **72–97%** and specificity **11–66%**. Useful for **ruling out**.
> - **Crossed SLR: specific, not sensitive** — sensitivity **23–42%**, specificity **85–100%**. **A positive crossed SLR is strong evidence of significant nerve root compression**, often from a large central or paracentral disc, and it is the finding that should raise your concern most.
> - **Sensitivity falls markedly with age** — one source reports sensitivity of only **33%** in patients over 60, with a two-thirds false-negative rate. **A negative SLR in an older patient means very little**, and in that age group the differential (spinal stenosis, malignancy, fracture, vascular claudication) is different anyway.

> [!danger] **CAUDA EQUINA SYNDROME must be actively excluded in every patient with severe or bilateral sciatica.**
> Ask about and document: **urinary retention or incontinence, faecal incontinence, saddle (perineal) anaesthesia, bilateral leg symptoms, and progressive or profound motor weakness.** **Examine perianal sensation and anal tone if there is any suspicion.** This is an **emergency requiring urgent MRI and same-day neurosurgical or spinal referral** — delay causes permanent incontinence and sexual dysfunction, and "arrange an outpatient scan" is not an acceptable plan. See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]].

> [!warning] **False positives are common.** Hamstring tightness, hip pathology, sacroiliac pain and simple back pain all limit the raise. **Sensitising manoeuvres** — **ankle dorsiflexion** or **neck flexion** at the point of pain — increase neural tension without changing muscle length, and worsening pain on those manoeuvres supports a genuinely neural origin. Reproducing back pain alone is not a positive test.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore the other red flags for back pain**: age over 50 with new pain, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, fever or intravenous drug use, night pain, trauma, prolonged corticosteroids, or any progressive neurological deficit. These change the pathway from conservative management to urgent imaging. See also `NEW_Investigations_Orthopaedics_Neurology_and_Other.md` 0.8.

**Alt:** **Slump test** (reported as more sensitive, less specific); crossed SLR; **femoral stretch test** (0.12) for upper lumbar roots; neurological examination by myotome, dermatome and reflex (see [[11_07a_Ortho_-_Dermatomes_and_Myotomes_Reference]]); **MRI lumbar spine** — urgently where red flags or cauda equina features are present.

## 0.12 Femoral Stretch Test (Reverse Lasègue / Prone Knee Bend)

**D:** With the patient **prone** (or lying on the unaffected side), **flex the knee** and then **extend the hip**, stretching the femoral nerve and the upper lumbar roots.

**Tests for:** Irritation of the **upper lumbar nerve roots — L2, L3 and L4** — and of the femoral nerve. **It is the upper-lumbar counterpart of the straight leg raise**, which tests L5 and S1.

**Positive:** Reproduction of pain in the **anterior thigh** (and sometimes the medial lower leg via the saphenous branch), not merely tightness in the quadriceps.

> [!info] **Why you need both tests.** Most lumbar disc herniations are at L4/5 and L5/S1, which is why the SLR is the test everyone knows. **Upper lumbar herniations are much less common and are correspondingly more often missed** — and they present differently: **anterior thigh pain, quadriceps weakness, a reduced or absent knee jerk**, rather than posterior leg pain and an absent ankle jerk. If the symptoms are anterior, the SLR is the wrong test.

> [!warning] **Published diagnostic accuracy for this test is sparse** — the sources retrieved on straight leg raising did not provide comparable sensitivity and specificity figures for the femoral stretch test, and **none are quoted here** rather than borrowing SLR figures that do not apply. Interpret it as a supportive sign within the neurological examination, not as a stand-alone test.

> [!danger] **Anterior thigh pain with quadriceps weakness has a differential beyond disc disease, and some of it is urgent**
> - **Diabetic amyotrophy (lumbosacral radiculoplexus neuropathy)** — severe anterior thigh pain with wasting and weakness, often with weight loss, in a person with diabetes.
> - **Retroperitoneal or psoas haematoma** compressing the femoral nerve — think of this in anyone **anticoagulated**, and it is a diagnosis that is missed until the deficit is fixed.
> - **Psoas abscess** — fever, back or flank pain, a flexed hip that resists extension.
> - **Malignancy or infection in the upper lumbar spine or retroperitoneum.**
> - **A femoral nerve palsy after hip surgery, prolonged lithotomy positioning, or a groin procedure.**

> [!danger] **Do not ignore** — as with the SLR, **exclude cauda equina syndrome**, and remember that **a positive stretch test localises the level but never establishes the cause**. Imaging and the rest of the neurological examination do that.

**Alt:** Straight leg raise (0.11) for the lower roots; knee jerk and quadriceps power; sensory testing over the anterior thigh and medial calf; **MRI lumbar spine**; CT abdomen/pelvis where a retroperitoneal cause is suspected; EMG/NCS.

## 0.13 Modified Schober Test

**D:** With the patient standing, mark the **lumbosacral junction** (at the level of the posterior superior iliac spines / the dimples of Venus), then mark **10 cm above** and **5 cm below** it. Ask the patient to **bend forward maximally** with the knees straight, and re-measure the distance between the outer marks.

**Tests for:** **Lumbar spine flexion** — as an objective measure of the spinal restriction of **axial spondyloarthritis / ankylosing spondylitis**, and to track it over time.

**Positive (abnormal):** An **increase of less than 5 cm** on the 15 cm baseline indicates reduced lumbar flexion; sources describe an increase of **more than 5 cm** as a healthy range of motion.

> [!warning] **Know which version you are performing, and record it.** The **original Schober** used 10 cm above the lumbosacral junction only; the **modified** version adds the 5 cm below (baseline 15 cm); the **modified-modified (Macrae-Wright)** version uses different landmarks again. **They give different numbers and are not interchangeable** — serial measurements must use the same method and, ideally, the same examiner.

> [!danger] **A single measurement is nearly useless; the trend is the point.** Its value is in **monitoring** an individual patient over years and in composite disease activity indices, not in diagnosing anyone. Sources include a study concluding that **the Schober test is not a valid assessment tool for lumbar mobility** when validated against radiographic measurement — so treat it as a crude, reproducible, cheap surrogate rather than a measurement of what it claims to measure.

> [!danger] **The diagnosis is not made by this test, and delay is the real problem.** Suspect **axial spondyloarthritis** from the history: **inflammatory back pain** — insidious onset **before age 45**, **worse with rest and better with exercise**, **morning stiffness over 30 minutes**, **night pain waking the patient in the second half of the night** — with peripheral and extra-articular features: **enthesitis** (Achilles, plantar fascia), **dactylitis**, **acute anterior uveitis**, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, and a family history. **Diagnostic delay in ankylosing spondylitis is typically measured in years**, largely because inflammatory back pain is treated as mechanical.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore**
> - **Acute anterior uveitis is an ophthalmic emergency** — a painful red eye with photophobia in a patient with back pain needs same-day ophthalmology, and it is often the presentation that reveals the underlying spondyloarthritis.
> - **The ankylosed spine fractures easily and unstably.** Minor trauma in a patient with advanced ankylosing spondylitis can cause a **three-column cervical fracture** with a high risk of cord injury; the threshold for CT is very low, and plain films are inadequate.
> - **Investigate with** inflammatory markers, HLA-B27 (supportive, not diagnostic — it is common in the general population), **sacroiliac joint imaging** (X-ray, and **MRI** which detects inflammation before radiographic change), and rheumatology referral.
> - **Exercise and physiotherapy are first-line treatment**, alongside NSAIDs and, where indicated, biologic therapy. Measure chest expansion and occiput-to-wall distance as well.
> - See [[12_02_Rheum_-_Ankylosing_Spondylitis__Gout__Pseudogout__Reactive_Arthritis__Fibromyalgia__PMR__CFS]].

**Alt:** Occiput-to-wall and tragus-to-wall distance; chest expansion; lateral lumbar flexion; cervical rotation; composite metrology and disease activity indices used in rheumatology; sacroiliac joint **MRI**.

## 0.14 Compression Test (Spurling's Test)

**D:** **Spurling's manoeuvre**: the patient's neck is **extended and laterally flexed (and often rotated) towards the symptomatic side**, and the examiner applies gentle **axial compression** to the top of the head, narrowing the neural foramen on that side.

**Tests for:** **Cervical radiculopathy** — nerve root compression, usually from a disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte.

**Positive:** Reproduction of the patient's **radicular** arm pain or paraesthesia in a dermatomal distribution. **Local neck pain alone is NOT a positive test.**

> [!info] **Performance — specific, not sensitive, so it confirms rather than excludes.** Sources report sensitivity around **30%** with specificity around **93%** in acute radiculopathy; a later study in patients with unilateral radiculopathy of at least 4 weeks reported much higher figures (sensitivity 95%, specificity 94%) — **the sources genuinely conflict**, and the honest summary is that Spurling's test shows **low-to-moderate sensitivity and high specificity**. **A positive test is clinically useful for confirming radiculopathy; a negative test does not rule it out.**

> [!danger] **Do not perform this test if there is any suspicion of cervical instability, fracture, or myelopathy.**
> Axial compression on a potentially unstable cervical spine is dangerous. **Contraindications include acute trauma, known instability (rheumatoid arthritis, Down syndrome), suspected fracture, and signs of cord compression.** Stop immediately if the test produces bilateral symptoms, lower limb symptoms or any suggestion of cord involvement.

> [!danger] **Distinguish radiculopathy from MYELOPATHY — this is the point that changes management.**
> **Cervical myelopathy** is cord compression, and it is progressive, often painless, and easily missed: **clumsy hands** (difficulty with buttons and coins), **deteriorating handwriting**, a **broad-based unsteady gait**, **bladder disturbance**, **upper motor neurone signs** (hyperreflexia below the level, Hoffmann sign, Babinski, clonus), and **Lhermitte phenomenon**. It requires **urgent MRI and spinal surgical referral** — decompression prevents further deterioration but does not reliably reverse established damage, so time matters.

> [!warning] **Do not ignore**
> - **Red flags in neck pain**: trauma, fever, malignancy history, weight loss, night pain, progressive neurological deficit, **Horner syndrome**, and vertebrobasilar symptoms (dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, drop attacks) — any of which redirects the work-up entirely.
> - **Not every arm pain is cervical**: consider brachial plexopathy, thoracic outlet syndrome, peripheral entrapment (carpal or cubital tunnel), shoulder pathology, and **Pancoast tumour** (apical lung cancer with C8/T1 symptoms and Horner syndrome — a classic miss in a smoker).
> - Most cervical radiculopathy improves with conservative management; surgery is for persistent or progressive deficit.
> - See [[11_06_Ortho_-_Spinal_Orthopaedics]] and [[04_Neurology]].

**Alt:** **Cervical distraction test** (0.15); upper limb neurodynamic (tension) tests; **shoulder abduction relief sign**; full neurological examination by dermatome, myotome and reflex; **MRI cervical spine**; EMG/NCS where the level or the peripheral/central distinction is unclear.

## 0.15 Distraction Test (Cervical Distraction)

**D:** With the patient supine, the examiner grasps the head under the **occiput and chin** and applies gentle **axial traction**, unloading the cervical spine and widening the neural foramina.

**Tests for:** **Cervical radiculopathy** — it is the **relief** counterpart to Spurling's compression test.

**Positive:** **Relief or reduction** of the patient's radicular arm symptoms during traction. Unusually among provocative tests, **the positive finding is that the patient feels better.**

> [!info] **Performance and the limitation to know.** Sources report **sensitivity around 44%** with **high specificity** — the same pattern as Spurling's. Critically, they also note that **manual distraction relieves cervical facetogenic and discogenic pain as well as radicular pain**, so a positive test does not prove that the pain is radicular in origin. It is used **in combination** with Spurling's, upper limb neurodynamic testing and cervical rotation as a **cluster**, which performs far better than any single test.

> [!warning] **The same contraindications apply as for the compression test** — do not distract a spine that might be unstable or fractured, and stop for any bilateral, lower limb or cord symptoms. Apply traction gently and gradually; a sudden pull is both frightening and unsafe.

> [!danger] **The clinical value is that it points towards a treatment.** A patient whose arm pain is relieved by manual distraction is one in whom **traction-based physiotherapy and postural management** may help — and it gives the patient the reassuring information that a position exists in which the pain is less. **The corollary matters too:** a patient made *worse* by distraction, or one with cord signs, is not a candidate for traction.

> [!danger] **Do not ignore** — as with 0.14, **exclude myelopathy** (clumsy hands, gait disturbance, upper motor neurone signs, bladder symptoms) before treating anything as simple radiculopathy, and re-check for red flags at every review. Progressive weakness or worsening deficit is a surgical referral, not another physiotherapy appointment.

**Alt:** Spurling's compression test (0.14); shoulder abduction (relief) sign; upper limb neurodynamic tests; the validated cervical radiculopathy test cluster; **MRI cervical spine**; EMG/NCS.
