## 1.13 Cranial Nerve Examination

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this, but it's implied by extensive content already built in [[04_Neurology]] (Bell's Palsy, Horner's Syndrome, brainstem stroke syndromes, myasthenia gravis) that depends on a systematic cranial nerve exam — added given how foundational and high-yield this station is.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (testing the nerves of the face and head), gain consent.

**Step-by-step sequence (CN I–XII):**
1. **CN I (olfactory):** ask about any change in sense of smell (rarely formally tested with actual scents in an OSCE setting, but ask the question).
2. **CN II (optic):** visual acuity (Snellen chart), visual fields (confrontation testing — screens for the hemianopia/quadrantanopia patterns detailed in [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation and Bitemporal Hemianopia box), pupillary light reflex (direct and consensual — relevant to Horner's syndrome and raised ICP assessment, see [[04_Neurology]]), fundoscopy (papilloedema — see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP).
3. **CN III, IV, VI (oculomotor, trochlear, abducens):** eye movements in an "H" pattern, screening for diplopia and specific palsies (CN III palsy — ptosis + "down and out" eye + dilated pupil, relevant to raised ICP/herniation; CN VI palsy — cannot look laterally, a false localising sign in raised ICP given its long intracranial course, see [[04_Neurology]] Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumour Cerebri) Hypertension); nystagmus (relevant to cerebellar and vertigo assessment — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo).
4. **CN V (trigeminal):** facial sensation in all three divisions (ophthalmic, maxillary, mandibular), corneal reflex, motor function (clench jaw, feel masseter/temporalis bulk) — relevant to [[04_Neurology]] Trigeminal Neuralgia.
5. **CN VII (facial):** "raise your eyebrows," "screw your eyes shut," "puff out your cheeks," "show me your teeth" — **the single most important test here is whether the forehead is spared or involved**, distinguishing an UMN (stroke) from LMN (Bell's palsy) facial weakness — see [[04_Neurology]] Bell's Palsy for the full explanation of this sign.
6. **CN VIII (vestibulocochlear):** hearing (whisper test or finger rub, formal audiometry if indicated), Rinne and Weber tests to distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss — see the dedicated Rinne and Weber Tests entry below for the full technique and interpretation, not repeated here; ask about vertigo — see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Vertigo.
7. **CN IX, X (glossopharyngeal, vagus):** ask the patient to say "ah" and watch for symmetrical palatal elevation (uvula deviates away from the side of the lesion), gag reflex (rarely tested directly given discomfort, but mention it), swallowing/voice quality.
8. **CN XI (accessory):** shoulder shrug and head turn against resistance (trapezius and sternocleidomastoid).
9. **CN XII (hypoglossal):** tongue protrusion — deviates **toward** the side of a LMN lesion (unlike the uvula, which deviates away) — a commonly confused point worth stating explicitly when narrating.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each nerve as you test it and what a positive/abnormal finding would mean; explicitly state the forehead-sparing distinction when examining CN VII, since this is one of the highest-yield teaching points in the whole cranial nerve exam.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** report by nerve number in order, stating clearly which nerves were normal and which were abnormal, then localise (e.g. "CN VII palsy with forehead involvement, consistent with a lower motor neuron lesion such as Bell's palsy, rather than a central/UMN cause").

**Cross-reference:** see [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation, Bell's Palsy, Horner's Syndrome, and Myasthenia Gravis for the disease-level differentials this exam feeds into.

## 1.14 Peripheral Neurological Examination (Upper/Lower Limb)

> [!note] As with the cranial nerve exam above, no exact-matching CSV row, but this is the essential bedside counterpart to nearly all the [[04_Neurology]] content on weakness, UMN vs LMN patterns, and specific peripheral conditions (GBS, myasthenia, peripheral neuropathy, radiculopathy) — added given its foundational importance.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination, gain consent, expose the limbs appropriately while maintaining dignity.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. **Inspection:** look for muscle wasting, fasciculations (LMN sign — see [[04_Neurology]] Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) Sclerosis), tremor, involuntary movements, scars, deformity, pes cavus/high-arched feet (Charcot-Marie-Tooth, Friedreich's ataxia — see [[04_Neurology]]).
2. **Tone:** assess tone at each major joint — increased tone can be spastic (velocity-dependent "clasp-knife," UMN pattern) or rigid (constant resistance throughout range, extrapyramidal — cogwheel rigidity in Parkinson's, see [[04_Neurology]] Parkinson's Disease (PD)); decreased tone (hypotonia) suggests a LMN, cerebellar, or acute UMN (spinal shock) cause.
3. **Power:** test each major muscle group against resistance, grade using the MRC scale (0 = no movement, through to 5 = normal power); compare proximal vs distal and left vs right — the pattern (proximal-predominant vs distal-predominant, symmetrical vs asymmetrical) is itself diagnostically useful — see [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis.
4. **Reflexes:** biceps, triceps, supinator (upper limb); knee, ankle (lower limb); plantar response (Babinski sign — upgoing plantar is a UMN sign, though normal in infants). Hyperreflexia supports a UMN cause; hyporeflexia/areflexia supports a LMN, NMJ, or peripheral nerve cause (e.g. the classic areflexia of Guillain-Barré Syndrome — see [[04_Neurology]]).
5. **Coordination:** finger-nose-finger test (intention tremor/past-pointing — cerebellar), heel-shin test, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia) — see the DANISH cerebellar mnemonic in [[04_Neurology]] Brain Lesion Localisation.
6. **Sensation:** light touch, pinprick (spinothalamic tract), vibration and proprioception (dorsal columns) — testing each modality separately matters, since a dissociated sensory loss pattern (e.g. loss of one modality with preservation of another) localises to specific tract lesions — see [[04_Neurology]] Spinal Disorders (Brown-Séquard syndrome, subacute combined degeneration) for how these patterns map to specific cord syndromes. Map any deficit to a dermatomal, peripheral nerve, or "glove and stocking" (peripheral neuropathy) distribution.
7. **Gait:** observe walking, including heel walking, toe walking, and tandem (heel-to-toe) gait — screens for foot drop, cerebellar ataxia, and the specific gait patterns associated with named conditions (parkinsonian shuffling gait, high-stepping gait of foot drop — see [[04_Neurology]] Parkinson's Disease (PD) and Charcot-Marie-Tooth Disease).
8. **Complete the examination:** offer to examine the cranial nerves (see above) and check for a sensory level on the trunk if a cord lesion is suspected.

**What to say aloud:** narrate the pattern you're building as you go (e.g. "I'm finding reduced power distally with preserved proximal strength and reduced reflexes, which would suggest a peripheral neuropathy rather than a myopathy").

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** summarise using the UMN/LMN framework — "This patient has [increased/decreased] tone, [pattern of] weakness, [increased/decreased] reflexes, and [an upgoing/downgoing] plantar response, consistent with a [UMN/LMN] lesion at the level of [localisation]." Then state the differential this points toward.

**Cross-reference:** see [[04_Neurology]] Weakness — Differential Diagnosis for the full structured DDx this exam feeds directly into, and Brain Lesion Localisation/Spinal Disorders for how specific patterns localise to specific anatomical levels.

## 1.15 Dermatological Examination

`CF-PAIR` **4 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `Skin lesion morphology — reference terms` · `0.1 Describing a Rash` · `0.1 Assessing a Pigmented Lesion` · `0.5 Nails` — from 09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous.md · G1_Acute_Rash_and_Erythema.md · G5_Lumps__Ulcers_and_Nails.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV (Dermatology category: "Skin and soft tissue surgery including skin cancers," "Skin lesions" among others, Medium yield) — a systematic examination technique without a dedicated template despite 9 full disease-category files having been built ([[09_01_Dermatology_-_Dermatological_Emergencies]] through [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]]). This entry covers the examination approach and systematic lesion description; cross-references the disease sections for diagnosis-specific detail rather than repeating it.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking closely at the skin, and any specific lesion of concern), gain consent, ensure adequate lighting and privacy, expose the relevant area (with observer as appropriate) — a full skin examination should extend beyond just the presenting lesion, given incidental findings are common and occasionally more significant than the presenting complaint.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **General inspection from a distance first:** overall distribution pattern (localised vs generalised, symmetrical vs asymmetrical), general skin colour/pigmentation, obvious scarring or previous excision sites, nail and hair involvement, any signs of systemic illness (pallor, jaundice, cachexia).
2. **Close inspection and systematic description of the primary lesion(s)** — using the standard morphological vocabulary already established in [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Skin lesion morphology — reference terms (macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, pustule, nodule, wheal, erosion, ulcer — not repeated here): **site and distribution**, **size** (measured, not estimated, and photographed/documented for future comparison where malignancy is a concern), **shape** (regular vs irregular border), **colour** (uniform vs variegated), **surface characteristics** (scaly, crusted, weeping, ulcerated, umbilicated), **secondary change** (excoriation from scratching, lichenification from chronic rubbing, secondary infection).
3. **Palpation:** texture (smooth, rough, indurated), temperature (warm suggests active inflammation/infection), tenderness, fluctuance (suggests an abscess/fluid collection), mobility/fixation to underlying structures (fixation raises concern for malignant infiltration), blanching on pressure (the glass test — see [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Non-blanching rashes — approach and differential for the full differential this feeds into, not repeated here).
4. **Regional lymphadenopathy:** examine the draining lymph node basin for any lesion with infective or malignant potential — a routinely-omitted step that is directly relevant to staging in skin cancer and to identifying spread in infective conditions.
5. **Mucosal and nail examination where relevant:** oral mucosa (relevant to the SJS/TEN spectrum, lichen planus, pemphigus — see the relevant disease sections, not repeated here), nail changes (pitting and onycholysis in psoriasis, nail-fold telangiectasia in connective tissue disease, melanonychia/Hutchinson's sign as a red flag for subungual melanoma — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma).

**Dermoscopy (if available/trained):** a magnified, polarised-light examination technique used particularly for pigmented lesion assessment — screens for the specific dermoscopic features referenced throughout [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] (e.g. milia-like cysts and comedo-like openings in seborrhoeic keratosis, arborising vessels and leaf-like pigmentation in pigmented BCC), not a substitute for biopsy when malignancy is suspected but a useful adjunct to clinical judgement.

**Suspicious pigmented lesion assessment:** apply the **ABCDE** framework (Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variability, Diameter >6mm, Evolution) alongside the **"ugly duckling sign"** (a lesion that looks different from the patient's other naevi) — see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]] Melanoma for the full detail and differential, not repeated here.

**What to say aloud:** narrate the systematic description as you go (site, size, shape, colour, surface, palpation findings), rather than jumping straight to a diagnosis — examiners are typically assessing the systematic process as much as the final answer.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe the lesion(s) systematically using the framework above before offering a differential, then state the differential in order of likelihood with the features that support/argue against each (e.g. "this is a 1.2cm, irregularly-bordered, variably-pigmented macule on the back with recent evolution reported — my leading differential is melanoma, given the ABCDE features present, and I would want to proceed to dermoscopy and consider excision biopsy").

**Cross-reference:** see [[09_02_Dermatology_-_Melanocytic_Lesions_and_Mimickers]], [[09_03a_Dermatology_-_Non-Melanoma_Skin_Cancer]], and [[History-Taking]] Skin Lesion / Rash for the disease-level differentials and history-taking approach this exam feeds into.

## 0.1 Assessing a Pigmented Lesion
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!danger] The single most important feature is CHANGE
> **Australia has the highest incidence of melanoma in the world, and melanoma is a leading cause of cancer death in young Australian adults** — which sets the threshold for how seriously a changing lesion should be taken.
> **ABCDE: ASYMMETRY · BORDER irregularity · COLOUR variation within the lesion · DIAMETER (conventionally above about 6 mm) · EVOLVING.**
> **"E" IS THE MOST IMPORTANT. A lesion that is CHANGING — in size, shape, colour, elevation, or by bleeding, itching or ulcerating — warrants assessment regardless of how it scores on the other letters.**
> **A patient who says "this one has changed" is providing better information than any single morphological feature.**

> [!tip] The ugly duckling sign — often more useful than ABCDE
> **Most people's naevi RESEMBLE EACH OTHER — they share a family likeness.**
> **THE UGLY DUCKLING is the lesion that looks DIFFERENT from the patient's others.**
> **This works because it uses the patient as their own control, and it detects the lesion that ABCDE misses in someone with many atypical naevi (where every lesion scores badly on ABCDE) or someone with very few (where a small early melanoma may not yet meet the criteria).**
> **Stand back and look at the whole back at once, rather than examining lesions one at a time through a lens.**

> [!warning] The history that stratifies risk
> **· PERSONAL history of melanoma or keratinocyte cancer — the strongest single risk factor for another.**
> **· FAMILY history of melanoma, particularly in a first-degree relative or at a young age.**
> **· SKIN TYPE — fair skin, red or blond hair, blue eyes, freckling, poor tanning.**
> **· SUN EXPOSURE HISTORY, and specifically the number of episodes of BLISTERING SUNBURN, particularly in CHILDHOOD.** Intermittent intense exposure relates to melanoma; cumulative chronic exposure relates to SCC.
> **· Number of naevi, and the presence of ATYPICAL naevi.**
> **· IMMUNOSUPPRESSION — see 0.3, where the transplant population is discussed. This dramatically changes risk.**
> **· Previous solaria use, outdoor occupation, and prior phototherapy.**

> [!danger] Examine the whole skin, and if in doubt, excise
> **A "check this mole" consultation should include a FULL SKIN EXAMINATION — including the SCALP (part the hair), BEHIND THE EARS, BETWEEN THE TOES, the SOLES, the NAILS, and the genital and perianal skin where appropriate and consented.**
> **Melanomas occur at sites that never see the sun, and acral and subungual melanomas are diagnosed late precisely because nobody looks.**
> **AND THE OPERATING RULE: IF YOU ARE NOT SURE, EXCISE IT OR REFER IT. DO NOT "WATCH" A SUSPICIOUS LESION.**
> **"Review in three months" applied to a lesion that concerns you is how thin, curable melanomas become thick ones — and the patient frequently does not return.**
> **Serial photography has a role for monitoring multiple naevi in high-risk patients under specialist follow-up; it is not a substitute for excising a lesion you are worried about.**

## Skin lesion morphology — reference terms
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!info] Flat, non-palpable changes in skin colour
> - **Macule:** flat, non-palpable change in skin colour, <0.5cm diameter. Freckles are pigmented macules.
> - **Patch:** flat, non-palpable change in skin colour, >0.5cm diameter.

> [!info] Elevation due to fluid in a cavity
> - **Vesicle/Blister:** fluid within the upper layers of skin, <0.5cm diameter.
> - **Bulla:** large, fluid-filled lesion below the epidermis, >1.0cm diameter.
> - **Pustule:** collection of pus in the subcutis, >0.5cm diameter.

> [!info] Elevation due to solid masses
> - **Papule:** raised area, <0.5cm diameter.
> - **Plaque:** raised area, >2cm diameter.
> - **Nodule:** mass/lump, >0.5cm diameter.
> - **Wheal:** dermal oedema.

> [!info] Loss of skin
> - **Erosion:** partial epidermal loss, heals without scars.
> - **Ulcer:** full-thickness skin loss.
> - **Fissure:** linear crack.
> - **Atrophy:** thinning of the epidermis.

> [!info] Vascular changes
> - **Telangiectasia:** easily visible superficial blood vessels.
> - **Spider naevus:** single telangiectatic arteriole in the skin.
> - **Purpura:** rash caused by blood in the skin, often multiple petechiae.
> - **Petechiae:** micro-haemorrhage, 1–2mm diameter.

## 0.1 Describing a Rash
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] The vocabulary — because an accurate description gets you a diagnosis over the phone
> **PRIMARY LESIONS:**
> **· MACULE — flat, small, colour change only. PATCH — flat, larger.**
> **· PAPULE — raised, small. PLAQUE — raised, larger, with a flat top. NODULE — raised and DEEPER.**
> **· VESICLE — fluid-filled, small. BULLA — fluid-filled, larger. PUSTULE — pus-filled.**
> **· WHEAL — a transient, oedematous, itchy swelling that moves and resolves within hours (the defining feature of urticaria).**
> **· PURPURA — NON-BLANCHING. PETECHIAE are small; ECCHYMOSES are large.**
> **SECONDARY CHANGES: scale · crust · excoriation · LICHENIFICATION (thickened skin with accentuated markings, from chronic rubbing) · EROSION (loss of epidermis only — heals WITHOUT scarring) · ULCER (extends into dermis — heals WITH a scar) · fissure · atrophy.**
> **CONFIGURATION: annular · TARGETOID · linear · GROUPED/herpetiform · reticulate (net-like) · serpiginous · DERMATOMAL.**
> **DISTRIBUTION: flexural · extensor · PHOTODISTRIBUTED (face, V of neck, dorsal hands, sparing under the chin and behind the ears — the shadowed sites are the clue) · acral · truncal · symmetrical or asymmetrical.**

> [!danger] Two things to do with every rash
> **1. PRESS ON IT. BLANCHING VERSUS NON-BLANCHING is the single most important bedside test in dermatology**, and it separates a benign exanthem from purpura requiring urgent assessment. Use a glass. Cross-refer [[G3]].
> **2. EXAMINE THE WHOLE SKIN, UNDRESSED — plus the MUCOSAE (mouth, eyes, genitals), the NAILS, the SCALP, and the PALMS AND SOLES.**
> **Mucosal involvement changes the diagnosis completely** — it is what distinguishes a straightforward drug rash from Stevens-Johnson syndrome. **A rash examined through a gap in a gown is a rash half seen.**

> [!danger] Erythema is under-recognised in darker skin — and it causes delayed diagnosis
> **Erythema in richly pigmented skin may appear VIOLACEOUS, BROWN, GREY or simply darker rather than red**, and the classic textbook descriptions ("bright red", "salmon pink") are written from white skin.
> **The consequences are documented and serious: DELAYED DIAGNOSIS OF CELLULITIS · under-recognition of erythroderma · missed CYANOSIS and missed pallor · and jaundice detected late.**
> **What to do instead: PALPATE — warmth, induration, tenderness and swelling do not depend on colour · COMPARE with the patient's own unaffected skin, ideally the contralateral side · use good natural light · examine the mucosae, conjunctivae, palms and soles where colour change is easier to appreciate · and ASK the patient what has changed, since they know their own skin.**
> **Also: post-inflammatory HYPER- and HYPOpigmentation are more prominent and more persistent in darker skin, and are a significant cause of distress that should be acknowledged rather than dismissed as cosmetic.**
> **Photograph lesions with consent** — for monitoring, for referral, and because dermatology advice by phone is far better with an image.

## 0.5 Nails
`CF-PAIR §1.15` **This block and Part 1 §1.15 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] Nail signs of systemic disease
> **· CLUBBING — cross-refer [[A4]] 0.1.**
> **· KOILONYCHIA (spoon-shaped) — IRON DEFICIENCY.**
> **· BEAU LINES — transverse grooves across all nails, caused by temporary arrest of nail growth during a severe systemic illness.** **They are useful because they DATE THE INSULT: knowing that nails grow at a roughly predictable rate, the distance from the cuticle indicates how long ago the illness occurred.**
> **· LEUKONYCHIA (white nails) — hypoalbuminaemia, from liver disease or nephrotic syndrome.**
> **· TERRY NAILS (proximal white nail with a distal band) — cirrhosis. HALF-AND-HALF nails — chronic kidney disease.**
> **· SPLINTER HAEMORRHAGES — TRAUMA is by far the commonest cause, but they are a recognised sign of INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS and vasculitis.** Cross-refer [[B5]] 0.1.
> **· PERIUNGUAL TELANGIECTASIA AND ABNORMAL NAILFOLD CAPILLARIES — a genuinely valuable bedside sign of CONNECTIVE TISSUE DISEASE, particularly systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis.** **Look at the nailfolds with a dermatoscope or even an ophthalmoscope in anyone with Raynaud phenomenon — dilated, dropped-out capillaries indicate a secondary rather than primary Raynaud and change the workup entirely.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4 and [[L3]] 0.6.
> **· Nail PITTING, ONYCHOLYSIS and oil-drop change — PSORIASIS.** Cross-refer [[G4]] 0.2.
> **· Yellow nail syndrome — with lymphoedema and pleural effusions.**

> [!danger] Confirm onychomycosis before treating it
> **A substantial proportion of dystrophic nails that look fungal are NOT — the differential includes PSORIASIS, TRAUMA (particularly the great toenails in runners and in people with foot deformity), lichen planus, and — critically — SUBUNGUAL MELANOMA.**
> **SYSTEMIC ANTIFUNGALS ARE NOT TRIVIAL: they require months of treatment, carry HEPATOTOXICITY, and have significant DRUG INTERACTIONS (terbinafine and the azoles).**
> **TAKE NAIL CLIPPINGS AND SUBUNGUAL DEBRIS FOR MICROSCOPY AND CULTURE BEFORE PRESCRIBING** — and take an adequate sample from the affected proximal part, not just a snip of the distal edge.
> **Treating a psoriatic or traumatic nail with months of terbinafine achieves nothing and risks harm.**
> **PARONYCHIA: ACUTE — usually staphylococcal, painful, red, may need incision and drainage. CHRONIC — associated with WET WORK and irritant exposure, with loss of the cuticle and secondary candida; the treatment is AVOIDING MOISTURE AND IRRITANTS plus a topical steroid, and antifungals alone do not work.**

## 1.16 Lymph Node Examination

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this, but it's implied by extensive content already built that depends on a systematic lymph node exam — [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]], [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]], and [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy — added given how foundational and high-yield this examination is, similarly to the Cranial Nerve and Dermatological Examination entries above.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (feeling the lymph node regions), gain consent, adequately expose the relevant areas (neck, axillae, groin at minimum) with appropriate privacy/observer for axillary and groin examination.

**Systematic approach — examine all major node groups even if only one area is symptomatic, given the distinction between localised and generalised lymphadenopathy is itself diagnostically important:**
1. **Head and neck** — a genuinely systematic sub-sequence is expected: submental, submandibular, pre-auricular, post-auricular, occipital, then working down the neck — anterior cervical chain, posterior cervical chain, supraclavicular (palpate from behind the seated patient, which is the standard technique for this examination). **Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy is a particular red flag** — classically associated with intra-abdominal or intra-thoracic malignancy (e.g. Virchow's node on the left specifically, associated with gastric cancer) and should never be dismissed as reactive without further work-up.
2. **Axillary** — examine with the patient's arm relaxed and supported by the examiner (tensing the arm makes axillary examination harder, a common technique error), systematically covering the five axillary node groups (anterior/pectoral, posterior/subscapular, lateral/brachial, central, apical) — directly relevant to breast cancer staging, see the Breast Examination entry below.
3. **Inguinal** — palpate along the inguinal ligament; note that mild, small, non-tender inguinal lymphadenopathy is a common normal finding (reflecting minor lower-limb trauma/infection over a lifetime) in a way that wouldn't be considered normal in other node groups — a useful "don't over-call it" point specific to this site.
4. **Epitrochlear** (medial to the elbow) — a less commonly examined but occasionally high-yield site, associated with specific causes (e.g. some lymphomas, cat scratch disease if the exposure is on the ipsilateral hand/arm — see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Cat scratch disease).
5. **Abdominal/hepatosplenomegaly** — palpate for splenomegaly and hepatomegaly as part of the same systematic assessment, given lymphoproliferative and myeloproliferative disorders frequently cause organomegaly alongside lymphadenopathy (see [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Splenomegaly for the disease-level causes, not repeated here).

**For each palpable node, systematically describe:** site, size (measured), number (single vs multiple/matted), consistency (soft, firm, rubbery, hard/stony), mobility (mobile vs fixed to surrounding structures or skin), tenderness, overlying skin changes (erythema, warmth suggesting infective cause; skin tethering/ulceration suggesting malignant infiltration).

**Key discriminating features — benign/reactive vs malignant pattern:**
- **Reactive/infective:** tender, soft/rubbery, mobile, often with an identifiable local source of infection, may fluctuate in size, associated systemic infective symptoms.
- **Malignant/lymphomatous:** classically non-tender, firm/rubbery to hard, may be matted (fixed to adjacent nodes) or fixed to surrounding tissue, progressive enlargement, associated B-symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss — see [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]] for the disease-level detail on this pattern, not repeated here) — though note this is a general pattern, not an absolute rule, and any persistently enlarged or atypical node ultimately needs appropriate work-up rather than being reassured on examination findings alone.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each node group as you examine it and describe findings systematically using the framework above, rather than jumping to "no lymphadenopathy found" without describing the process.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe distribution (localised vs generalised — a genuinely important distinction, since localised lymphadenopathy points toward a local cause in the draining region while generalised lymphadenopathy broadens the differential to systemic causes per [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy), then the characteristics of the most significant node(s) using the systematic description above, then a differential.

**Cross-reference:** see [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]], [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]], and [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Lymphadenopathy for the disease-level differentials this exam feeds into.

## 1.17 Breast Examination

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 Triple Assessment` — from O7_Breast_Disease.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] No single exact-matching CSV row for this specific examination technique, but it's implied by extensive content already built in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] (breast cancer, fibroadenoma, and the other breast lump differentials) that depends on a systematic breast exam — added given how foundational and high-yield this station is.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination clearly and sensitively (looking at and feeling the breast tissue and the draining lymph node areas), gain explicit consent, **offer and arrange a observer regardless of examiner/patient gender combination** — this is a standard, expected step for this specific examination given its intimate nature, and omitting it is a common and significant OSCE mark-scheme penalty. Position the patient sitting initially for inspection, then reclined at 45° for palpation.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Inspection (sitting, arms relaxed by sides, then raised above the head, then hands pressed on hips to tense pectoral muscles)** — each position can reveal different findings: symmetry, contour, skin changes (dimpling — often more apparent with arms raised, given tethering to underlying tissue becomes more visible under tension; peau d'orange — a specific sign of dermal lymphatic involvement, classically associated with inflammatory breast cancer, see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer), nipple changes (inversion, retraction, discharge, eczematous change — see Paget's disease of the breast in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer, not repeated here), visible masses.
2. **Palpation (reclined at 45°, examine each breast systematically)** — use the flat of the fingers (not fingertips/pinching, which can create a false sense of a "lump" from normal glandular tissue) in a systematic pattern (commonly a clockwise/spiral pattern from the nipple outward, or a quadrant-by-quadrant approach) covering all breast tissue including the axillary tail (the tissue extending into the axilla — a commonly missed area if the examination stops at the anatomical breast border).
3. **For any palpable lump, systematically describe** — using the general lump-description framework already established in [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Fibroadenoma (site, size and shape; colour, contour, consistency; tethering, tenderness; fluctuance ± transillumination, not repeated here) — plus specifically for breast lumps: mobility (mobile vs fixed to skin or underlying chest wall — fixation is a red flag for malignant infiltration), relationship to the nipple.
4. **Nipple examination** — inspect for discharge (note colour/character if present — bloody discharge is a specific red flag, see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Duct papilloma for the classic association, not repeated here), inversion, or eczematous change.
5. **Lymph node examination** — axillary, supraclavicular, and infraclavicular nodes are an essential, integral part of the breast examination, not a separate optional step — see the Lymph Node Examination entry above for the systematic technique, not repeated here; axillary nodal status is directly relevant to breast cancer staging and surgical planning (see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] Breast cancer Mx, not repeated here).

**What to say aloud:** narrate the systematic inspection positions and palpation pattern as you go, and describe any lump found using the full framework above before offering a differential.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe inspection findings first, then palpation findings for each breast systematically, then lymph node findings, then a differential in order of likelihood with the features supporting/arguing against each.

**Cross-reference:** see [[10_12_Oncology_-_Breast]] for the full differential (breast cancer, fibroadenoma, fibrocystic changes, fat necrosis, lipoma, galactocele, phyllodes tumour, mammary duct ectasia, duct papilloma) this exam feeds into, not repeated here.

`CF-PAIR` **2 blocks covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and were NOT merged into this section:** `Fundoscopy is the examination that most often makes the diagnosi` · `0.2 Drugs for Eye Examinations and Procedures` — from B2_Hypertension_Spectrum.md · NEW_Drugs_11_Eye.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking at the back of the eye using a light and lens), gain consent. Dim the room lights — pupil dilation (physiological or pharmacological with tropicamide, if permitted/available) significantly improves the view and is worth doing whenever practical, particularly for a genuinely thorough exam rather than a quick screen.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Ask the patient to fix their gaze on a distant point over your shoulder and keep looking at it throughout — this keeps the eye still and directs your view toward the optic disc as you approach along the correct visual axis.
2. Hold the ophthalmoscope in your right hand and use your right eye to examine the patient's right eye (and left/left for the other side) — standard convention that avoids your head/the instrument blocking the patient's fixation point and keeps you out of each other's way.
3. Start ~30cm away and identify the red reflex first (*why:* an absent or abnormal red reflex is itself diagnostically significant — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Retinal Detachment (red reflex lost), Cataracts (decreased red reflex), and is the standard first screening step in neonates/children for congenital cataract or retinoblastoma; *what:* a normal red-orange reflex; absence suggests a media opacity (cataract, vitreous haemorrhage) or, rarely, a more serious posterior pathology).
4. Move closer (to within a few cm) and locate the optic disc — following a blood vessel toward where it thickens/converges is a reliable way to find the disc if it's not immediately in view.
5. Systematically assess the optic disc: colour (pale suggests optic atrophy), margins (blurred/swollen — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Papilloedema and [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP), cup-to-disc ratio (increased — see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Open-Angle Glaucoma fundoscopy signs).
6. Follow the four major vascular arcades out from the disc in each quadrant, assessing vessel calibre and any AV nicking (see [[05_Ophthalmology]] Hypertensive Retinopathy), haemorrhages, exudates, cotton wool spots (see Diabetic Retinopathy and Hypertensive Retinopathy), and the venous pattern specifically if vein occlusion is suspected (see Retinal Vein Occlusion).
7. Finally, ask the patient to look directly at the light to bring the macula into view (briefly, given this is uncomfortable) — assess for drusen, macular scarring, or haemorrhage (see AMD).

**What to say aloud:** narrate each structure as you assess it and what an abnormal finding at each step would mean, particularly the red reflex check, since this is often specifically assessed as a standalone competency.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe systematically in the same order examined — red reflex, disc (colour/margin/cupping), vessels, retina/quadrants, macula — then state the differential the findings point toward.

**Cross-reference:** see the specific [[05_Ophthalmology]] disease sections referenced throughout above for the disease-level significance of each fundoscopic finding, not repeated here.

## 1.19 Otoscopy

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.1 Examining the Ear and Assessing Hearing` — from F1_Ear.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — the Ophthalmology-category CSV row explicitly named both Fundoscopy and Otoscopy together, and the ENT category (referenced constantly throughout [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]], [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]], and [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]]) is now built, resolving this project's oldest outstanding forward-reference.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (looking inside the ear canal with a light and a cone-shaped attachment), gain consent, ask about pain in either ear first and examine that side last/most gently if pain is present.

**Step-by-step sequence:**
1. Select the largest speculum that comfortably fits the ear canal (a larger speculum gives a better, more stable view and is more comfortable than repeatedly needing to reposition a too-small one).
2. **Straighten the ear canal before inserting the otoscope** — in adults, gently pull the pinna **up and back**; in young children, pull the pinna **down and back** — this genuinely different technique between adults and children reflects the different natural angle of the ear canal at different ages, and using the wrong direction makes the view meaningfully harder to obtain.
3. Hold the otoscope like a pen, with the ulnar side of your hand resting against the patient's face/head — this acts as a stabilising fulcrum, so if the patient moves suddenly the otoscope moves with their head rather than being driven further into the canal, a genuine safety technique rather than just a comfort measure.
4. Insert gently under direct vision, advancing slowly and looking for the tympanic membrane as you go — do not advance blindly or forcefully, given the ear canal has a natural bend and forcing the speculum risks pain or trauma.
5. Systematically inspect the ear canal on the way in: wax (cerumen — may obscure the view and require removal before a full assessment is possible), discharge (colour/character — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Causes of a discharging ear for the differential this feeds into, not repeated here), erythema/swelling (otitis externa — see the same file's Otitis externa entry), foreign bodies.
6. **Systematically assess the tympanic membrane** using the "cone of light" (a reflection normally seen anteroinferiorly, more prominent in children) as an orientation landmark, then work through: colour (pearly grey is normal; red/bulging suggests acute otitis media — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Otitis media (acute); dull/grey/yellow with visible fluid level or bubbles suggests glue ear — see the same file's Glue ear entry), position (bulging vs retracted vs normal), translucency, and any perforation (note the size and position — anterior/posterior, and whether central or marginal, since marginal perforations carry higher cholesteatoma risk — see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] Causes of a discharging ear, where cholesteatoma sits, not repeated here), or a visible landmark abnormality (loss of the light reflex, or the handle/lateral process of the malleus being unusually prominent, both associated with a middle ear effusion per the file above).

**What to say aloud:** narrate the canal findings and each systematic TM assessment point as you go, particularly explicitly stating whether the light reflex and landmarks are visible/normal, since this is often specifically assessed.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe canal findings first (wax, discharge, erythema), then TM findings systematically (colour, position, light reflex, any perforation), then a differential.

**Cross-reference:** see [[13_01_ENT_-_Otalgia__Otitis_Externa__Otitis_Media__Glue_Ear]] for the full disease-level differential (otitis externa, acute otitis media, glue ear, chronic otitis media, cholesteatoma) this exam feeds into, not repeated here.

## 0.1 Examining the Ear and Assessing Hearing
`CF-PAIR §1.19` **This block and Part 1 §1.19 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

> [!tip] Otoscopy — and the part of the drum everyone forgets
> **· PULL THE PINNA UP AND BACK IN ADULTS, DOWN AND BACK IN INFANTS AND YOUNG CHILDREN** — the infant canal runs in a different direction, and pulling the wrong way obscures the view.
> **· Use the LARGEST speculum that comfortably fits (it gives more light and a wider view), hold the otoscope like a pen, and rest your hand against the patient's head so that a sudden movement moves the scope with them.**
> **· EXAMINE THE UNAFFECTED EAR FIRST** — it establishes what normal looks like in that patient and it is less painful.
> **· Be systematic: the pinna and post-auricular area · the CANAL (wax, debris, oedema, discharge, granulation) · then the DRUM — colour, translucency, the light reflex, position (bulging or retracted), mobility, perforation, and any fluid or bubbles behind it.**
> **AND LOOK AT THE ATTIC — THE PARS FLACCIDA — EVERY TIME.**
> **The pars flaccida sits above the lateral process of the malleus, it is small, it is easy to skip, and it is WHERE CHOLESTEATOMA HIDES.** **A retraction pocket or crust in the attic is the finding that matters, and it is missed by clinicians who look only at the pars tensa.** See 0.3.

> [!danger] Tuning fork tests — and the false negative Rinne
> **Use a 512 Hz fork.**
> **RINNE — compare air conduction (beside the ear) with bone conduction (on the mastoid):**
> **· AC > BC ("Rinne positive") = NORMAL or SENSORINEURAL loss.**
> **· BC > AC ("Rinne negative") = CONDUCTIVE loss in that ear.**
> **WEBER — fork on the vertex or forehead, ask where the sound is heard:**
> **· Lateralises TOWARDS a CONDUCTIVE loss.**
> **· Lateralises AWAY FROM a SENSORINEURAL loss.**
> **THE TRAP — THE FALSE NEGATIVE RINNE: in SEVERE UNILATERAL SENSORINEURAL loss, the bone-conducted sound crosses the skull and is heard by the OPPOSITE, normal cochlea — so the test appears to show BC > AC, mimicking a conductive loss.**
> **THE WEBER RESOLVES IT: in a true conductive loss the Weber lateralises TO the affected ear; in a dead ear with a false negative Rinne it lateralises AWAY from it.**
> **Always do both tests together, and interpret them together.** **This matters most in sudden hearing loss — see 0.5.**

> [!tip] The other assessments, and how to talk to a hearing-impaired patient
> **· WHISPERED VOICE TEST — a reasonable bedside screen: mask the other ear, stand behind the patient at arm's length, and whisper a combination of numbers and letters after a full exhalation.**
> **· PURE TONE AUDIOMETRY with air and bone conduction — the formal test, distinguishing conductive from sensorineural and quantifying the loss.**
> **· TYMPANOMETRY — measures middle ear compliance. A FLAT (type B) trace indicates middle ear fluid or a perforation; a negative-pressure (type C) trace indicates Eustachian tube dysfunction.** `UNVERIFIED — the classification and normal values.`
> **· Always also examine the NOSE, THROAT, NECK for lymphadenopathy, and the CRANIAL NERVES — particularly the facial nerve.**
> **COMMUNICATING WITH A HEARING-IMPAIRED PATIENT — practical and consistently done badly:**
> **· FACE THEM, at their level, with your face in good light so they can lip-read. Do not talk while looking at the notes or the screen, and do not talk from behind or while wearing a mask if it can be avoided.**
> **· SPEAK CLEARLY AND SLIGHTLY MORE SLOWLY — do NOT SHOUT, which distorts speech and looks aggressive.**
> **· REDUCE BACKGROUND NOISE — turn off the television, close the door, move away from the nurses' station.**
> **· CHECK THE HEARING AID IS IN, TURNED ON, AND HAS A WORKING BATTERY.** **Hearing aids left in the locker are a routine and preventable cause of "confusion" in hospitalised older people, and they contribute directly to delirium.** Cross-refer [[D2]] 0.2.
> **· WRITE DOWN key information, and confirm understanding.**

## 1.20 Rinne and Weber Tests

> [!note] Gap-filled — referenced as part of the Cranial Nerve Examination (CN VIII) above and constantly assumed throughout [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Hearing loss — differential diagnosis, but the actual technique and interpretation had never been written up as its own entry — a genuinely classic, high-yield bedside pair of tests using a 512Hz tuning fork to distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss before any formal audiometry is available.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination (using a tuning fork to test hearing by bone and air conduction), gain consent. A **512Hz tuning fork** is standard — lower frequencies give a falsely prolonged bone conduction sensation, and higher frequencies decay too quickly to reliably compare, so this specific frequency isn't arbitrary.

**Technique — strike the tuning fork gently on a soft surface (e.g. your elbow or knee, not a hard surface) to set it vibrating without an overly harsh overtone.**

### 1.20.1 Rinne's test (compares air vs bone conduction in one ear at a time)

1. Place the vibrating fork's base firmly on the mastoid process (bone conduction) and ask the patient to say when they can no longer hear it.
2. Immediately move the fork (still vibrating) to just beside the ear canal, prongs facing forward (air conduction), and ask if they can now hear it again.
3. **Normal result (Rinne positive):** air conduction is heard for longer than bone conduction (as expected physiologically, given air conduction is normally more efficient) — the patient can still hear the fork by air after bone conduction has faded.
4. **Abnormal result (Rinne negative):** bone conduction is heard for longer than or equal to air conduction — suggests a **conductive** hearing loss in that ear, given the normal air-conduction advantage is lost or reversed.
5. Repeat on the other ear.

> [!danger] A "false negative" Rinne is a genuinely important pitfall: in **severe unilateral sensorineural hearing loss**, sound conducted through the skull bone during "bone conduction" testing can be picked up by the *opposite, better-hearing* cochlea, giving a falsely negative-looking result (mimicking conductive loss) in an ear that's actually profoundly sensorineural-deaf — this is exactly why Rinne is always interpreted **together** with Weber's test below, never in isolation, since Weber's test resolves this ambiguity.

### 1.20.2 Weber's test (compares bone conduction between the two ears)

1. Place the vibrating fork's base firmly in the midline of the forehead (or vertex).
2. Ask the patient **"where do you hear the sound — in the middle, or more to one side?"**
3. **Normal result:** the sound is heard centrally/equally in both ears.
4. **Lateralises to the affected ear:** suggests a **conductive** loss in that ear — the affected ear's bone conduction pathway isn't competing with as much ambient air-conducted noise, so the sound is paradoxically perceived as louder on the conductively-impaired side.
5. **Lateralises to the unaffected (better-hearing) ear:** suggests a **sensorineural** loss in the other ear — the affected cochlea simply cannot process the sound as well, so the good ear "wins."

**Combining Rinne and Weber to reach a conclusion — this pairing is the actual clinical skill, not either test alone:**

| Rinne | Weber | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Positive (normal) bilaterally | Central | Normal hearing |
| Negative in one ear | Lateralises to the **same** (abnormal) ear | Conductive hearing loss in that ear |
| Positive (normal) in both ears | Lateralises to **one** ear | Sensorineural hearing loss in the **opposite** (non-lateralising) ear |
| Negative in one ear (the "false negative" pitfall above) | Lateralises to the **opposite** ear | Severe sensorineural hearing loss in the "negative" ear — the Weber result reveals the true picture Rinne alone would have missed |

**What to say aloud:** narrate each step and ask the patient to respond clearly at each stage, then explicitly state your interpretation using the combined table logic above rather than reporting Rinne and Weber as two disconnected findings.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** state the Rinne result for each ear, the Weber lateralisation, then the combined interpretation and which side/type of hearing loss this indicates.

**Cross-reference:** see [[13_02_ENT_-_Hearing_Loss__Tinnitus__Vertigo__DDx_Charts_]] Hearing loss — differential diagnosis for the full conductive vs sensorineural differential these findings feed into, not repeated here.

## 1.21 Vertigo Examination (Dix-Hallpike and the HINTS Exam)

`CF-PAIR` **1 block covering this ground arrived in Part 2 and was NOT merged into this section:** `0.2 Acute Vestibular Syndrome and the HINTS Examination` — from D5_Dizziness__Vertigo_and_Gait.md. **Both copies kept in full.**

> [!note] Gap-filled — the Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre and head impulse test are referenced diagnostically throughout [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] and [[History-Taking]] Vertigo and Dizziness, but neither the specific technique nor the broader HINTS bedside exam (a genuinely high-yield central-vs-peripheral discriminator) had ever been written up as an examination entry.

**Opening:** wash hands, introduce, confirm identity, explain the examination clearly (specific head movements/positions to check for dizziness, and watching your eyes), gain consent, **warn the patient explicitly that the test is designed to reproduce their vertigo and this is expected, not a sign something has gone wrong** — given the whole point of Dix-Hallpike is provoking the symptom, an unwarned patient can understandably panic mid-test.

### 1.21.1 Dix-Hallpike manoeuvre (diagnostic test for BPPV)

1. Sit the patient upright on the examination couch with legs extended, positioned so that when they lie back their head will hang slightly off the end of the couch.
2. Turn the patient's head 45° toward the side being tested.
3. Rapidly (but smoothly, not roughly) lower the patient from sitting to lying, with the head extended approximately 20-30° below the horizontal (hanging off the end of the couch), maintaining the 45° rotation throughout.
4. **Observe the eyes closely for nystagmus and ask the patient if they feel their usual vertigo** — a positive test for BPPV shows a brief latency (a few seconds) before onset, then rotatory/torsional nystagmus beating toward the undermost ear, lasting under a minute, with the vertigo and nystagmus **fatiguing** (reducing) on repeat testing — this fatiguability is itself a useful confirmatory feature of BPPV specifically.
5. Slowly return the patient to sitting, then repeat testing the other side if the first side was negative.
6. If positive, the same position can transition directly into the **Epley manoeuvre** (the repositioning treatment for BPPV) — see [[13_03_ENT_-_Deafness_and_Vertigo_Conditions]] Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) for the treatment-level detail, not repeated here.

> [!tip] A **negative** Dix-Hallpike doesn't fully exclude BPPV (particularly if performed between episodes, or if the causative canal isn't the one tested) — a compatible history with a negative single test can still warrant a treatment trial or repeat testing rather than immediately abandoning the diagnosis.

### 1.21.2 HINTS exam (bedside discrimination of peripheral vs central acute vestibular syndrome)

> [!info] HINTS stands for **H**ead Impulse, **N**ystagmus, **T**est of **S**kew — used specifically in a patient with acute, continuous vertigo (not episodic/positional vertigo, which Dix-Hallpike addresses instead) to help distinguish a peripheral cause (vestibular neuronitis/labyrinthitis) from a dangerous central cause (posterior circulation stroke) — genuinely higher-yield for this specific scenario than CT imaging, given early posterior fossa stroke can be missed on CT.

1. **Head Impulse test:** ask the patient to fixate on your nose; briskly and unpredictably rotate their head a small amount to one side, watching their eyes. **Normal (abnormal-appearing) result — a peripheral cause:** a brief corrective saccade (the eyes briefly lose fixation off your nose then rapidly "catch up" back to it) — this reflects a genuinely impaired vestibulo-ocular reflex on the affected side, which is what's expected in peripheral vestibular failure. **Abnormal (normal-appearing) result — concerning for a central cause:** the eyes stay fixated on your nose throughout with no corrective saccade — a **normal-looking** head impulse test in a patient with acute continuous vertigo is the counterintuitive, genuinely important finding that should raise concern for a central cause, since an intact vestibulo-ocular reflex despite significant vertigo suggests the problem isn't peripheral.
2. **Nystagmus:** observe for direction and whether it changes direction with gaze. **Peripheral:** unidirectional, horizontal nystagmus that doesn't change direction with gaze position. **Central (concerning):** direction-changing (bidirectional) nystagmus, or vertical/torsional nystagmus — any of these should raise concern for a central cause.
3. **Test of Skew:** alternately cover each eye while the patient fixates on a distant point, watching for vertical correction as each eye is uncovered. **Peripheral:** no skew deviation. **Central (concerning):** a vertical corrective movement as the covered eye is uncovered (skew deviation) suggests a brainstem/central cause.

> [!danger] The mnemonic for the concerning (central-suggestive) HINTS pattern is sometimes given as "INFARCT": **I**mpulse **N**ormal, **F**ast-phase **A**lternating, **R**efixation on **C**over **T**est — i.e. a normal head impulse test, direction-changing nystagmus, and a positive skew test together suggest a central cause and warrant urgent stroke work-up (see [[Exam_01_ABCDE_Cardioresp_Abdominal_and_Perioperative]] ABCDE Assessment (Acutely Unwell / Deteriorating Patient) and [[04_Neurology]] for the disease-level stroke pathway this feeds into, not repeated here) — genuinely counterintuitive given a "normal" head impulse test result is the worrying one in this specific context, opposite to how a normal test result is usually reassuring.

**What to say aloud:** narrate each of the three HINTS components and your interpretation as you go, and explicitly state whether the overall pattern is peripheral-favouring or central-concerning.

**Presenting findings back to an examiner:** describe the clinical context first (acute continuous vs episodic/positional vertigo, which determines whether Dix-Hallpike or HINTS is the appropriate test), then the specific findings for whichever test was performed, then the resulting differential and any escalation this triggers.

## 0.2 Acute Vestibular Syndrome and the HINTS Examination
`CF-PAIR §1.21` **This block and Part 1 §1.21 cover the same ground. Both kept in full; neither supersedes the other, and they have NOT been reconciled.**

**D:** Acute-onset **continuous** vertigo lasting more than 24 hours, with nausea and vomiting, nystagmus, head-motion intolerance and gait unsteadiness.

**A/P:** The differential is essentially two conditions with opposite implications: **vestibular neuritis** (benign, self-limiting, from inflammation of the vestibular nerve, often post-viral) and **posterior circulation stroke** — cerebellar or brainstem infarction. **A meaningful proportion of patients presenting with isolated acute vertigo have a stroke**, and they are the group most likely to be discharged home.

> [!danger] HINTS is more sensitive than early MRI for posterior circulation stroke
> **Early diffusion-weighted MRI has a false negative rate in the first 24–48 hours for small posterior fossa infarcts.** A properly performed HINTS examination by a trained examiner outperforms it.
> **This is the single most important fact in this file**, because it inverts the instinct to reassure on the basis of a negative scan.

> [!info] HINTS — the three components
> **Only valid in a patient with CONTINUOUS, ONGOING vertigo and nystagmus at the time of examination.** It is **not** applicable to episodic vertigo, and it is **not** applicable to a currently asymptomatic patient — using it there produces dangerously misleading results.
>
> **1. Head Impulse test** — rapid small-amplitude head turn while the patient fixates on your nose.
> **· ABNORMAL (a corrective catch-up saccade) = PERIPHERAL** — the vestibulo-ocular reflex is broken on that side.
> **· NORMAL (eyes stay locked on target) = CENTRAL.** This is counterintuitive: **a normal test is the worrying one.**
>
> **2. Nystagmus** —
> **· Unidirectional, horizontal (with a torsional component), fast phase always in the same direction = PERIPHERAL.**
> **· Direction-changing on gaze to either side, or PURELY VERTICAL, or purely torsional = CENTRAL.**
>
> **3. Test of Skew** — alternate cover test.
> **· Any vertical corrective movement (skew deviation) = CENTRAL.**
>
> **The mnemonic for the central pattern is "INFARCT": Impulse Normal, Fast-phase Alternating, Refixation on Cover Test.**
> **Any ONE central finding makes it central.** All three must be peripheral to call it peripheral.

> [!warning] HINTS-plus, and why hearing loss does not mean peripheral
> Adding **new unilateral hearing loss** to the examination improves sensitivity.
> **New hearing loss with acute vertigo is NOT reassuring** — an **anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) infarct supplies the labyrinth as well as the brainstem and cerebellum, so it can cause hearing loss with vertigo.** The historical teaching that hearing loss localises to the ear is not safe in this setting.

> [!danger] Other features that make it central
> **· Inability to stand or walk unaided.** **A patient with acute vertigo who cannot walk without falling has a central lesion until proven otherwise** — patients with vestibular neuritis veer toward the affected side but can generally walk.
> **· Severe headache or neck pain** — consider **vertebral artery dissection**, especially in a younger patient.
> **· Any other brainstem or cerebellar sign** — diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, facial numbness, limb ataxia, Horner syndrome, hemisensory loss.
> **· Sudden onset**, vascular risk factors, or age.
> **· Deterioration over hours** — cerebellar stroke swells and compresses the brainstem. Cross-refer [[D3]] 0.2.

> [!tip] Vestibular neuritis — treat, but do not over-treat
> Vestibular suppressants (prochlorperazine, promethazine, benzodiazepines) relieve acute symptoms.
> **Limit them to the first few days.** **Prolonged use PREVENTS central vestibular compensation and produces a patient who remains dizzy for months.** This is a common and avoidable iatrogenic problem.
> **Early vestibular rehabilitation — movement rather than rest — accelerates compensation**, and telling the patient to lie still in a dark room is the wrong advice beyond the first day or two. `UNVERIFIED — the recommended duration limit for suppressants, and the evidence for corticosteroids, which is contested.`

**S/Smx:** Continuous vertigo, nausea and vomiting, nystagmus, unsteadiness, and — where central — additional neurological signs.

**Ix:** **HINTS examination in the patient with ongoing nystagmus** (*why:* more sensitive than early MRI, immediate, and free; *what:* the INFARCT pattern versus a peripheral pattern). **Gait assessment — can the patient stand and walk unaided?** (*why:* inability to walk is a strong central indicator and takes seconds to test; *what:* truncal ataxia, falling). **MRI brain with diffusion, including posterior fossa** where central features are present or suspicion persists (*why:* CT is very poor for the posterior fossa; *what:* infarct — noting the false negative rate in the first 48 hours, so **a negative early MRI in a patient with a central HINTS pattern does not exclude stroke and should be repeated**). CT angiography of the vertebral and basilar arteries where dissection or vertebrobasilar disease is suspected (*why:* dissection is a leading cause of posterior circulation stroke in the young; *what:* dissection, stenosis). Audiometry where hearing loss (*why:* characterises the loss and distinguishes labyrinthine from retrocochlear; *what:* pattern and side). ECG and cardiac assessment (*why:* embolic source, and arrhythmia as an alternative cause of "dizziness"; *what:* AF, arrhythmia). Postural blood pressure (*why:* excludes orthostatic hypotension; *what:* postural drop).

### 0.2.1 Mx – Immediate
**HINTS and gait assessment.** Antiemetics and vestibular suppressants short-term. **Urgent imaging and stroke pathway if any central feature.** Admit and observe if the patient cannot mobilise safely.

### 0.2.2 Mx – Definitive
Stroke management per [[D3]], or supportive care with early vestibular rehabilitation for neuritis.

### 0.2.3 Mx – Chronic/long-term
**Vestibular rehabilitation** for persistent symptoms, which is effective and under-referred. Secondary prevention after stroke. Driving advice.
