## 1.1 Chest X-Ray — Systematic Approach

**Why this matters:** CXR is the single highest-yield imaging investigation across the MCQ exam and OSCE image-interpretation stations — it recurs across almost every system (respiratory, cardiac, GI free air, lines/tubes placement) and rewards a consistent systematic approach far more than memorising individual "classic" images in isolation.

### 1.1.1 Step 1 — Check the basics (RIPE or similar)
- **R**otation: clavicle heads should be equidistant from the spinous processes — rotation distorts mediastinal width and can mimic pathology.
- **I**nspiration: count posterior ribs — adequate inspiration usually shows ~8–10 posterior ribs above the diaphragm; poor inspiration can falsely suggest cardiomegaly or basal atelectasis/crowding that isn't real pathology.
- **P**rojection: PA vs AP (AP films — common in unwell/supine/ICU patients — magnify the cardiac silhouette and mediastinum, so cardiomegaly can't be reliably assessed on an AP film) and check for patient positioning (erect vs supine — supine films obscure air-fluid levels and make pneumothorax/effusion harder to detect).
- **E**xposure: vertebrae should be just visible through the cardiac shadow — an overexposed (too dark) or underexposed (too white) film can mask or mimic pathology.

### 1.1.2 Step 2 — Airway
Trachea should be central (or very slightly right of midline normally) — deviation suggests a pulling (volume loss/collapse pulls trachea toward it) or pushing (tension pneumothorax, large effusion, mass pushes trachea away) process. Check the carina and proximal bronchi for any obvious mass/lymphadenopathy.

### 1.1.3 Step 3 — Breathing (lung fields and pleura)
- Compare both lung fields systematically — apex to base, side to side — for consolidation (air-space opacification, may show air bronchograms), collapse (volume loss, crowded structures, elevated hemidiaphragm), masses/nodules, or interstitial change (reticular/ground-glass pattern).
- Check the pleura for effusion (blunted costophrenic angle, meniscus sign — see [[01_Cardiovascular]]/[[02_Respiratory]] Pleural Effusions), pneumothorax (visible pleural line with absent lung markings peripheral to it — see [[02_Respiratory]] Pneumothorax), and pleural thickening/plaques (asbestos-related — see [[02_Respiratory]] Mesothelioma/Asbestosis).
- Zonal distribution of interstitial/fibrotic change is itself diagnostically useful — see the upper vs lower zone fibrosis differentials in [[02_Respiratory]] (IPF section).

### 1.1.4 Step 4 — Cardiac
Cardiothoracic ratio (cardiac width ÷ thoracic width) >50% suggests cardiomegaly — **only valid on an erect PA film**, not AP or supine (see Step 1). Check heart borders for silhouette sign (loss of a normally-visible border indicates adjacent consolidation/collapse — e.g. loss of the right heart border suggests right middle lobe pathology).

### 1.1.5 Step 5 — Diaphragm
Right hemidiaphragm normally sits slightly higher than left. Look for free air under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum — surgical emergency, best seen on an erect film), diaphragmatic elevation (suggests volume loss/collapse on that side, or a subpulmonic effusion, or phrenic nerve palsy), or flattening (hyperinflation, e.g. COPD — see [[02_Respiratory]] COPD).

### 1.1.6 Step 6 — Everything else (bones, soft tissues, lines/tubes)
- Bones: rib fractures, lytic/sclerotic lesions (metastases), shoulder pathology incidentally captured.
- Soft tissues: surgical emphysema (air tracking through tissue planes — check for this specifically post-chest-drain, see [[02_Respiratory]] Pneumothorax), breast shadows (can mimic basal opacification), calcifications.
- Lines/tubes (especially relevant in unwell/ICU patients): endotracheal tube tip should sit ~5cm above the carina; central line tip should sit in the SVC, not the right atrium; NG tube should be clearly below the diaphragm and not coiled in the airway; chest drain position relative to any effusion/pneumothorax.

### 1.1.7 Common patterns worth recognising at a glance (cross-referenced, not re-explained here)
- Batwing/bilateral perihilar shadowing + Kerley B lines + effusions → pulmonary oedema, see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Chronic Heart Failure
- "Ground glass" + "honeycombing" → pulmonary fibrosis, see [[02_Respiratory]] Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF)
- Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy → sarcoidosis (see [[02_Respiratory]] Sarcoidosis) as a leading differential, though also consider TB/lymphoma
- Upper lobe cavitation → TB, see [[02_Respiratory]] Tuberculosis
- Widened mediastinum → aortic dissection, see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Aortic Dissection (though a normal CXR doesn't exclude it)
- Visible pleural line with no distal lung markings → pneumothorax, see [[02_Respiratory]] Pneumothorax
- "Tram-track"/ring shadows → bronchiectasis, see [[02_Respiratory]] Bronchiectasis

### 1.1.8 Common pitfalls / artefacts
- AP/supine films exaggerating heart size — don't diagnose cardiomegaly on a non-erect-PA film.
- Rotation mimicking mediastinal widening or asymmetric lung markings.
- Breast shadows or overlying clothing/jewellery/ECG leads mimicking pathology.
- A normal CXR does not exclude several important diagnoses — early pneumonia, PE, aortic dissection, and mild-moderate asthma/COPD exacerbations can all have an unremarkable CXR.

## 1.2 CT Head — Systematic Approach

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("CT head (trauma/stroke — pairs naturally with X-ray systematic approach)," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as its own systematic-interpretation entry despite the CXR entry above existing for exactly this reason, and despite CT head being used constantly throughout [[04_Neurology]]'s disease entries (Extradural/Subdural Haematoma, Haemorrhagic Stroke, Raised ICP) — this entry covers the *systematic reading approach*; the specific disease-level appearances (biconvex vs crescent shape, density changes over time) are already built in those entries and cross-referenced below rather than repeated here.

**Why this matters:** CT head is the first-line, time-critical investigation in trauma and suspected stroke — a systematic approach matters precisely because missing one abnormality (e.g. spotting an obvious bleed but not continuing to check the rest) is a genuine, recognised error pattern, not a hypothetical risk.

**Mnemonic — "Blood Can Be Very Bad":**

- **Blood:** scan all slices for any evidence of bleeding, including the scalp (extracranial swelling can be the first clue pointing to an underlying injury/fracture beneath it). Acute blood appears bright white (hyperdense) and **becomes progressively less dense over time** — isodense to brain by 1–3 weeks, isodense to CSF beyond ~3 weeks — which is precisely why older/chronic bleeds (e.g. a chronic subdural) can be genuinely harder to spot than an acute one, and may require inferring their presence from mass effect or sulcal effacement rather than the blood itself being obviously visible. See [[04_Neurology]] Extradural (Epidural) Haematoma and Subdural Haematoma for the specific biconvex vs crescent-shaped appearance and why each does/doesn't cross suture lines, and Haemorrhagic Stroke for intraparenchymal appearance, not repeated here.
- **Cisterns:** check the four key CSF-filled cisterns (circummesencephalic, suprasellar, quadrigeminal, sylvian) for effacement (loss of the normal visible space) or blood — **circummesencephalic cistern effacement is one of the earliest signs of raised ICP**, and the suprasellar cistern is where subarachnoid haemorrhage classically collects (~80% of SAH occurs around the circle of Willis/suprasellar region) — see [[04_Neurology]] Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH), not repeated here.
- **Brain:** assess symmetry (sulci/gyri pattern should match side to side), grey-white matter differentiation (**loss of this differentiation is the earliest CT sign of ischaemic stroke**, often subtle and easy to miss early), and midline shift (the falx should sit at the midline — shift indicates a mass effect from haemorrhage, tumour, or oedema).
- **Ventricles:** check size and symmetry — dilated ventricles suggest hydrocephalus (communicating hydrocephalus is often first visible as dilation of the temporal horns specifically, since these are normally slit-like and subtle dilation is easier to spot there); compressed/effaced ventricles alongside effaced cisterns suggests raised ICP from mass effect instead.
- **Bone:** switch to bone windows specifically (bone and acute blood have similar density on standard windows, so bone windows are needed to reliably distinguish fracture from adjacent haemorrhage) — small, linear, non-depressed fractures can be difficult to distinguish from normal sutures; **pneumocephalus** (air within the cranium, appearing very dark/black) is a useful indirect sign of an underlying fracture communicating with an air-containing space (sinus, mastoid).

**Practical point:** work through the entire mnemonic even after finding an obvious abnormality early (e.g. a large extradural hemorrhage) — there can be a second, less obvious finding elsewhere that's easy to miss if scanning stops once something significant is already found.

## 1.3 Spirometry and Peak Flow

**What it measures:** airflow and lung volumes over time, distinguishing obstructive from restrictive patterns and quantifying severity.

**Systematic approach:**
1. Check the FEV1/FVC ratio first — this is the primary determinant of obstructive vs restrictive pattern.
   - **Obstructive** (FEV1/FVC <0.7, or below the lower limit of normal): FEV1 reduced more than FVC. See [[02_Respiratory]] Asthma (reversible) and COPD (fixed) for the key differential — bronchodilator reversibility testing (FEV1 improves ≥12% and ≥200mL) distinguishes them.
   - **Restrictive** (FEV1/FVC normal or increased, but both FEV1 and FVC reduced): see [[02_Respiratory]] Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF), Sarcoidosis, and the pneumoconioses for the interstitial/fibrotic causes; also consider extrapulmonary causes (chest wall deformity, neuromuscular weakness, severe obesity) not covered in this respiratory-focused file.
2. Check the degree of reduction against predicted values (age/sex/height-adjusted) to grade severity (e.g. GOLD staging for COPD — see [[02_Respiratory]] COPD).
3. Peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) is a simpler, more portable measure primarily used for asthma monitoring (diurnal variability, response to treatment) rather than diagnosis — see [[02_Respiratory]] Asthma for the PEFR-based severity classification in acute exacerbations.

**Pitfalls:** effort-dependent — a poor technique/effort can mimic obstruction or simply produce an unreliable trace; always check the flow-volume loop shape (not reproduced here) alongside the raw numbers where available.

## 1.4 Pleural Fluid Analysis (Pleural Tap)

**What it measures:** distinguishes the cause of a pleural effusion by biochemical/cytological analysis of aspirated fluid.

**Systematic approach:**
1. Apply Light's criteria to classify transudate vs exudate — see [[02_Respiratory]] Pleural Effusions for the full criteria and the transudate/exudate differential table.
2. If exudate — check pH, glucose, and Gram stain/culture specifically to screen for a complicated parapneumonic effusion/empyema (pH <7.2, low glucose, positive Gram stain/culture all indicate the need for drainage rather than antibiotics alone) — see [[02_Respiratory]] Empyema and Haemothorax.
3. Cytology — screens for malignant cells if a malignant effusion is suspected.
4. Other fluid markers as clinically indicated (e.g. amylase for a pancreatitis-related effusion, triglycerides for suspected chylothorax).

**Cross-reference:** see [[02_Respiratory]] Pleural Effusions for the full transudate/exudate cause list and [[Procedures]] §1.8 Pleural Aspiration (Procedure) for the practical procedure/technique.

## 1.6 Abdominal X-Ray — Systematic Approach

**Why this matters:** less universally diagnostic than CXR, but still tested for its classic patterns — bowel obstruction, free air (usually better seen on erect CXR — see the CXR entry above), toxic megacolon, and calcifications. A systematic approach avoids missing a subtle but significant finding.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Check the basics:** patient identifiers, projection (supine is standard for AXR — note this means air-fluid levels are NOT well seen, unlike an erect film; free air is far better assessed on an erect CXR, not the AXR itself), adequacy of the film (ensure both hemidiaphragms and the pelvis are included).
2. **Bowel gas pattern — the core of the AXR read:**
   - Distinguish small from large bowel: small bowel — valvulae conniventes cross the full width of the lumen, more centrally located; large bowel — haustra cross only part of the width, more peripherally located, and normally contains more visible faecal residue (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Small Bowel Obstruction (SBO) for this distinction as originally noted there).
   - Measure dilation against the standard thresholds: small bowel >3cm, colon >6cm, caecum >9cm (the caecum's larger normal diameter and thinner wall — Laplace's law — make it the highest-risk site for perforation in large bowel obstruction; see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Large Bowel Obstruction (LBO) Obstruction).
   - Identify the transition point if possible (dilated bowel proximal, collapsed distal) — this favours mechanical obstruction; diffuse dilation without a clear transition point favours ileus (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Ileus).
3. **Look for specific patterns:**
   - "Coffee bean sign" (sigmoid volvulus) or a markedly dilated caecum displaced toward the left upper quadrant (caecal volvulus) — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Sigmoid and Caecal Volvulus.
   - Toxic megacolon — colonic diameter >6cm with loss of normal haustral pattern in the context of severe colitis (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Inflammatory Bowel Disease, Toxic Megacolon).
   - Calcifications — gallstones (only ~10-15% are radio-opaque, so a normal AXR doesn't exclude them), renal tract calculi, pancreatic calcification (chronic pancreatitis, in ~30% — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Chronic Pancreatitis), vascular calcification (aortic aneurysm wall).
   - Rib notching, foreign bodies, surgical clips/mesh (relevant surgical history clues).
4. **Bones and soft tissues:** vertebral bodies, pelvis, femoral heads incidentally captured — don't neglect these in the focus on bowel gas.

**Pitfalls:**
- AXR is a supine film by convention — do not use it to assess for free intraperitoneal air (pneumoperitoneum); an erect CXR is the correct test for that (see the CXR entry above).
- Faecal loading can mimic or mask other pathology — correlate with the clinical picture rather than over-calling constipation as the sole finding.
- A normal AXR does not exclude significant pathology (early obstruction, ischaemia, most gallstones) — CT is far more sensitive and is the next step whenever clinical suspicion remains high despite a normal/equivocal AXR.

## 1.8 CT Chest / CT Abdomen (brief orientation)

> [!note] Brief stub — CT interpretation is primarily taught and practised per clinical scenario rather than as a single systematic "read," so this entry orients rather than exhaustively teaches the technique. Cross-reference the disease-specific CT findings already documented throughout [[01_Cardiovascular]], [[02_Respiratory]], and [[03_Gastrointestinal]] rather than repeating them here — e.g. CT pulmonary angiogram findings under Pulmonary Embolism, CT aortogram under Aortic Dissection, high-resolution CT patterns under IPF and the pneumoconioses, CT findings in pancreatitis/appendicitis/diverticulitis/bowel obstruction/ischaemic bowel under their respective GI sections.

**General principles:** CT is read systematically per body region/organ system (a full "trauma CT" or "CT-TAP" read follows a structured checklist covering every organ in sequence — lungs, mediastinum, liver, spleen, pancreas, kidneys, bowel, vasculature, bones — rather than being interpreted by symptom alone). Contrast phase matters for interpretation: arterial phase is best for vascular pathology and hypervascular lesions (e.g. HCC — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Liver Cancers), portal venous phase is the standard general-abdominal phase, and delayed/excretory phase is used for renal tract assessment. Always check whether IV and/or oral contrast was given, since this changes what can and can't be reliably assessed (e.g. bowel wall enhancement needs IV contrast; luminal detail benefits from oral/rectal contrast).

## 1.9 Ascitic Fluid Analysis (Paracentesis)

**What it measures:** distinguishes the cause of ascites and screens for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) — the abdominal analogue of pleural fluid analysis (see above), with a similarly structured diagnostic approach.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG)** — the key first step, having largely replaced the older transudate/exudate protein-based split used for pleural fluid: SAAG = serum albumin − ascitic albumin.
   - **SAAG ≥11g/L** — portal hypertension is the cause (cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd-Chiari, hepatic/portal vein thrombosis) — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Cirrhosis and Ascites (under Alcohol-Related Liver Disease).
   - **SAAG <11g/L** — non-portal-hypertensive cause (malignancy, TB peritonitis, pancreatic ascites, nephrotic syndrome).
2. **Neutrophil count — screens for SBP specifically:** >250 cells/µL is diagnostic of SBP regardless of culture result — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) (under Alcohol-Related Liver Disease) for the full Mx once this threshold is met. This is the single most important number to check in any diagnostic ascitic tap in a cirrhotic patient, given how easily SBP can present subtly.
3. **Culture** — often negative even with a diagnostic neutrophil count (treat on the neutrophil count, not the culture result) — bedside inoculation of culture bottles improves yield compared to sending fluid in a plain container.
4. **Additional tests as clinically indicated:** total protein (low ascitic protein <15g/L identifies patients at higher SBP risk who may warrant prophylactic antibiotics — see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) for the prophylaxis criteria), cytology if malignancy suspected, amylase if pancreatic ascites suspected, triglycerides if chylous ascites suspected.

**Pitfalls:**
- Don't rely on the older transudate/exudate protein-only classification for ascites the way it's still used for pleural fluid — SAAG is the better-validated and more clinically useful split for ascitic fluid specifically.
- A single ascitic tap with a low neutrophil count doesn't exclude evolving SBP if the clinical picture is highly suggestive — repeat if clinical deterioration continues despite an initially reassuring tap.

**Cross-reference:** see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Ascites (in ArLD) and Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis for full disease-level Mx, and [[Procedures]] §1.11 Abdominal Paracentesis (Procedure) for the practical procedure/technique.

## 1.10 Amylase / Lipase (brief orientation)

> [!note] Brief stub — the full clinical context (why ordered, what result means for acute pancreatitis specifically) is already documented under [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Acute Pancreatitis, so not repeated here. Quick reference: amylase — ~90% sensitive for acute pancreatitis but non-specific (also raised in other acute abdominal pathology, salivary gland disease, renal impairment) and has a shorter half-life, so may normalise in a late presentation. Lipase — more sensitive and specific for pancreatic origin specifically, with a longer half-life making it the better choice for a patient presenting later after symptom onset, though less universally available. Neither level correlates with severity — a very high amylase/lipase does not predict a worse disease course, so don't use the absolute value beyond the diagnostic 3x-ULN threshold (see [[03_Gastrointestinal]] Acute Pancreatitis for the full diagnostic criteria and severity scoring, which uses separate parameters — Glasgow score — rather than the enzyme level itself).

## 1.11 Full Blood Count and Blood Film — Systematic Approach

> [!note] Gap-filled — one of the single most fundamental, high-yield investigations in medicine, without a dedicated systematic-interpretation template despite the extensive anaemia, leukaemia, and platelet/clotting content already built across [[10_04_Haemonc_-_Anaemia_Overview_and_Microcytic_Anaemia]] through [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]]. This entry covers the systematic reading approach and consolidates blood film morphology terms scattered throughout those files into one reference, in the same spirit as the Chest X-Ray and Abdominal X-Ray systematic-approach entries above; disease-specific detail is cross-referenced rather than repeated here.

**Systematic approach — work through each cell line in turn, rather than jumping straight to a diagnosis from the first abnormal number:**

1. **Haemoglobin and red cell indices** — is the patient anaemic (see [[10_04_Haemonc_-_Anaemia_Overview_and_Microcytic_Anaemia]] for the definition, not repeated here)? If so, **MCV is the first branch point**: microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic — see [[10_04_Haemonc_-_Anaemia_Overview_and_Microcytic_Anaemia]] Anaemia — differential diagnosis by MCV and reticulocyte index for the full branching differential (microcytic → iron deficiency/thalassaemia/anaemia of chronic disease/sideroblastic; normocytic → further split by reticulocyte index into haemolytic vs non-haemolytic; macrocytic → megaloblastic vs non-megaloblastic), not repeated here — this single MCV-based branching structure is the backbone of anaemia work-up and worth having genuinely automatic.
2. **White cell count and differential** — is the total WCC raised, low, or normal, and **which specific cell line is driving the abnormality**? A raised WCC driven by neutrophils suggests a different process (bacterial infection, or a myeloproliferative process — see [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]] How to interpret blood results — a quick approach for the specific WCC-magnitude and differential clues distinguishing reactive neutrophilia from CML, not repeated here) than one driven by lymphocytes (viral infection, or CLL — see [[10_01_Haemonc_-_Leukaemias_and_Myeloproliferative_Disorders]] Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL)) or eosinophils (allergy, parasitic infection, or a reactive/neoplastic eosinophilia). A **low WCC/neutropaenia** should prompt the differential in [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] Neutropaenia, not repeated here, and — critically — should always prompt asking about fever/recent chemotherapy given the neutropenic sepsis risk (see [[10_10a_Haemonc_-_Haematological_and_Oncological_Emergencies]] Neutropenic sepsis).
3. **Platelet count** — raised (see [[10_06b_Haemonc_-_Thrombophilia__APS__Thrombocytosis__Methaemoglobinaemia]] Thrombocytosis) or low (see [[10_07_Haemonc_-_Platelet_and_Clotting_Disorders__Neutropaenia]] Thrombocytopaenia — causes by severity), not repeated here — and specifically whether the abnormality is **isolated** (affecting only one cell line, narrowing the differential considerably) or **combined with other cytopaenias/cytoses**, which points toward a global marrow process (aplastic anaemia, marrow infiltration/malignancy, myelodysplasia) rather than a cell-line-specific cause.
4. **Reticulocyte count** — the key test for distinguishing whether anaemia reflects **under-production** (low/inappropriately normal reticulocyte count — marrow failure, iron/B12/folate deficiency, anaemia of chronic disease) or **increased loss/destruction** (raised reticulocyte count — active haemolysis or acute bleeding, with the marrow appropriately compensating) — see the reticulocyte index branch point in [[10_04_Haemonc_-_Anaemia_Overview_and_Microcytic_Anaemia]] Anaemia — differential diagnosis by MCV and reticulocyte index, not repeated here.
5. **Consider the pattern as a whole** — a single abnormal cell line points toward a targeted differential; multiple simultaneously abnormal cell lines (pancytopaenia, or combined cytopaenia + cytosis patterns) point toward a global marrow process and should prompt escalation toward bone marrow examination and haematology referral rather than pursuing single-cell-line explanations separately.

## 1.12 Cardiac Markers (Troponin) and Lactate

`CF-PAIR` **`GER7 §0.4 Troponins and Cardiac Biomarkers` arrived in Part 3 and was NOT merged into this section.** Third home: `Cardio_merged.md §0.5`. All three kept in full.

**Troponin — what it measures:** a marker of myocardial cell injury/necrosis (troponin I or T, released when cardiomyocytes are damaged) — highly sensitive and the cornerstone of the modern ACS diagnostic pathway, but critically not specific to a single cause.

**Systematic approach to an elevated troponin:**
1. **Check the trend, not just a single value** — modern high-sensitivity troponin assays are diagnostic based on a rise and/or fall pattern on serial testing (e.g. 0h/1h or 0h/3h protocols), not a single elevated number. A static, unchanging troponin (even if elevated) points away from acute MI and toward a chronic elevation cause (see below) — see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) for the full STEMI/NSTEMI/unstable angina diagnostic pathway this feeds into.
2. **Correlate with the clinical picture and ECG** — troponin is interpreted alongside symptoms and ECG changes, never in isolation; a rising troponin without a compatible clinical/ECG picture should prompt a search for a non-ACS cause of myocardial injury rather than an automatic ACS diagnosis.
3. **Consider non-ACS causes of an elevated/rising troponin** (myocardial injury without atherothrombotic ACS) — a genuinely broad differential worth knowing explicitly: PE (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Pulmonary Embolism (PE) — RV strain causes troponin release), myocarditis/pericarditis (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Pericarditis), sepsis, severe heart failure decompensation, tachyarrhythmia, renal impairment (reduced clearance causes a persistently mildly elevated baseline, not a true acute rise), Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Takotsubo Cardiomyopathy — troponin rise classically out of proportion to the degree of LV dysfunction seen on imaging), strenuous exercise, cardiac contusion.
4. **A "normal" troponin does not exclude ACS on a single early sample** — this is why serial testing protocols exist; a single normal troponin taken too soon after symptom onset can still miss a true MI.

**Lactate — what it measures:** a marker of anaerobic metabolism/tissue hypoperfusion, used across critical care as a marker of shock severity and resuscitation response.

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Elevated lactate signals tissue hypoperfusion** — most commonly from any form of shock (see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Shock for the cardiogenic/hypovolaemic/distributive/obstructive classification), but also from severe respiratory failure, seizure (transient), or high-dose salbutamol/adrenaline (a recognised drug-induced cause worth knowing, since it can confound interpretation in a patient recently treated for severe asthma or anaphylaxis — see [[02_Respiratory]] Asthma and [[01_Cardiovascular]] Anaphylactic shock).
2. **Trend the lactate, not just the initial value** — a falling lactate on serial measurement is one of the most useful markers of successful resuscitation in sepsis/shock; a static or rising lactate despite treatment signals inadequate resuscitation or ongoing/worsening pathology.
3. **Cross-reference:** see [[01_Cardiovascular]] Shock (Septic shock specifically) for lactate's role in sepsis severity assessment and resuscitation monitoring.

## 1.13 CSF Interpretation (Lumbar Puncture)

> [!note] The full comparison table (opening pressure, appearance, WCC, predominant cell type, protein, glucose, CSF:plasma glucose ratio across normal/bacterial/viral/TB/fungal meningitis) is already built in [[04_Neurology]] CSF Interpretation — not repeated here. This entry adds the systematic interpretation approach and cross-cutting relevance the CSV explicitly notes ("fits psych/neuro differentials and general medicine").

**What it measures:** CSF analysis characterises the composition of the fluid bathing the brain and spinal cord, used across a wide range of presentations beyond meningitis specifically — encephalitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage (xanthochromia), demyelinating disease (oligoclonal bands), Guillain-Barré/CIDP (albuminocytological dissociation), raised/low ICP (opening pressure), and CNS malignancy (cytology).

**Systematic approach:**
1. **Opening pressure first** — measured with the patient lying laterally, before any fluid is removed; raised in bacterial/TB/fungal meningitis and raised ICP generally (see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP and Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension), low in CSF leak/post-LP headache (see [[04_Neurology]] Low ICP).
2. **Appearance** — clear vs turbid/cloudy (suggests a high cell count, typically bacterial) vs xanthochromic (yellow-tinged, from breakdown blood products — the key confirmatory test for subarachnoid haemorrhage when CT is negative or delayed, see [[04_Neurology]] Subarachnoid Haemorrhage (SAH); requires the sample to be taken ≥12h after symptom onset to allow xanthochromia to develop, and is why LP timing relative to symptom onset matters so much in this specific context).
3. **Cell count and predominant cell type** — neutrophil-predominant pleocytosis points bacterial; lymphocyte-predominant points viral/TB/fungal (or partially-treated bacterial meningitis, which can shift the picture); the magnitude of the WCC elevation itself is also informative (bacterial causes are typically much higher than viral — see the comparison table in [[04_Neurology]] for the specific ranges).
4. **Protein** — elevated in infection (degree varies by organism, TB/fungal typically causing the highest elevations) and notably in Guillain-Barré Syndrome/CIDP **with a normal cell count** — this specific pattern (albuminocytological dissociation) is a key diagnostic clue distinguishing GBS from an infective process, see [[04_Neurology]] Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) and Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy.
5. **Glucose and CSF:plasma glucose ratio** — always interpreted relative to a paired serum glucose taken at the same time, since CSF glucose normally tracks serum glucose; a low ratio (<0.66) reflects consumption by bacteria/inflammatory cells and is most pronounced in bacterial and TB meningitis, typically normal or only mildly reduced in viral meningitis.
6. **Additional targeted tests depending on clinical suspicion:** PCR (HSV/VZV/enterovirus for suspected viral meningitis/encephalitis — see [[04_Neurology]] Encephalitis), oligoclonal bands (multiple sclerosis — present in CSF but not serum, indicating intrathecal antibody synthesis, see [[04_Neurology]] Multiple Sclerosis), cytology (suspected CNS malignancy/lymphoma, see [[04_Neurology]] Primary CNS Lymphoma), cryptococcal antigen (see [[04_Neurology]] Cryptococcosis), VDRL (neurosyphilis, see [[04_Neurology]] Tabes Dorsalis).

**Pitfalls:**
- Never perform an LP if raised ICP from a mass lesion is suspected without first excluding this on imaging — risk of coning/herniation (see [[04_Neurology]] Raised ICP for this explicit contraindication).
- A traumatic tap (blood introduced by the needle itself, not true subarachnoid haemorrhage) can be distinguished from true SAH by a falling red cell count between the first and last collection tubes, and by the absence of xanthochromia (traumatic taps don't have time to develop breakdown products) — don't over-call SAH on a bloody sample without checking for this distinction.
- Don't interpret CSF glucose in isolation — always pair with a same-time serum glucose to calculate the ratio, since CSF glucose alone can be misleading in a hyperglycaemic or hypoglycaemic patient.

**Cross-reference:** see [[04_Neurology]] CSF Interpretation for the full bacterial/viral/TB/fungal comparison table, and the specific disease sections cross-referenced throughout above for the disease-level context each CSF pattern feeds into.

## 1.14 Urinalysis and Urine Microscopy, Culture & Sensitivity (MC&S)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Urine MC&S," Renal & Urology category, High yield) — the disease-level Mx this feeds into (UTI, pyelonephritis, glomerulonephritis, AKI) is already built in [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] — not repeated here. This entry covers the systematic interpretation of the test itself.

**What it measures:** urine dipstick gives a rapid, cheap bedside screen; microscopy characterises cells/casts/crystals present; culture identifies any causative organism and its antibiotic sensitivities. The three are complementary — dipstick alone is a screening test, while microscopy and culture provide the diagnostic/localising detail that actually guides Mx in most non-trivial presentations.

**Systematic approach to the dipstick:**
1. **Leukocytes and nitrites together suggest bacterial UTI** (*why:* leukocyte esterase reflects pyuria/inflammation; nitrites reflect bacterial conversion of urinary nitrates — a positive nitrite is fairly specific for infection, though a negative nitrite doesn't exclude it, since not all uropathogens produce nitrite-reducing enzymes and a short bladder dwell time can also cause a false negative; *what:* both positive strongly supports UTI — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Urinary Tract Infections for the full AU-verified Mx pathway this triggers).
2. **Blood (haematuria)** — screens for a wide differential from simple UTI/stones through to glomerular disease and malignancy; the pattern and associated findings (protein, casts on microscopy) help localise the source — see point 4 below.
3. **Protein** — screens for glomerular disease or another cause of proteinuria; quantified further with ACR/PCR if significant — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) (Staging) and Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome for the fuller interpretation framework, not repeated here.
4. **Don't rely on the dipstick alone in specific populations** — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Infectious Cystitis for the explicit warning against routine dipstick testing in patients ≥65yo or with catheters (near-universal false positives in these groups), a genuinely important pitfall.

**Systematic approach to microscopy:**
1. **Cell counts** — pyuria (elevated WBCs) supports infection/inflammation; the presence of red cells alongside supports either a urological (stone, tumour, infection) or glomerular source, distinguished further by red cell morphology and casts below.
2. **Dysmorphic red cells / red cell casts point to a glomerular source** (*why:* red cells damaged in transit through an inflamed glomerular basement membrane and tubules develop characteristic morphological changes not seen when bleeding originates lower in the urinary tract; *what:* supports nephritic syndrome/glomerulonephritis — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome for the fuller diagnostic framework, not repeated here) — in contrast, normal-shaped ("isomorphic") red cells favour a lower urinary tract source (stone, infection, tumour).
3. **Casts more broadly are diagnostically useful** — granular "muddy brown" casts support acute tubular necrosis (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN) Necrosis); white cell casts support pyelonephritis (upper tract infection) rather than simple cystitis (lower tract), a genuinely useful distinguishing feature when the clinical picture is ambiguous.
4. **Crystals** — the specific crystal type can suggest the stone type if urolithiasis is relevant — see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Urinary Tract Stones for the full stone-type comparison table, not repeated here.

**Systematic approach to culture:**
1. **Colony count thresholds matter** — traditionally ≥10⁵ CFU/mL supports a true UTI rather than contamination, though lower thresholds are used in symptomatic patients or with certain collection methods (e.g. catheter specimens), and interpretation should always be combined with the clinical picture rather than applied as a rigid cutoff in isolation.
2. **Contamination is common with poorly-collected midstream specimens** — mixed growth of multiple organisms, or growth of typical skin/perineal flora, suggests contamination rather than true infection, and should prompt a repeat clean-catch specimen rather than treatment, particularly in an asymptomatic patient.
3. **Sensitivities guide targeted therapy** — initial empirical antibiotic choice (see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Infectious Cystitis and Pyelonephritis for the current AU-specific empirical regimens, not repeated here) should be rationalised to the narrowest effective agent once sensitivities return, standard antimicrobial stewardship practice.

**Pitfalls:**
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria (positive culture without symptoms) generally should **not** be treated in most populations, given treatment doesn't improve outcomes and contributes to antimicrobial resistance — the main exceptions are pregnancy and prior to urological procedures likely to cause mucosal bleeding, where treatment is indicated even without symptoms.
- Don't over-interpret an isolated positive dipstick in an elderly or catheterised patient without symptoms — see the specific warning under Infectious Cystitis above.
- A negative dipstick doesn't fully exclude UTI, particularly early in the illness course or with an atypical organism — treat according to the overall clinical picture, not the dipstick result in isolation, if clinical suspicion remains high.

**Cross-reference:** see [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Urinary Tract Infections, Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome, Acute Tubular Necrosis, and Urinary Tract Stones for the disease-level context each finding above feeds into.

## 1.19 Growth Charts and Percentile Interpretation

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Growth charts / percentile interpretation," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as a systematic skill despite growth being referenced constantly throughout the Paediatrics files (congenital hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, malabsorption, genetic syndromes, and many others all reference "faltering growth" or specific growth patterns without a standalone approach to actually reading the chart).

**What growth charts plot:** height/length, weight, and (in infants/young children) head circumference, each plotted against age on a chart specific to the child's sex, given normal growth trajectories genuinely differ between boys and girls — using the wrong chart (or the wrong measurement type, e.g. plotting length instead of standing height inappropriately for the child's age) produces a misleading result.

**Percentiles — the core interpretive concept:** a percentile line shows where a given measurement sits relative to a reference population of the same age and sex — e.g. a child on the 50th percentile for weight is average for their age/sex; a child on the 9th percentile is smaller than 91% of their age/sex peers, but is **not automatically abnormal** — many healthy children track consistently along a percentile line well below the 50th throughout childhood, reflecting their genetic growth potential rather than any pathology.

**The single most important interpretive principle: trend over time matters more than any single measurement.** A child's own trajectory across serial measurements is the key signal — genuinely concerning patterns include:
- **Crossing percentile lines downward** (e.g. a child moving from the 75th percentile to the 25th over several visits) — even if the child remains within a "normal" range at each individual visit, this crossing pattern itself is the red flag, and is a more sensitive marker of a developing problem than any single low reading.
- **A measurement significantly below the parental target/mid-parental height centile** — a child's expected height range can be estimated from parental heights, and a child tracking well below this expected range (even if nominally "on a percentile line") warrants closer assessment.
- **Disproportionate patterns** — e.g. weight falling while height is preserved (suggests a nutritional/malabsorptive or acute illness cause), versus both height and weight falling together (suggests a more chronic process, e.g. an endocrine or chronic disease cause, or a genetic/syndromic short stature) — this height-vs-weight relationship is itself diagnostically useful, not just a matter of recording two numbers separately.

**Head circumference specifically:** plotted the same way, with a **crossing-percentiles pattern being a particularly important red flag** given both microcephaly (crossing down, or persistently very low) and macrocephaly (crossing up, or persistently very high) can be the presenting sign of a range of significant underlying conditions — see [[15_12b_Paeds_-_Brain_Tumours]] Red flags prompting consideration of a brain tumour (macrocephaly specifically flagged there as the most common presenting symptom of a brain tumour in children <4 years old, not repeated here) and the various genetic syndrome entries throughout [[15_20a_Paeds_-_Trisomies_and_Sex_Chromosome_Disorders]] through [[15_21b_Paeds_-_Fragile_X__Achondroplasia__Noonan__Marfan]] for specific conditions associated with abnormal head growth patterns.

**"Faltering growth" (failure to thrive):** see [[History-Taking]] Growth Failure (Faltering Growth) for the history-taking approach to this specific presentation, not repeated here — growth chart interpretation as described above is the objective data underpinning that clinical assessment, not a substitute for the fuller history and examination.

**Cross-reference:** see [[15_07_Paeds_-_Abdominal_Pain__Neuroblastoma__Coeliac_Disease__Malnutrition__Diarrhoea_and_Vomiting]] Coeliac disease in children and Malnutrition, [[15_16a_Paeds_-_Hypothyroidism]], and the genetic syndrome files for specific disease-level causes of growth abnormality this feeds into, not repeated here.

## 1.20 Paediatric Bloods and Imaging — How Interpretation Differs from Adults

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Quick revisit of bloods/imaging as they present differently in kids," Medium yield) — a deliberately brief, high-level orientation rather than an exhaustive reference, given the specific numeric ranges below are exactly the kind of figures that should be checked against current local paediatric reference ranges rather than memorised from a static note.

**The core principle:** many blood results and imaging findings that would be clearly abnormal in an adult are **normal, expected findings in a child**, varying continuously with age — applying adult reference ranges to a paediatric result is a genuine, avoidable source of misinterpretation, not a minor technicality.

**Full blood count — age is a major determinant of normal ranges:**
- **Haemoglobin:** newborns have a physiologically high Hb at birth (reflecting the relatively hypoxic intrauterine environment), which **falls over the first 2–3 months of life** as fetal haemoglobin is replaced ("physiological nadir") — a Hb that would represent significant anaemia in an older child or adult can be an entirely normal finding in a 6–8 week old infant; see [[15_14_Paeds_-_Anaemia__Sickle_Cell__Hereditary_Spherocytosis__HSP]] Anaemia in children — approach for the disease-level interpretation this feeds into, not repeated here.
- **White cell count and differential:** infants and young children normally run a relatively higher lymphocyte-predominant differential compared to the neutrophil-predominant pattern typical in adults — a "reversed" differential that would prompt concern in an adult is often simply the normal paediatric pattern, particularly in the first few years of life.
- **Platelets, electrolytes, and other chemistry:** several reference ranges (e.g. alkaline phosphatase, which is physiologically elevated during periods of active bone growth) differ meaningfully by age and pubertal stage — always interpret against an age-specific paediatric reference range from the reporting laboratory rather than an adult range.

**Chest X-ray — normal paediatric findings that can be mistaken for pathology:**
- **The thymus** is a normal, often prominent mediastinal structure in infants and young children, producing a widened mediastinal shadow (sometimes with a "sail sign" appearance) that can be mistaken for mediastinal pathology by an examiner expecting an adult-pattern mediastinum — it involutes with age and becomes progressively less prominent through childhood.
- Cardiothoracic ratio and expected lung findings also differ somewhat from adult norms, and a degree of variability with respiratory phase/rotation is more pronounced given the practical difficulty of obtaining a perfectly positioned, fully inspired film in a young child — some allowance for technical factors is routinely needed when interpreting a paediatric CXR that might otherwise be over-called as abnormal.

**Vital signs:** already covered under [[Examination]] Approach to Paediatric Examination above — heart rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure normal ranges all vary substantially and continuously with age, and infant cardiac output is regulated predominantly by heart rate change rather than stroke volume, not repeated here.

**Practical principle for any paediatric result:** when a result looks abnormal, the first question should be **"abnormal for what age?"** — check the age-specific reference range (from the reporting lab or current local paediatric guidance) before concluding a finding is pathological, given how many genuinely normal paediatric findings would be flagged as abnormal against an adult range.

## 1.21 Inflammatory Markers (CRP and ESR)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR)," Medium yield) — genuinely absent as a dedicated entry despite being referenced constantly throughout this project's disease files, with only a passing mention of ESR (in the context of Rouleaux formation/myeloma) existing elsewhere.

**The core distinction — different substances, different kinetics, and this is exactly why they're not interchangeable:**
- **CRP (C-reactive protein):** an acute-phase protein produced by the liver in direct response to IL-6 — **rises rapidly, within 6–8 hours of an inflammatory stimulus, and falls quickly once the stimulus resolves** (half-life ~19 hours), making it a genuinely good marker for tracking an acute or rapidly evolving process (e.g. day-to-day response to antibiotic treatment).
- **ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate):** an indirect measure — reflects how fibrinogen and other acute-phase proteins alter red cell aggregation (rouleaux formation increases sedimentation rate) — **rises and falls much more slowly than CRP**, over days, making it better suited to tracking chronic or slowly-evolving inflammatory processes than acute day-to-day changes.

**Practical consequence of the kinetic difference:** in a genuinely acute process (e.g. early bacterial infection), CRP may already be significantly elevated while ESR is still normal or only mildly raised, simply because ESR hasn't had time to catch up — this isn't a discordant or confusing result, it's the expected pattern given the different kinetics, and shouldn't be over-interpreted as excluding an acute process.

**Causes of a discordant CRP/ESR pattern — genuinely useful to recognise rather than dismiss as an inconsistent result:**
- **CRP raised, ESR normal/low:** early acute infection/inflammation (kinetic lag, as above); viral infection (CRP classically rises less than in bacterial infection, though this is a general tendency rather than a reliable rule-out test); steroid use (can blunt ESR specifically).
- **ESR raised, CRP normal/low:** a genuinely useful pattern pointing toward specific conditions where CRP characteristically stays low despite significant inflammation — **SLE** is the classic example (ESR often significantly elevated while CRP remains normal, unless there's a co-existing infection or serositis); also consider conditions altering red cell factors independent of inflammation (anaemia raises ESR; polycythaemia, sickle cell disease, and other RBC-shape abnormalities lower it), paraproteinaemia (multiple myeloma — see [[10_02_Haemonc_-_Lymphomas_and_Multiple_Myeloma]], not repeated here — markedly raised ESR via paraprotein-driven rouleaux formation, often with a relatively unremarkable CRP), and pregnancy (physiologically raises ESR).

**Neither is specific for a cause — both are markers of inflammation in general, not diagnostic of any particular disease.** A raised CRP/ESR should prompt looking for a cause (infection, autoimmune/inflammatory disease, malignancy, tissue damage/necrosis, or another inflammatory process) via the history, examination, and further targeted investigation — not treated as a diagnosis in itself. Conversely, **a normal CRP/ESR does not fully exclude significant pathology** — some genuine inflammatory or infective processes (including some early infections and some autoimmune flares) can present with only modestly elevated or even normal inflammatory markers, so clinical suspicion should still guide further assessment where the picture doesn't fit.

**Practical use — trend over a single value, much like the growth-chart principle established elsewhere in this project:** serial CRP measurements are genuinely useful for tracking response to treatment (e.g. a falling CRP over days of antibiotic therapy is reassuring; a static or rising CRP despite treatment warrants reassessment) — a single isolated value is less informative than the trajectory over time.

## 1.22 Cognitive Screening Tools (MMSE, MoCA, AMTS)

> [!note] Gap-filled — these tools are referenced by name repeatedly throughout [[04_Neurology]] (Vascular Dementia, Alzheimer's Disease, frontotemporal dementia, and Delirium all cite MMSE as their cognitive-testing Ix), and in [[Examination]] Mental State Examination (MSE), but none of these citations actually explain what the tools test or how to interpret a score — this entry fills that gap. Scoped specifically to the tools themselves, not the broader Geriatrics assessment context (falls risk, frailty, polypharmacy), which sits outside a not-yet-uploaded category.

**The core practical distinction — these are screening tools, not diagnostic tests.** A score below or above a given cutoff raises or lowers suspicion of cognitive impairment; it does not itself diagnose a specific dementia subtype or exclude significant pathology, particularly at the milder end of impairment. All are influenced by the patient's baseline education level and cultural/language background, which should be factored into interpretation rather than applying a single fixed cutoff blindly to every patient.

**Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE):**
- 30-point scale, assessing orientation, registration, attention/calculation, recall, and language (including a visuospatial task — copying a design).
- **Traditional cutoff: <24 suggests cognitive impairment** (i.e. 23/24 as the threshold), though this should be interpreted alongside clinical context rather than as an absolute diagnostic line.
- **Genuine limitation, already reflected in this project's own dementia entries:** MMSE weights memory and language heavily but has limited assessment of executive function — it can therefore **under-detect early frontotemporal dementia specifically**, where memory is relatively preserved but executive/behavioural function is significantly impaired (see [[04_Neurology]] for the relevant dementia-subtype detail this feeds into, not repeated here) — a genuine diagnostic pitfall worth holding in mind, not just a general caveat about screening tools.

**Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA):**
- Also scored out of 30, but developed specifically to be **more sensitive than MMSE for milder cognitive impairment**, given a broader assessment of executive function, language, and visuospatial processing (via tasks including trail-making, clock-drawing, and verbal fluency) that MMSE assesses less thoroughly.
- **Cutoff: <26 suggests cognitive impairment** — a higher numerical threshold than MMSE despite the same maximum score, reflecting the tools' different scoring distributions rather than being directly interchangeable (an identical raw score on each test doesn't represent the same level of impairment).
- Takes slightly longer to administer than MMSE (~10–15 minutes) but is increasingly preferred specifically because of its superior sensitivity for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and for executive-function-predominant presentations.

**Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTS):**
- A much shorter 10-item screen (age, time, an address for recall, current year, current location, recognition of two people, date of birth, a historical date, the reigning monarch/head of state, and counting backwards from 20) — genuinely useful as a rapid bedside screen (particularly in acute/inpatient settings, e.g. suspected delirium) rather than a detailed cognitive assessment.
- **Score ≤6/10 suggests cognitive impairment** — a much coarser tool than MMSE/MoCA, appropriately used for a quick initial screen rather than to characterise the pattern or severity of impairment in detail.

> [!danger] **The cutoffs above are not universal, and applying them to the wrong patient manufactures a diagnosis.** All three tools were developed and normed in specific populations, and **score is affected by education, literacy, first language and cultural familiarity with the task** — someone who left school early, or is completing the test in their second language, can score below a cutoff with entirely normal cognition. A "positive" screen in that situation is a property of the instrument, not the patient.
>
> **For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients this is concrete rather than theoretical: MMSE and MoCA are not validated for many, and a validated alternative exists — the KICA (Kimberley Indigenous Cognitive Assessment)** and related instruments, which should be used instead. This is already established in [[04_Neurology]] Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI); it is repeated here because **this is the entry a reader reaches when they want the cutoff**, and a caveat that lives only beside the disease content does not reach them. (Note the scope question recorded as `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` B22 — the entry does not claim national validation for KICA and should not be edited to imply it.)
>
> *The general rule, which applies to every screening cutoff in this file:* **a screening threshold carries its derivation population with it.** Before acting on a score, ask whether the patient resembles the group the instrument was normed in — the same question the [[PH1_Population_Health_and_Research_Literacy]] Study Design and Bias — Reading the Evidence Behind a Recommendation raises about risk scores and trial results.

**Practical principle tying all three together:** the choice of tool should match the clinical question — AMTS for a rapid bedside screen (e.g. as part of an acute confusion/delirium work-up, see [[04_Neurology]] Delirium, not repeated here), MMSE as the most widely used and validated general screening tool, MoCA where mild impairment or an executive-predominant pattern is specifically suspected. None of the three substitutes for a full clinical assessment and collateral history where dementia or a specific cognitive syndrome is genuinely suspected.

## 0.1 Interpreting Any Investigation

> [!danger] A reference range means 95% of a healthy population — so abnormal results are guaranteed
> **Most reference ranges are set to encompass the central 95% of a healthy reference population.**
> **THEREFORE ROUGHLY 1 IN 20 HEALTHY PEOPLE HAS AN "ABNORMAL" RESULT FOR ANY GIVEN TEST, BY DEFINITION.**
> **Order a panel of twenty tests on a well person and the probability of at least one abnormal result approaches certainty.**
> **THIS IS THE MATHEMATICAL CASE AGAINST SHOTGUN TESTING** — each unnecessary test adds a near-independent chance of a false alarm, and each false alarm generates repeat testing, imaging, referral, anxiety and occasionally an invasive procedure.
> **THE QUESTION BEFORE ORDERING: what is the pre-test probability, and what will I DO differently with a high result, a low result and a normal one?** **If the answer is "nothing", do not order it.** Cross-refer [[EBM1]] 0.1 and [[PH1]] 0.3.

> [!warning] Four things that make a number uninterpretable in isolation
> **1. THE RANGE DEPENDS ON THE POPULATION AND THE ASSAY.** **Ranges differ by AGE, SEX, PREGNANCY, and sometimes ancestry — and CRITICALLY BETWEEN LABORATORIES AND ASSAYS.** **A troponin, a TSH or a free light chain result from one laboratory is not directly comparable with one from another.** **When comparing serial results, check they came from the same assay.**
> **2. THE TREND BEATS THE SINGLE VALUE.** A creatinine of 110 means nothing without the baseline; a stable troponin means something different from a rising one.
> **3. PHYSIOLOGICAL STATE ALTERS THE NUMBER** — pregnancy lowers creatinine and urea and raises D-dimer; acute illness suppresses TSH and raises ferritin; posture and tourniquet time alter calcium and potassium.
> **4. AN ABNORMAL RESULT IN THE ABSENCE OF A CLINICAL QUESTION IS USUALLY NOISE.**
> **AND: SOMEONE MUST OWN THE RESULT.** **A test ordered and never looked at is worse than no test — the abnormality exists in the record, nobody acted, and the medicolegal and clinical consequences fall on the system that generated it.** **Pending results at discharge need a NAMED person responsible.** Cross-refer [[EBM1]] 0.5.

## 0.2 Haematinics and Red Cell Folate

> [!tip] Serum folate versus red cell folate — what each actually measures
> **· SERUM FOLATE reflects RECENT INTAKE and fluctuates day to day.**
> **A few days of improved eating — or a hospital diet, or a single supplement — normalises it. Conversely, a brief period of poor intake lowers it in someone with adequate stores.**
> **So a normal serum folate does not exclude tissue depletion, and a low one may reflect nothing more than the last few days.**
> **· RED CELL FOLATE is incorporated into the erythrocyte during erythropoiesis and remains there for the cell's lifespan of about 120 days.**
> **It therefore reflects LONGER-TERM TISSUE STORES and is not perturbed by recent intake — making it a better measure of true folate status.**
> **THE PRACTICAL POSITION: serum folate is adequate for most purposes and is what most laboratories offer by default. RED CELL FOLATE is worth requesting where the serum result is EQUIVOCAL, where INTAKE HAS RECENTLY CHANGED (including admission to hospital, or supplementation started before the test), or where the clinical picture and the serum result conflict.** `UNVERIFIED — availability, cost and reference ranges, which vary by laboratory.`

> [!danger] Red cell folate is falsely low in B12 deficiency — and never treat folate without checking B12
> **THE MECHANISM — the "METHYL FOLATE TRAP": B12 is the cofactor for methionine synthase, the enzyme that converts methyltetrahydrofolate back into the tetrahydrofolate needed for cellular reactions.**
> **In B12 deficiency, folate becomes trapped in the methyl form and cannot be used or retained normally — so RED CELL FOLATE CAN BE LOW IN A PATIENT WHOSE PROBLEM IS ACTUALLY B12 DEFICIENCY.**
> **This is why a low folate result must never be interpreted in isolation.**
> **AND THE RULE THAT FOLLOWS: NEVER GIVE FOLATE ALONE TO A PATIENT WHO MAY BE B12 DEFICIENT.**
> **Folate corrects the anaemia and the macrocytosis while the NEUROLOGICAL damage of B12 deficiency — subacute combined degeneration — progresses unchecked, and it may be precipitated or accelerated.** **The haematological improvement provides false reassurance while the cord degenerates.**
> **CHECK BOTH. If treatment is needed before the B12 result returns, GIVE B12 AS WELL.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.3 and [[D4]] 0.2.

> [!warning] The other haematinics, briefly
> **· FERRITIN is an ACUTE PHASE REACTANT — falsely normal or raised in inflammation, infection, malignancy, liver disease and chronic kidney disease.** **A "normal" ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency in an inflamed patient, which describes most inpatients.** **Interpret it with a CRP, and use TRANSFERRIN SATURATION, which is less affected.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.2.
> **· SERUM B12 is an imperfect assay.** **Where the level is borderline and the suspicion is real, METHYLMALONIC ACID and HOMOCYSTEINE are more sensitive — both rise in B12 deficiency, and homocysteine alone rises in folate deficiency.** **And in FUNCTIONAL B12 deficiency from NITROUS OXIDE use, the serum B12 may be entirely NORMAL while the patient has subacute combined degeneration.** Cross-refer [[N2]] 0.4.

## 0.3 Rheumatoid Factor and Lupus Serology

> [!danger] Autoantibodies are not screening tests
> **THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE: autoantibody tests have POOR POSITIVE PREDICTIVE VALUE in unselected populations, because the conditions are uncommon and the tests are not specific.**
> **Ordering an "autoimmune screen" on a patient with non-specific fatigue and aches produces a positive result often enough to generate referral, anxiety, further testing and sometimes a wrong label — in a patient who does not have connective tissue disease.**
> **ORDER THEM TO ANSWER A SPECIFIC CLINICAL QUESTION RAISED BY THE HISTORY AND EXAMINATION, not to explain symptoms you cannot otherwise explain.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.6.

> [!warning] Rheumatoid factor — what it actually is, and why it is a poor test
> **RHEUMATOID FACTOR IS AN ANTIBODY DIRECTED AGAINST THE Fc PORTION OF IgG — an antibody against antibody. It is not specific to rheumatoid arthritis and never was.**
> **IT IS POSITIVE IN: healthy people, with prevalence RISING WITH AGE · SJÖGREN SYNDROME (in a very high proportion — often higher than in RA itself) · systemic lupus erythematosus and other connective tissue disease · CHRONIC INFECTION, notably INFECTIVE ENDOCARDITIS, tuberculosis and osteomyelitis · HEPATITIS C and CRYOGLOBULINAEMIA · chronic liver and lung disease · and sarcoidosis.**
> **TWO CONSEQUENCES:**
> **1. A POSITIVE RF DOES NOT DIAGNOSE RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS.** **In a patient with joint pain and a positive RF, the differential remains wide — and a positive RF with fever and a murmur should make you think of endocarditis, not rheumatology.**
> **2. A NEGATIVE RF DOES NOT EXCLUDE IT.** **SERONEGATIVE RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS is common, and a substantial proportion of patients with genuine erosive RA are RF-negative.** **Treating a negative RF as reassurance delays diagnosis in a disease where the window for preventing erosion is measured in months.**
> **ANTI-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) IS THE BETTER TEST: far MORE SPECIFIC for rheumatoid arthritis, frequently positive EARLY (sometimes before symptoms), and PROGNOSTIC — anti-CCP positivity predicts more erosive, aggressive disease.** **If you are asking the RA question, ask for anti-CCP alongside RF.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.2, 0.6.

> [!danger] Lupus investigation — the sequence, and what each test does
> **THE SEQUENCE MATTERS, and ordering everything at once wastes money and generates confusion.**
> **STEP 1 — ANA (antinuclear antibody), the SCREENING test.**
> **HIGHLY SENSITIVE: a NEGATIVE ANA makes SLE very unlikely, and this is the test's main value.**
> **POORLY SPECIFIC: positive in a substantial proportion of HEALTHY people — particularly at LOW TITRE, in WOMEN, and with INCREASING AGE — as well as in other autoimmune disease, chronic infection, malignancy and with certain drugs.**
> **SO A LOW-TITRE POSITIVE ANA IN A PATIENT WITHOUT CLINICAL FEATURES IS NOT A DIAGNOSIS — it is a common finding that causes disproportionate alarm, and explaining that clearly is part of the job.** **The TITRE and the PATTERN both carry information.**
> **STEP 2 — if the ANA is positive and the clinical suspicion holds, the SPECIFIC antibodies:**
> **· ANTI-dsDNA — SPECIFIC for SLE, and it CORRELATES WITH DISEASE ACTIVITY, particularly LUPUS NEPHRITIS. Useful both diagnostically and for monitoring.**
> **· ANTI-SMITH (anti-Sm) — HIGHLY SPECIFIC for SLE but INSENSITIVE, so its absence means nothing and its presence is close to diagnostic.**
> **· The other ENAs — anti-Ro and anti-La (Sjögren, and importantly NEONATAL LUPUS AND CONGENITAL HEART BLOCK in pregnancy) · anti-RNP · anti-Scl-70 and anti-centromere (systemic sclerosis) · anti-Jo-1 (myositis).**
> **STEP 3 — DISEASE ACTIVITY AND ORGAN INVOLVEMENT:**
> **· COMPLEMENT C3 AND C4 FALL in active lupus, because immune complexes CONSUME complement.** **This is the opposite of the acute phase behaviour clinicians expect, and a falling complement with a rising anti-dsDNA indicates a flare.**
> **· ESR RISES in active lupus while the CRP OFTEN STAYS NORMAL** — so **a HIGH CRP in a lupus patient should prompt a search for INFECTION rather than being attributed to the lupus.** **That discrepancy is genuinely useful in an immunosuppressed patient where the distinction between flare and infection is the whole clinical question.**
> **· AND CHECK THE URINE. EVERY TIME.** **LUPUS NEPHRITIS IS SILENT, it determines the prognosis, and it is detected by a dipstick and a urine protein-creatinine ratio.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.4 and [[H1]] 0.3.
> **ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID ANTIBODIES — lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I — REQUIRE PERSISTENT POSITIVITY ON TWO OCCASIONS AT LEAST 12 WEEKS APART, because transient positives are common with infection.** **And note the paradox: the lupus anticoagulant PROLONGS the APTT in vitro while causing THROMBOSIS in vivo.** Cross-refer [[J3]] 0.1, 0.6.

## 0.4 Troponins and Cardiac Biomarkers
`CF-PAIR §1.12` **THREE HOMES, now two of them in this file. Part 1 §1.12 Cardiac Markers (Troponin) and Lactate covers the same assay, and `Cardio_merged.md §0.5 Troponin and Cardiac Biomarkers` is the third. All kept in full, NOT reconciled — this one owns the injury-versus-infarction distinction.**

> [!danger] Troponin is a marker of myocardial INJURY, not of myocardial INFARCTION
> **THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT INTERPRETIVE POINT IN ACUTE MEDICINE, AND HIGH-SENSITIVITY ASSAYS HAVE MADE IT MORE IMPORTANT, NOT LESS.**
> **Troponin is released whenever myocytes are injured, by any mechanism. It says nothing about the cause.**
> **THE FRAMEWORK:**
> **· TYPE 1 MI — atherosclerotic PLAQUE RUPTURE with thrombus. This is what "heart attack" means and what antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation and revascularisation are for.**
> **· TYPE 2 MI — ischaemia from SUPPLY-DEMAND MISMATCH without plaque rupture: severe ANAEMIA, SEPSIS, sustained TACHYARRHYTHMIA, hypotension, hypoxia, severe hypertension, coronary spasm.** **The treatment is the underlying cause, not the cath lab.**
> **· NON-ISCHAEMIC MYOCARDIAL INJURY — MYOCARDITIS · PULMONARY EMBOLISM (right ventricular strain) · heart failure · SEPSIS · renal failure · cardiac contusion · arrhythmia · takotsubo · STROKE AND SUBARACHNOID HAEMORRHAGE · extreme exertion · and cardiotoxic chemotherapy.**
> **THE CLINICAL CONSEQUENCE: A RAISED TROPONIN IN A SEPTIC, TACHYCARDIC 85-YEAR-OLD IS USUALLY TYPE 2 OR NON-ISCHAEMIC INJURY.**
> **Treating that patient as an acute coronary syndrome — dual antiplatelets plus anticoagulation — can cause serious bleeding while the actual problem goes untreated.** **The troponin is telling you the heart is under strain, not that a coronary artery has occluded.** Cross-refer [[B1]] 0.2.

> [!warning] The delta, and the chronically raised troponin
> **THE CHANGE BETWEEN SERIAL SAMPLES — the "DELTA" — IS WHAT DISTINGUISHES ACUTE INJURY FROM CHRONIC ELEVATION.**
> **· A RISING OR FALLING pattern indicates ACUTE myocardial injury.**
> **· A STABLE elevated troponin across serial samples indicates CHRONIC injury.**
> **CHRONIC ELEVATION IS COMMON in chronic kidney disease, heart failure, left ventricular hypertrophy, and in the elderly — and IT IS NOT A FALSE POSITIVE.** **The troponin is genuinely elevated because there is genuinely ongoing low-grade myocardial injury, and it predicts worse cardiovascular outcomes.**
> **But it means a single elevated value in a patient with CKD tells you almost nothing acutely — you need the delta, and ideally a previous baseline.**
> **RAPID RULE-OUT PATHWAYS use high-sensitivity troponin at defined intervals with defined thresholds, integrated with the ECG and clinical risk.** `UNVERIFIED — the assay-specific thresholds and timing, which differ between assays and hospitals and must come from your local pathway.`
> **AND: DO NOT ORDER A TROPONIN WITHOUT A CLINICAL QUESTION.** **An unselected troponin on a sick medical inpatient will frequently be raised, and you will then spend the admission explaining a result you did not need.**

> [!tip] The other cardiac and vascular biomarkers, and their traps
> **· BNP / NT-proBNP — most useful for its NEGATIVE predictive value: a low level makes heart failure unlikely and is a good rule-out in undifferentiated breathlessness.**
> **RAISED by: age, atrial fibrillation, renal impairment, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, right heart strain and valvular disease — so a raised level is not specific.**
> **AND — THE COUNTERINTUITIVE ONE — LEVELS ARE LOWER IN OBESITY.** **So a "normal" BNP in an obese breathless patient does NOT exclude heart failure with the same confidence.** Cross-refer [[CV-X]] 0.2.
> **· D-DIMER — only interpretable at LOW or INTERMEDIATE pre-test probability, where a negative result rules out venous thromboembolism.** **Raised by infection, malignancy, PREGNANCY, surgery, trauma, inflammation and increasing age — hence age-adjusted thresholds.** **At high probability it is useless in both directions.** Cross-refer [[J3]] 0.4.
> **· CK — raised in rhabdomyolysis, myositis, after seizures, after exertion, after intramuscular injection, and in statin-associated myopathy.** Cross-refer [[A7]] 0.6 and [[L3]] 0.2.

## 0.6 Pre-Analytical Error — Where Wrong Results Actually Come From

> [!danger] Most laboratory error happens before the sample reaches the laboratory
> **The analytical phase is highly quality-controlled. The PRE-ANALYTICAL phase — collection, tube, labelling, handling, transport and timing — is where the great majority of erroneous results originate.**
> **Recognising a spurious result saves the patient from being treated for a disease they do not have.**

> [!warning] The spurious results you will actually encounter
> **· HAEMOLYSIS → SPURIOUSLY HIGH POTASSIUM (the single commonest cause of unexpected hyperkalaemia), plus raised LDH and AST and a falsely low sodium.**
> **A haemolysed sample reporting a high potassium in a well patient with a normal ECG is almost always artefact — REPEAT IT before treating.** **But do not dismiss a high potassium in a patient who is unwell, oliguric or on relevant drugs without an ECG and a repeat.**
> **Causes: a small needle, prolonged tourniquet, fist clenching, forcing blood through a needle into the tube, vigorous shaking, and delayed or cold transport.**
> **· PSEUDOHYPERKALAEMIA also occurs with very high PLATELET or WHITE CELL counts (potassium released during clotting in the tube) — send a plasma sample if suspected.**
> **· PSEUDOTHROMBOCYTOPENIA — EDTA-induced platelet clumping. The film shows clumps; repeat in citrate.** **Check this before investigating or transfusing an unexpected isolated low platelet count.** Cross-refer [[J1]] 0.6.
> **· EDTA CONTAMINATION from filling tubes in the wrong order — produces a markedly HIGH POTASSIUM with a very LOW CALCIUM, a combination that should immediately suggest artefact rather than disease.**
> **· SAMPLING FROM A DRIP ARM — dilution, plus spuriously high glucose, potassium or sodium depending on the infusion. Take from the other arm.**
> **· CALCIUM must be CORRECTED FOR ALBUMIN, or measured as ionised calcium — an uncorrected total calcium in a hypoalbuminaemic inpatient is misleading in both directions.** Cross-refer [[I3]] 0.1.

> [!warning] Timing and handling — the samples that must be taken at the right moment
> **· DIGOXIN — at least several hours after the dose; earlier sampling reports a distribution phase level and looks falsely toxic.**
> **· LITHIUM — a trough level at a standardised interval after the dose.**
> **· VANCOMYCIN and aminoglycosides — trough or AUC-based sampling per protocol.**
> **· CORTISOL — timing matters because of diurnal variation.**
> **· PROLACTIN — venepuncture stress itself raises it, so a modestly raised result should be repeated under rested conditions before investigating.**
> **· RENIN AND ALDOSTERONE — posture-, drug- and time-dependent, and several antihypertensives must be withheld beforehand.**
> **· AMMONIA — must go ON ICE and to the laboratory IMMEDIATELY, or it rises spuriously.**
> **· BLOOD GASES — analysed promptly; delay alters pO₂, pCO₂ and lactate.**
> **· LACTATE — a tourniquet and a clenched fist raise it.**
> **· BLOOD CULTURES — BEFORE antibiotics, adequate volume, and from separate sites.** **BUT NEVER DELAY ANTIBIOTICS IN SEPSIS TO OBTAIN THEM** — the antibiotic saves the life and the culture informs the next step. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.7.
> `UNVERIFIED — all sampling intervals.`

> [!danger] Labelling — the error with the worst consequence
> **WRONG-BLOOD-IN-TUBE is the pre-analytical error with the most catastrophic potential, because it causes ABO-INCOMPATIBLE TRANSFUSION.**
> **The controls exist because the error happens: LABEL AT THE BEDSIDE, from the patient's identification band, immediately after taking the sample — never in advance and never at the desk.** **Positively identify the patient by asking them to state their name and date of birth where they are able.**
> **This is the most tedious rule in medicine and it prevents a preventable death.** Cross-refer [[A9]] 0.2.
> **AND FINALLY — POINT-OF-CARE TESTING has real limitations: capillary glucose is unreliable at extremes and in poor perfusion, capillary blood gases approximate arterial values imperfectly, and point-of-care haemoglobin can mislead in acute haemorrhage.** **Confirm a result that will drive a major decision.**
