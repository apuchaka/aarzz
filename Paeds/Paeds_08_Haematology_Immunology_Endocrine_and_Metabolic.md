## Anaemia in children — approach

> [!danger] Red flags: SOB; syncope; fever; lymphadenopathy; bruising; hepatosplenomegaly; jaundice; unexplained bone pain; ↓WCC; haematuria; melaena; faltering growth; ↓platelets.

- **Ix:** FBC, reticulocytes, film, ferritin, CRP/ESR.

> [!info] Differential diagnosis by MCV
> - **Microcytic:** iron deficiency anaemia (↓Fe, ↓ferritin); thalassaemia; G6PD deficiency; sideroblastic anaemia.
> - **Normocytic:** haemolysis; marrow failure; thyroid dysfunction; anaemia of chronic disease.
> - **Macrocytic:** ↓folate (malabsorption, phenytoin); ↓B12 (breast milk from a vegetarian mother, ↓intrinsic factor, malabsorption); Diamond-Blackfan anaemia; Fanconi anaemia.

> [!info] Severe tropical anaemia — malaria; bacteraemia; worms; HIV, TB; ↓B12, G6PD deficiency, malnutrition, sickle cell disease.
> Eosinophilia + recent tropical travel → consider helminth infection (hookworm, roundworm, etc).

### Approach to haemolysis

- Is malaria or sickle cell disease possible?
- Intravascular vs extravascular — intravascular suggests haemoglobinuria; extravascular suggests splenomegaly.
- Inborn error or acquired defect? Acquired: Coombs test.

*(See [[10_04_Haemonc_-_Anaemia_Overview_and_Microcytic_Anaemia]] and [[10_05_Haemonc_-_Normocytic_Anaemia_and_Sickle_Cell_Disease]] for more detail on anaemia in general, not repeated here.)*

## Iron deficiency anaemia in children

- 26% of infants worldwide; peak at 18 months.
- **S/Smx:** less happy baby, ↓psychomotor development, poor cognition. Pallor, lethargy, stomatitis, koilonychia, pica.
- **Dx/Ix:** in persistent IDA, suspect bleeding (menorrhagia, IBD, cow's milk protein allergy, Meckel's diverticulum).
- **Mx:** trial of iron supplementation (e.g. iron fumarate syrup). Aim for a Hb rise of >10g/L/month — response confirms the diagnosis. Warn of the dangers of overdose. Deworm patients.
- **Prevention:** no cow's milk if <1 year old. If formula-fed, use iron-fortified formula, wean at 4–6 months. Adequate vitamin C intake; iron supplement if premature.

## Hereditary spherocytosis

- **D:** inherited abnormality of the RBC caused by defects in structural membrane proteins → sphere-shaped RBCs.
- **A:** autosomal dominant in 75%.
- **R:** family history of splenectomy, anaemia, jaundice, or HS; Northern European ancestry (1 in 5000).
- **P:** spherical RBCs + less flexibility → ↑fragility → selectively removed and destroyed in the spleen → splenomegaly and ↑bilirubin.
- **S/Smx:** failure to thrive; neonatal jaundice, gallstones; splenomegaly; aplastic crisis precipitated by parvovirus infection; degree of haemolysis is variable; MCHC elevated.
- **Dx/Ix:** family history + typical clinical features + labs (blood film — spherocytes, ↑MCHC, ↑reticulocytes) — usually no further tests required. If the diagnosis is equivocal, EMA binding test and cryohaemolysis test. If atypical presentation, electrophoresis analysis of erythrocyte membranes. Osmotic fragility test is not recommended.
- **DDx:** hereditary elliptocytosis — RBCs are ellipse-shaped, also autosomal dominant, similar S/Smx and Mx.
- **Mx:**
  - Acute haemolytic crisis: supportive ± transfusion PRN.
  - Long term: folate replacement; splenectomy ± cholecystectomy if gallstones are an issue.

> [!warning] **Splenectomy is recommended above and neither hereditary spherocytosis entry said what follows it.** Asplenia carries a lifelong risk of overwhelming sepsis from encapsulated organisms, and the preparation is time-sensitive: **vaccination should be given before an elective splenectomy, not after.** [[10_09b_Haemonc_-_Miscellaneous_Haematology]] Hyposplenism owns the vaccination and prophylaxis schedule, and [[08_09_Infectious_Disease_-_Miscellaneous]] Post-splenectomy sepsis owns the acute presentation. Added by the pairs audit (2026-08-29).


## Sickle cell disease

- **D:** autosomal recessive condition that causes sickle-shaped RBCs.
- **R:** family history — if both parents are carriers, the child has a 1 in 4 chance. African, Caribbean, Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, Indian and South-East Asian ancestry. *(A UK carrier-prevalence figure previously quoted here has been removed — it was a UK population statistic in an Australian corpus, and Australia's affected population arises largely through migration, so a UK denominator does not transfer. Ancestry matters for the index of suspicion; a specific Australian carrier rate should not be quoted without a source.)*
- **A:** valine replaces glutamic acid in the β-globin chain → Hb polymerises within the RBC, forming long, stiff fibres of Hb tetramers.
- **P:** triggered by hypoxia and acidosis → vaso-occlusion in small vessels and hypercoagulability. Sickle RBCs are more prone to haemolysis → anaemia. Symptoms only show after ~6 months, as HbF is present until then and can compensate.

### Screening

- Antenatal screening if parents are known carriers, e.g. chorionic villus sampling at 8–10 weeks.
- Screening in newborns.

### S/Smx after 4–6 months

- Swelling of joints, especially dactylitis.
- Leukocytosis in the absence of infection.
- Protuberant abdomen (secondary to enlarged spleen) ± umbilical hernia.
- Cardiac systolic flow murmur secondary to anaemia.
- Maxillary hypertrophy with overbite, due to extramedullary haematopoiesis (not universal).

### Management

- **Dx/Ix:** Hb electrophoresis.
- **Mx of crisis:**
  - Analgesia (e.g. opiates) — must be offered within 30 minutes of presentation; intranasal fentanyl may be used for children.
  - Resuscitation as needed: IV fluids for rehydration, oxygen, etc.
  - Antibiotics if evidence of infection.
  - Blood transfusion — exchange transfusion only if stroke or acute chest syndrome (requires an operating theatre).
- **Mx long-term:**
  - Hydroxycarbamide (↑HbF) — same MOA as hydroxyurea, approved for children.
  - Penicillin prophylaxis from 3 months to 5 years old (erythromycin if penicillin-allergic; lifelong if increased infection risk).
  - Folic acid supplementation if nutritionally insufficient.
  - Pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine every 5 years.
  - Definitive: haematopoietic stem cell transplantation — especially recommended if neurological injury, severe/frequent pain, or recurrent acute chest syndrome.

## Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP, IgA vasculitis)

- **R:** genetics (history of allergy, atopy), M>F 2:1, age 2–10, prior infection (URTI). ↑Age at onset → poorer prognosis.
- **A:** IgA immune complexes prompt an autoimmune response. **P:** tissue deposition of IgA in affected organs (e.g. skin, kidney).
- **S/Smx:** palpable purpuric rash (with localised oedema) over the buttocks and extensor surfaces of the arms and legs; polyarthritis (75%); abdominal pain (50%); features of IgA nephropathy, e.g. haematuria, renal failure (50%).
- **Mx:** supportive — analgesia, hydration. Use of steroids is debatable. Monitor with BP & urine dipstick.
- **Dx/Ix:** bloods (FBC, blood film, renal profile, serum albumin, CRP), blood cultures, urinalysis, urine PCR + observations.
- **DDx:** meningococcal septicaemia, leukaemia, ITP, HUS — rule these out.
- **P:** abdominal pain usually settles within a few days. Patients without kidney involvement can recover in 4–6 weeks. 1/3 of patients recur within 9 months (more common in older patients, generally milder). Very rarely, ESRD (5%).
- **Complications:** intussusception, massive GI bleeds, AKI (rare).

*(See [[12_04_Rheum_-_Vasculitis]] Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP) for the broader vasculitis classification this fits into, not repeated here.)*

## Primary immunodeficiencies

- **D:** group of disorders causing ↑risk of infection and malignancy. Many are X-linked or autosomal recessive genetic conditions.
- Most likely Gram-positive (80%), likely coagulase-negative staph.
- **S/Smx:**
  - Usually present after the first months of life, due to early protection from maternal antibodies.
  - Present with recurrent infections — e.g. ≥2× pneumonia per year; ≥2× meningitis/sepsis ever.
  - Faltering growth.
  - Family history of **PID (primary immunodeficiency** — note this abbreviation means **pelvic inflammatory disease** elsewhere in the corpus, e.g. in the paediatric abdominal pain differential**)**/consanguinity.
  - Unusual pathogens in unusual locations.
  - Persistent candidiasis.
  - Non-healing wounds.
  - Complications with live vaccines.
  - Chronic sinusitis; absent tonsils.
  - Chronic diarrhoea.
- **Dx/Ix:** FBC + differential, blood film, U&Es, CRP/ESR, blood cultures, Ig levels, lymphocyte subsets (T and B cells, monocytes, NK cells), vaccine responses ± HIV tests.
- **Mx:** refer to a paediatric immunologist. In general: treat infections promptly and aggressively; antibiotic prophylaxis; avoid live vaccines; caution with blood products; Ig replacement (SE: headache, abdominal pain, anaphylaxis — prepare adrenaline, hydrocortisone, antihistamines).

> [!note] DDx: a normal child can have ≤12 self-limiting infections a year. Other conditions include atopy, allergic rhinitis, asthma, chronic disease (e.g. GORD with aspiration), acquired immunodeficiency (HIV, malignancy).

### Complications

- **Chest:** bronchiectasis, granulomas, lymphoma.
- **GI:** malabsorption, giardia, cholangitis, atrophic gastritis, colitis.
- **Liver:** acquired hepatitis, chronic active hepatitis, biliary cirrhosis.
- **Blood:** autoimmune haemolysis, ITP, anaemia of chronic disease, aplasia.
- **Eyes:** keratoconjunctivitis, uveitis, granulomas, encephalitis.
- **Others:** septic arthropathy, arthralgia, splenomegaly.

> [!tip] In IgA deficiency, blood products should be given from IgA-deficient donors, or use washed RBCs.

### Disorders by category

- **Neutrophil disorders:** chronic granulomatous disease; Chediak-Higashi syndrome; leukocyte adhesion deficiency.
- **B-cell disorders:** common variable immunodeficiency (CVID); Bruton's agammaglobulinaemia; selective IgA deficiency.
- **T-cell disorders:** DiGeorge syndrome.
- **Combined B & T-cell disorders:** SCID; ataxic telangiectasia; Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome; hyper-IgM syndromes.

*(See [[10_03a_Haemonc_-_Primary_Immunodeficiencies]] for detail on the individual disorders listed above.)*

## Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)

- **D:** a group of inherited genetic disorders characterised by a profound deficiency in cellular and humoral immunity, arising from one of many T-cell maturation defects in the bone marrow or thymus gland. Incidence 1 in 50,000–75,000.
- **R:** family history of SCID, family history of infant death, Native American ethnicity, consanguinity.
- **A:** genetic mutations. 50% have a mutation in the common γ chain on the X chromosome, which codes for IL receptors on T and B cells (X-linked recessive). Other mutations: JAK3, mutations leading to adenosine deaminase deficiency.
- **P:** severe immunodeficiency leads to ↑susceptibility to opportunistic and recurrent infections.
- **S/Smx:** presentation as for PID. Opportunistic infections can be severe and fatal, e.g. chickenpox, *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, CMV.
- **Mx:** the only definitive treatment is haematopoietic stem cell transplant. Others: Ig therapy, sterile environment.

### Omenn syndrome

- A rare cause of SCID (ΔRAG1/2).
- **S/Smx:** erythroderma (red, scaly, dry rash); alopecia; diarrhoea; failure to thrive; lymphadenopathy; hepatosplenomegaly.

*(See [[10_03a_Haemonc_-_Primary_Immunodeficiencies]] for more, and [[15_15a_Paeds_-_ITP_and_Acute_Lymphoblastic_Leukaemia]] for ALL.)*

## Hypothyroidism in children

- **D:** deficiency of thyroid hormones, leading to a generalised slowing of metabolic processes.

## Congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism)

- **Causes:** thyroid dysgenesis (85%); dyshormonogenesis (15%).
- **S/Smx:** mostly late — hence the importance of screening.

> [!warning] Prolonged neonatal jaundice; widely opened posterior fontanelle; poor feeding; hypotonia; dry skin; ± inactivity, ↑sleepiness, hoarse cry, constipation.

- **Late signs:** flat nasal bridge, protruding tongue, umbilical hernia, slowly relaxing deep tendon reflexes, ↓pulse. If untreated, poor growth, cognitive disability.
- **Dx:** Day 5 dried blood spot — tests for ↑TSH to detect primary hypothyroidism. Other tests: T4, thyroid imaging (for secondary or central hypothyroidism), thyroid antibody screen ± pituitary function.

## Acquired hypothyroidism

- **Primary:** prematurity, Hashimoto's thyroiditis (associated with Down syndrome, T1DM, coeliac disease, Turner syndrome), iodine deficiency, drugs, irradiation, etc.
- **Secondary:** hypopituitarism.

## Management

> [!info] Verified against Safer Care Victoria neonatal hypothyroidism guidance, Aug 2026, and current international literature (2023 AAP consensus, multiple recent dosing studies) — **the original ~15mcg/kg/day neonatal starting dose is genuinely too high** compared to current Australian and broader international guidance, not merely a UK-specific figure needing swap-out. Australian guidance recommends starting at **8–10mcg/kg/day as a single daily dose**; international guidelines more broadly cite a 10–15mcg/kg/day range, but recent evidence specifically shows **higher starting doses within that range are associated with a substantial rate of iatrogenic hyperthyroidism at follow-up** (over one-third of infants started at the higher end required dose reduction in one dosing study), while lower starting doses (10–12.5mcg/kg/day) achieve equivalent neurodevelopmental and growth outcomes without this overshoot risk. **The practical adjustment: start toward the lower end of the range (8–10mcg/kg/day) rather than 15mcg/kg/day**, with the tablets ground and mixed with a small amount of milk for administration in neonates, given this age group can't take tablets whole. Treatment should begin as soon as the diagnosis is confirmed, ideally within the first 2 weeks of life, given delayed treatment beyond 6 weeks carries a substantial risk of permanent cognitive impairment — this timing urgency is genuinely internationally consistent, not the part needing correction.

- Lifelong levothyroxine.
  - Neonates: start ~8–10mcg/kg/day as a single daily dose (see the verified note above — genuinely lower than the original 15mcg/kg/day figure), adjust by 5mcg/kg every 2 weeks to a typical dose of 20–50mcg/day.
  - <2 years old: start with 5mcg/kg/day (max 50mcg/day), adjust by 10–25mcg every 2 weeks.
  - >2 years old: start with 50mcg/day, adjust by 25mcg every 2 weeks.
- Adjust according to growth and clinical state. Aim for high-normal T4 and low-normal TSH levels.

*(See [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hypothyroidism for more on thyroid disease in adults, not repeated here.)*

## Hyperthyroidism in children

- **A:** Graves' disease, autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's), thyroid nodules, adenomas, carcinomas.
- **S/Smx:** hyperactivity, irritability, ↑HR, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, heat intolerance, diarrhoea, hyperreflexia, weight loss despite ↑appetite, menstrual irregularity.
- **Specific to Graves':** exophthalmos, proptosis, lid lag, ophthalmoplegia.
- **Mx:**
  1. Dose titration with antithyroid drugs (carbimazole or PTU), OR
  2. Block and replace — induce complete thyroid suppression, replace with levothyroxine.
  3. (For multiple relapses) ablative treatment with radioiodine or thyroid surgery.

### Transient neonatal thyrotoxicosis

- **A:** maternal Graves' disease. **P:** TSHR stimulating antibodies cross the placenta and cause transient thyrotoxicosis in the newborn.
- **S/Smx:** flushing, sweating, irritability, poor weight gain, ↑HR, heart failure.
- Self-limiting, but antithyroid drugs and supportive treatment PRN.

*(See [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hyperthyroidism for more on thyroid disease in adults, not repeated here.)*

## Inherited metabolic diseases (IMDs) — approach

- **D:** conditions involving genetic defects (any mode of inheritance) that cause an enzyme in a cellular pathway to be dysfunctional or absent → block in a metabolic pathway ± toxic byproduct buildup.
- **E:** individual IMDs are rare, but the combined incidence of all IMDs is ~1:800.

> [!danger] General symptoms — consider IMD in the DDx of an acutely unwell child who does not have a clear diagnosis or does not respond to standard therapy. Often misdiagnosed as sepsis.

> [!danger] Red flags: neonatal presentation with a few days of well-being/feeding prior to presentation; previous episodes triggered by illness or fasting, including cyclical vomiting; change in diet precipitating illness (e.g. weaning); positive family history or consanguinity.

### Investigation

- ABG; lactate; glucose; ketones; ammonia; LFTs; CK.
- If IMD suspected: discuss urgently with an IMD centre. Consider: plasma amino acids, acylcarnitine profile, urine organic acids ± DNA, skin biopsy.

### Management

- Stop feeds to contain potentially toxic substrate.
- Provide adequate energy to promote anabolism.
- Diagnose and manage hyperammonaemia and hypoglycaemia.

### Hyperammonaemia

> [!danger] Medical emergency. Complications: cerebral oedema, ↑ICP → neurodisability, death.

- **S/Smx:** lethargy, vomiting, altered consciousness → coma. Early on: ↑RR + respiratory alkalosis.
- **DDx:** urea cycle defects, organic acidaemias, fat oxidation defects, non-IMD causes (liver failure, poor sampling).

## Type 1 diabetes mellitus

- **D:** metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia due to absolute insulin deficiency.
- **R:** genetics/family history — risk is 5% with 1 affected family member.
- **A/P:** autoimmune pancreatic β-cell destruction in genetically susceptible individuals. When 80–90% of β-cells are destroyed, hyperglycaemia develops.
- **S/Smx:** peak age of onset 5–7 years. Weeks of polyuria, polydipsia, lethargy, weight loss ± infection, poor growth, DKA. Secondary nocturnal enuresis.

> [!danger] If suspected in children, refer SAME DAY to the paediatric diabetes MDT.

- **Dx (WHO criteria):** symptoms + ↑blood glucose ≥11.1mmol/L (random); symptoms + ↑blood glucose ≥7mmol/L (fasting); ↑blood glucose on 2 separate occasions.
- **DDx:** T2DM (obesity, Asian or Black ethnicity). Consider monogenic or mitochondrial diabetes in children if diagnosed in the first year of life, absence of ketonaemia during hyperglycaemia, or syndromic features.
- **Ix:** diabetes-specific antibodies can be helpful. C-peptide levels low.
- **Mx:** give BG and ketone monitors. Education of the child + caregivers.

### Insulin regimens

- **Basal bolus:** combination of regular longer-acting insulin with "carb counting" and short- or rapid-acting insulin before meals.
- **Continuous SQ:** insulin pump.
- 1–3 regular injections of mixed insulin.

**Insulin requirements:** start with 0.5–0.75 U/kg/24h (total daily dose).
- If using basal-bolus: divide total daily dose as 1/2 basal insulin + 1/2 in 3 doses before meals.
- If using mixed insulins: give 2/3 pre-breakfast, 1/3 pre-dinner.
- Adjust based on general trends.

### Hypoglycaemia

> [!warning] **This entry treats hypoglycaemia without ever defining it.** No blood glucose threshold is stated anywhere in it — a reader has nothing to act on except the symptoms. The corpus holds **<3.3 mmol/L** for adults ([[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Hypoglycaemia) and **<2.6 mmol/L** for newborns ([[15_23a_Paeds_-_NEC__Neonatal_Hypoglycaemia__Hypotonia]] Neonatal hypoglycaemia, which notes there is no fixed definition), and **neither of those is the figure for a child with diabetes**. Reading the adult number across is the error this box exists to prevent. **The paediatric threshold is not stated here because it has not been verified against an Australian source** — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B55**. Added by the pairs audit, 2026-08-29.

- **S/Smx:** weakness, dizziness, shaking, palpitations, sweating, anxiety, hunger, vomiting; headache, confusion, blurred vision, lethargy, coma, convulsions. Behavioural changes: agitation, irritability.
- **Mx:**
  - 10–20g of fast-acting glucose by mouth + complex carbs. If not possible, give oral glucose gel — 1 teaspoon of sugar moistened with water under the tongue every 10–12 minutes if nothing else available.
  - Or 5mL/kg IV 10% glucose.
  - Severe, out of hospital: glucagon 1mg IM (500mcg if <8 years old or <25kg).
  - Expect quick return to consciousness; recheck BG within 15 minutes.

> [!warning] If BG is normal after hypo treatment, this may be a post-ictal state after a hypoglycaemic fit — DO NOT give more glucose, as this may cause or worsen cerebral oedema.

## Maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY)

- Autosomal dominant, non-ketotic diabetes. A single gene defect, as opposed to T1DM/T2DM, which have polygenic and environmental causes.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| MODY2 | Mild, asymptomatic. Drugs rarely needed, complications rare. |
| MODY3 | Most common type. Severe hyperglycaemia after puberty → often misdiagnosed as T1DM. Sensitive to sulfonylureas — some children may be able to stop insulin. Diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy often occur. |
| MODY5 | Associated with pancreatic atrophy, renal abnormalities, genital tract malformations. |
| MODY1, 4, 6 | Rare. |

- **Dx:** clinical suspicion (young age of onset, often non-obese, strong autosomal dominant family history spanning multiple generations, absence of diabetes-specific autoantibodies and typically preserved C-peptide — features that distinguish MODY from both T1DM and T2DM) confirmed by genetic testing, given the specific gene identified directly determines the management approach below rather than being purely academic.
- **Mx:** genuinely gene-subtype-specific, which is why establishing the exact MODY type matters clinically and isn't just diagnostic labelling — **MODY2** typically needs no pharmacological treatment at all given its mild, stable course (diet/lifestyle monitoring only); **MODY3** is characteristically sulfonylurea-sensitive, and a child previously started on insulin (having been misdiagnosed as T1DM, as above) can often be safely transitioned to a sulfonylurea instead once correctly diagnosed — genuinely important given this changes a lifelong insulin-dependent diagnosis into an oral-therapy-manageable one; the rarer subtypes are managed according to their specific features and associated complications (e.g. MODY5's renal and genital tract anomalies need their own dedicated follow-up beyond glycaemic control alone).

## Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)

- **D:** acute metabolic complication of diabetes, with a biochemical triad of hyperglycaemia (or history of diabetes), ketonaemia, and metabolic acidosis.
- **R:** inadequate or inappropriate insulin therapy, infection, MI, etc.
- **A:** ↓insulin + ↑glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol, growth hormone → metabolic derangements.
- **P:** leads to hyperglycaemia, volume depletion, electrolyte imbalance. Insulin deficiency leads to release of FFA from adipose tissue, hepatic fatty acid oxidation, and formation of ketone bodies → ketonaemia + acidosis.
- **S/Smx:** abdominal pain; polyuria, polydipsia, dehydration; Kussmaul respiration (deep hyperventilation); acetone-smelling breath ("pear-drops" smell).
- **Dx:** glucose >11 or known diabetes; pH <7.3; bicarbonate <15mmol/L; ketones >3 or urine ketones ++.
- **DDx:** hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) — ↑↑BG >30, little or no acidosis or ketones.

> [!note] The **adult** protocol is [[06_Metabolic_Medicine_and_Endocrinology]] Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) — same diagnostic triad and same resolution criteria (pH >7.3, ketones <0.6, bicarbonate >15, within 24h), but a materially different fluid and insulin approach. **Do not read across between them.**

### Management

> [!info] Verified against SA Health's Paediatric Clinical Practice Guideline for DKA in Children, and Queensland Children's Health guidance, Aug 2026 — the overall protocol structure below (deficit replacement over 48h, insulin infusion started only after the first 1–2h of fluids, no insulin bolus, cerebral oedema vigilance) is genuinely consistent with current South Australian and broader Australian practice, not a UK-specific structure needing wholesale replacement. Two genuinely useful SA-specific additions not in the original note: **the corrected sodium formula used to properly interpret sodium trends during treatment — Corrected Na = Measured Na + [0.4 × (glucose − 5.5)] mmol/L** (glucose in mmol/L) — given raw sodium readings are misleading in the hyperglycaemic state and this correction is specifically how SA guidance recommends interpreting them; and **explicit monitoring frequency** — neurological status (GCS) at least hourly (more frequently if any concern, given the cerebral oedema risk below), with bloods (Na, K, Cl, urea, creatinine, calcium, acid-base, blood ketones) 2–4 hourly. SA guidance also specifically recommends **early consultation with a paediatric endocrinologist and the state paediatric retrieval service** given how high-risk and protocol-specific this presentation is — a genuinely SA-specific process point. The exact fluid maintenance rates, potassium replacement thresholds, and other specific numbers below should still be checked against the current local/state protocol directly before clinical use, given these are the kind of figures reasonably updated between guideline versions.

- Severity categorised by degree of acidosis — mild or moderate (pH >7.1), severe (pH <7.1).
- Resuscitation: fluids only if shocked (10mL/kg 0.9% NaCl bolus) — isotonic saline only.
- Rapidly confirm diagnosis: BG, ABG, blood ketones, etc.
- Monitor: GCS (every 30 minutes), ECG (look for hyperkalaemia).
- Start IV fluids: requirement = fluid deficit + maintenance fluids, corrected over 48h. Use lower fluid maintenance rates than APLS.
  - K replacement: if K >5.5, none. If K 3.5–5.5, 40mmol/L. If <3.5, senior review.
- Formal investigations: weight, FBC, U&Es, lab glucose, Ca, infection screen.
- Start IV insulin only after 1–2h of fluids. DO NOT give an insulin bolus — start a fast-acting insulin infusion at 0.1U/kg/h.
- Once BG <14, add glucose to the fluids so the insulin infusion can continue without causing hypoglycaemia.

> [!danger] **The rate previously stated here was "10% dextrose infusion at 125mL/h" — an absolute, adult-sized fixed rate in a paediatric protocol, and it has NOT been corrected because the right figure is not verified.**
>
> **Why it is flagged rather than followed:** 125 mL/h does not scale. For a 50kg adolescent it is roughly maintenance; for a 10kg toddler it is several times maintenance — **in the condition where fluid overload is the mechanism of the complication that kills, cerebral oedema.** It is the same shape as the maintenance-fluid error corrected in the box below (`4mL/kg/h` where the band is a fixed `40 mL/h`), in the same entry.
>
> **What Australian paediatric protocols generally do instead:** change the running bag to a glucose-containing fluid at the **already-calculated weight-based rate**, rather than starting a second fixed-rate infusion alongside it. The total fluid rate is the thing under control in paediatric DKA, so adding an independent infusion is the part that does not fit.
>
> **Do not use a number from this line.** Take the rate from the current SA Health or Queensland Children's Health paediatric DKA guideline. See `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B49**. ### DKA resolution

- Defined as pH >7.3 + blood ketones <0.6 + bicarbonate >15mmol/L.
- Should resolve within 24h — if not, senior review needed.
- If resolved and the patient is eating & drinking, switch to SQ insulin — stop the infusion 30–60 minutes after SQ insulin.

### Complications (from DKA or treatment)

- Gastric stasis; thromboembolism; arrhythmias secondary to hyperkalaemia or iatrogenic hypokalaemia.

> [!danger] Cerebral oedema (iatrogenic) — children are especially vulnerable, especially 4–12h after treatment starts. 1:1 nursing + neuro obs essential. If suspected, CT head + senior review.

- ARDS; AKI.

## Glycogen storage disorders

- **D:** conditions resulting from defects in enzymes required for synthesis and degradation of glycogen.
- **A:** genetic (mostly autosomal recessive). **P:** abnormal glycogen stores are deposited in the liver, muscle, heart, or kidney ± CNS.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| Cori disease (type III) | Hypoglycaemia, hepatomegaly, ↓growth |
| Anderson disease (type IV) | — |
| McArdle disease (type V) | The most common GSD in teens. Secondary to myophosphorylase deficiency → stiffness or myalgia after exercise. Bloods after exercise: ↓lactate and pyruvate ± myoglobinuria. Biopsy: ↓phosphorylase |
| Hers disease (type VI) | — |
| Tauri disease (type VII) | — |

- **Mx:** depends on type. Specialised diets; enzyme replacement therapy; no extreme exercise; prevention of hypoglycaemia (e.g. a bedtime meal of slowly digested complex carbohydrates).

## Phenylketonuria (PKU)

- **D:** autosomal recessive inborn error of amino acid metabolism characterised by ↑blood phenylalanine (>6mg/dL or >363mmol/L). Also known as phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) deficiency.
- **R:** positive family history, white ethnicity.
- **A/P:** no PAH enzyme, or a defective PAH enzyme → ↑blood phenylalanine levels → ↑phenylalanine to the brain → neurotoxicity. Classic PKU leads to gradual cognitive impairment: ↓CNS dopamine, ↓protein synthesis, demyelination.
- **S/Smx:** fair hair, fits, eczema, musty urine.

> [!danger] ↓IQ (e.g. poor spelling, ↓cognition, inability to calculate) if untreated.

- **Dx:** newborn screening — tests for ↑blood phenylalanine (>120µmol/L).
- **Mx:** treatment started if >360µmol/L. Dietary restriction with a phenylalanine-free protein substitute enriched in tyrosine, and <300mg to 8g of natural protein per day (depending on severity). Aim to keep levels <360µmol/L. Adherence may be poor, and other problems (e.g. mental health) may arise.

> [!warning] Maternal PKU: preconception counselling is important. Effects on the baby if maternal phenylalanine is uncontrolled: facial dysmorphism, microcephaly, growth retardation, ↓IQ.

## Lysosomal storage diseases

- **A:** genetic mutations, usually autosomal recessive, but some are sex-linked (e.g. Fabry's disease).
- **P:** accumulation of undegraded substrates; enlargement of cells and organs; ± ↑apoptosis of neurons.

### Fabry's disease

- α-galactosidase deficiency.
- **S/Smx:** torturing, lancinating pains in the extremities ± abdomen — worse in cold, heat, exercise; secondary to neuritis (vasculitis of the vasa nervorum). Angiokeratomata (clusters of dark, non-blanching petechiae) in the "bathing trunk" area, especially the umbilicus and scrotum. ± Paraesthesia, corneal opacities, hypohidrosis, proteinuria, renal failure.
- **Mx:** enzyme replacement therapy ± carbamazepine.

### Tay-Sachs disease

- β-hexosaminidase A deficiency. Gangliosides accumulate in lysosomes, especially in neurons. More common in Ashkenazi Jews.
- **S/Smx:**
  - Infants: hyperacusia, macular "cherry red spot".
  - Juvenile form: optic atrophy, progressive dementia, ataxia, gait disturbance, failure to thrive, joint contracture, depression.
  - Young adults: psychosis, ataxia, dystonia, cataplexy.
