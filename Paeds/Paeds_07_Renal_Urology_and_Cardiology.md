## Urinary tract infection (UTI)

- Bacteriuria = bacteria in urine uncontaminated by urethral flora.
- More common in boys until 3 months old; overall, 8% of girls and 2% of boys have a UTI in childhood. Up to 40% have a GU abnormality.

> [!info] Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) — urine flowing backwards from the bladder to the kidneys via the ureters.
> - Grade I: incomplete filling of the upper urinary tract without dilatation.
> - Grade II: complete filling ± slight dilation.
> - Grade III: ballooned calyces.
> - Grade IV: megaureter.
> - Grade V: + hydronephrosis.
>
> Reflux nephropathy: renal scarring secondary to reflux, which can lead to hypertension and ESRD.

### Presentation

- Infant: poor feeding, vomiting, irritability.
- Younger children: abdominal pain, fever, dysuria.
- Older children: dysuria, frequency, haematuria.
- UTI: temperature >38°C, loin pain/tenderness.

### Investigation

- If asymptomatic, DO NOT test urine.
- Test urine if: symptoms suggestive of UTI; unexplained fever ≥38°C; alternative site of infection identified but remains unwell.
- Urine sample: clean catch preferred; if not, use urine collection pads. Invasive methods (e.g. suprapubic aspiration) as a last resort.

### Management

> [!info] Verified — trimethoprim, cephalexin/other cephalosporins, and amoxicillin all remain genuine current options for paediatric UTI in Australia too (not UK-specific choices), with gentamicin commonly used for upper UTI/pyelonephritis in Australian paediatric antibiotic guidelines specifically (a detail worth adding, given it's less prominent in the original note) — trimethoprim resistance rates should be considered locally given genuine, non-trivial resistance exists in community isolates, but this is a general antimicrobial stewardship consideration rather than an AU-specific correction. Check current eTG or local paediatric antibiogram for the precise current first-line choice and duration given antibiotic guidelines are reviewed regularly and resistance patterns are genuinely local/time-variable.

- Infants <3 months old — immediate referral to paediatrician.
- Upper UTI: consider admission. PO antibiotics (cephalosporin or co-amoxiclav) for 7–10 days.
- Lower UTI: trimethoprim, nitrofurantoin, cephalosporin, or amoxicillin for 3 days — come back if still unwell in 24–48h.
- Consider prophylaxis if recurrent.

## Nephrotic syndrome

> [!info] Defined by the presence of: (1) heavy proteinuria; (2) hypoalbuminaemia (<30g/L); (3) peripheral oedema. Not a single disease, but a syndrome caused by several renal diseases.
>
> [!danger] **The proteinuria threshold here was "> 3.5g per 24h" — the adult figure, in the paediatric entry.** It is an **absolute** quantity, so it does not scale: a 15kg child in florid nephrotic syndrome will never excrete 3.5g in a day, and applying this number to a child excludes the diagnosis it is supposed to define. Paediatric nephrotic-range proteinuria is **indexed to body size** — expressed per square metre per hour, or as a urine protein:creatinine ratio on a spot sample — rather than as an absolute daily mass. **The specific Australian paediatric threshold is not stated here because it has not been verified** — see `PENDING_GUIDELINE_CHECKS.md` **B64**. The absolute figure has been removed rather than replaced. Found by the G26 round, 2026-08-29; the adult entry is [[07_Renal_Medicine_and_Urology]] Nephritic vs Nephrotic Syndrome.

### Aetiology

- **Minimal change disease** — possibly idiopathic; secondary to Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukaemia, etc. Peak onset <6 years old, M:F 2:1.
- Intrinsic kidney disease: FSGS.
- Secondary to systemic illness: HSP, diabetes, infection, amyloidosis.

### Pathophysiology

- Podocytes are affected → glomerular proteinuria.
- Urinary loss of albumin secondary to glomerular damage — the liver tries to compensate for protein loss, and in the process, there is ↑cholesterol production.
- Oedema secondary to ↓oncotic pressure from hypoalbuminaemia + primary renal Na retention.
- Hypercoagulability and thrombosis secondary to loss of anticoagulant proteins.

### S/Smx

- A well child with insidious onset of pitting oedema, initially periorbital, then generalised. Often a history of recent URTI.
- May progress to anorexia, GI changes, irritability, ascites, oliguria, SOB.
- Risk of infection and thrombosis.

### Investigation

- Urine: frothy, protein 3+, PCR >200, Na <10.
- Blood: albumin <25, U&Es, lipids, C3/C4 (normal), Hb.
- MCD: urinalysis will show small molecular weight proteins and hyaline casts.

> [!note] Visible haematuria, hypertension, and markedly ↑creatinine are NOT typical features of nephrotic syndrome.

### Management

> [!info] Verified — the mg/m² body-surface-area-based steroid dosing structure for nephrotic syndrome (high-dose induction, then alternate-day tapering) is a long-established, internationally standard approach (originating from the classic ISKDC regimen and reflected in current KDIGO international guidelines) rather than a UK-specific protocol — this dosing structure is genuinely shared across jurisdictions including Australia, not something requiring wholesale replacement. Check current Australian paediatric nephrology guidance or eTG for the precise current total course duration and exact tapering schedule given steroid-course-length recommendations for nephrotic syndrome have evolved somewhat over recent years internationally (some evidence supports shorter courses than older regimens), but the core dosing structure and steroid-sensitive-vs-resistant distinction below remain clinically sound.

- Managed by an experienced paediatrician.
- High dose steroids (prednisolone): 60mg/m²/day (max 80mg) for 4 weeks, then 40mg/m² alternate days for 4 weeks, then taper. 80% are steroid sensitive; 80% will relapse at some point and require more steroids. Patients who struggle to wean are referred to as "steroid dependent". For steroid resistant disease, use ACE inhibitors and immunosuppressants (e.g. tacrolimus, ciclosporin).
- Low salt diet (high protein diet NOT recommended).
- Diuretics for oedema.
- Albumin infusion PRN.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis if severe.

> [!danger] Live vaccines are contraindicated in children receiving high dose prednisolone.

### Complications

- Hypovolaemia — fluid leaks from the intravascular space into the interstitial space → oedema and low BP.
- Thrombosis — kidneys leak clotting factors.
- Infection — kidney leaks immunoglobulins + use of steroids.
- Acute or chronic renal failure.
- Relapse: defined as ≥3+ proteinuria for 3 consecutive days.

### Minimal change disease

- **D:** the most common form of nephrotic syndrome in children, characterised by minimal histological changes in the kidney.
- **R:** age 1–8, Hodgkin's lymphoma, leukaemia, recent viral illness.
- **A:** 90% idiopathic.
- **S/Smx:** nephrotic syndrome; normotensive; highly selective proteinuria (e.g. albumin, transferrin).
- **Ix:** renal biopsy (normal glomeruli, fusion of podocytes, effacement of foot processes).
- **Mx:** as per nephrotic syndrome.
- **P:** 1/3 have frequent relapses, 1/3 have infrequent relapses.

## Acute glomerulonephritis

- **D:** glomerular injury usually involving inflammatory changes in the glomerular capillaries and the GBM.
- **A:** leukocyte infiltration, antibody deposition, complement activation. Causes: infections; systemic inflammatory conditions (e.g. SLE, RA, anti-GBM disease); drugs (e.g. lithium, PTU, NSAIDs); metabolic disorders (e.g. diabetes); malignancy; hereditary disorders (e.g. Fabry's disease, Alport syndrome); deposition diseases (e.g. amyloidosis).
- **P:** most nephritic syndromes are triggered by immune-mediated injury.

> [!info] Features: haematuria with red cell casts (macro or microscopic); proteinuria; oliguria; hypertension (~50%, more common in nephritic syndrome).

> [!note] A patient can have a "nephritic syndrome" (inflammation of the kidneys) resulting in "nephrotic syndrome" (the triad of proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, and peripheral oedema) — especially if glomerular inflammation damages the podocytes, causing severe proteinuria.

### Complicated presentations

- Hypertensive encephalopathy: restless, drowsy, severe headache, fits, ↓vision, vomiting, coma.
- AKI: acidosis, hyperkalaemia, seizure, stupor.
- Fluid overload: pulmonary oedema, cardiac failure.
- Mixed nephritic-nephrotic syndrome.

### Investigation and management

- **Dx/Ix:** urinalysis, bloods, throat swabs, CXR (if fluid overload suspected). Renal biopsy if severe.
- **Mx:** supportive. Treat underlying infection/cause. Monitor fluid balance — fluid restriction if oliguric, diuretics if overloaded. Check BP often + manage hypertension + low salt diet. If encephalopathy — give sodium nitroprusside. Haemodialysis if severe renal failure.

### Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis

- The most common cause of acute GN in children.
- Presents 7–21 days after strep infection (pharyngitis, impetigo).
- **S/Smx:** hypertension, haematuria ± oedema, malaise, anorexia, fever, abdominal pain.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical + confirm recent strep infection with ASO titre, anti-DNAse B, throat swab.
- **Mx:** supportive. PO penicillin for 10 days to reduce spread of nephritogenic bacterial strains. Oedema usually resolves in 5–10 days; hypertension, haematuria, proteinuria may last several weeks or months.
- **P:** very good; 95% recovery.

## Enuresis (urinary incontinence)

> [!note] Gap-filled from CSV ("Enuresis and Encopresis," Medium yield) — genuinely absent despite being an extremely common paediatric presentation, placed here given the topical/anatomical relevance to the rest of this file.

- **D:** involuntary urinary incontinence in a child old enough that continence would normally be expected (conventionally age ≥5 years) — **primary enuresis** (the child has never achieved a sustained period of dryness) is distinguished from **secondary enuresis** (a return of wetting after a prior dry period of ≥6 months), given this distinction meaningfully changes the differential.
- **A/P:** primary nocturnal enuresis is usually a developmental/maturational delay in bladder control (often with a family history), rather than an underlying pathological cause — genuinely the most common scenario and important to recognise as such, given inappropriately investigating every case as if it were pathological causes unnecessary anxiety and cost. Secondary enuresis is more likely to reflect an underlying trigger — UTI (see [[15_10_Paeds_-_UTI__Nephrotic_Syndrome__Glomerulonephritis]] Urinary tract infection (UTI) above, not repeated here), constipation (a genuinely common and under-recognised contributor, given a loaded rectum can mechanically reduce bladder capacity), diabetes (new-onset polyuria — see [[15_16b_Paeds_-_Diabetes_Mellitus__MODY__DKA]] Type 1 diabetes mellitus, not repeated here), or psychological/emotional stress (a new sibling, family disruption, bullying, or other significant life change).
- **S/Smx:** nocturnal (the most common pattern, "bedwetting") vs diurnal (daytime) wetting — diurnal enuresis is more likely to reflect an underlying bladder dysfunction or anatomical/neurological cause and generally warrants a lower threshold for further assessment than isolated nocturnal enuresis. Screen for associated symptoms: dysuria/frequency (UTI), straining/hard stool/soiling (constipation, see Encopresis below), polyuria/polydipsia (diabetes), and psychosocial stressors.
- **Ix:** primarily clinical — a careful history (including a bladder diary if diagnosis is unclear) and examination (including an abdominal exam to screen for a palpable loaded colon suggesting constipation) is usually sufficient; urinalysis to screen for UTI or glucosuria; further investigation (renal tract ultrasound, referral) reserved for atypical features (diurnal enuresis, secondary enuresis without an obvious trigger, associated red flags).
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** address any identified underlying cause first (treat UTI or constipation, optimise diabetes control) — treating enuresis as a standalone symptom without addressing a genuine underlying driver is unlikely to succeed.
  - **Definitive:** reassurance and general advice for uncomplicated primary nocturnal enuresis in younger children (fluid management — adequate daytime fluid intake with reduced intake before bed, regular voiding, avoiding punitive responses which are genuinely counterproductive and can worsen the child's distress without improving continence); an **enuresis alarm** (a moisture-sensing device that wakes the child at the first sign of wetting, training a conditioned response over weeks) is the most effective first-line intervention for children old enough to engage with it, given it addresses the underlying maturational delay directly rather than just suppressing symptoms; desmopressin (reduces nocturnal urine production) is a reasonable option for short-term control (e.g. sleepovers, camps) or where an alarm has failed/isn't appropriate, though it doesn't address the underlying maturational delay the way an alarm does.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** most primary nocturnal enuresis resolves with time/maturation regardless of intervention, and this natural history is itself an important part of reassuring the family; persistent enuresis despite the measures above, or the presence of red flag features, warrants referral to paediatric urology/nephrology.

## Encopresis (faecal incontinence)

- **D:** involuntary or voluntary passage of stool in an inappropriate location in a child old enough that continence would be expected — most commonly **overflow incontinence secondary to chronic constipation**, which is the single most important concept to understand about this presentation, given it changes both the framing of the problem for families and the treatment approach.
- **A/P:** chronic constipation leads to a large, hard faecal mass in the rectum; the rectum becomes chronically over-distended and loses normal sensation, and liquid stool from higher in the bowel leaks around the impacted mass — this **overflow** soiling is frequently mistaken by families (and sometimes clinicians) for diarrhoea or deliberate/behavioural soiling, when the actual underlying problem is severe constipation, not loose stool or defiance; correcting this misunderstanding is often itself a meaningful part of management, given how much shame and family conflict can build up around a presentation that's actually a straightforward (if severe) constipation problem.
- **R:** functional/dietary constipation is the overwhelming majority of cases; toilet-training difficulties or a history of a painful bowel movement leading to stool-withholding behaviour (a genuinely important vicious cycle — withholding worsens the constipation, which makes the next bowel motion even more painful); psychosocial stressors; rarely, an organic cause (e.g. Hirschsprung's disease — see [[15_08_Paeds_-_Surgical_Abdomen__Appendicitis__Intussusception__Pyloric_Stenosis__Hirschsprung__Oesophageal_Atresia_]] Hirschsprung disease, not repeated here — though this is typically identified earlier in infancy given the classic delayed meconium passage, making it a less common cause of encopresis presenting later in childhood specifically).
- **S/Smx:** soiling (often small amounts, sometimes mistaken for diarrhoea by families as above), a palpable abdominal faecal mass, infrequent bowel motions, pain on defecation, stool-withholding behaviour (typically posturing — stiffening, clenching buttocks — rather than actually attempting to pass stool), abdominal pain.
- **Ix:** primarily clinical (history and abdominal ± gentle rectal examination if needed and appropriate) — imaging is not routinely required for straightforward functional constipation with overflow soiling; screen for red flags (delayed meconium passage in the neonatal history, ribbon-like stools, growth faltering) that would raise concern for an organic cause such as Hirschsprung's disease.
- **Mx:**
  - **Immediate/acute:** disimpaction of the faecal mass first, given ongoing maintenance treatment cannot succeed while a large impacted mass remains — typically with an escalating osmotic laxative regimen (e.g. macrogol-based) per current local paediatric guidance.
  - **Definitive:** ongoing maintenance laxative therapy (typically continued for months, not weeks, given the rectum needs time to recover its normal calibre and sensation after chronic over-distension) alongside behavioural measures — a regular toileting routine (sitting on the toilet at consistent times, particularly after meals to use the gastrocolic reflex), a reward-based (not punitive) approach to engagement with the toileting routine, and adequate dietary fibre and fluid intake.
  - **Chronic/long-term:** family education is genuinely central to successful treatment — explicitly reframing the presentation as an overflow-from-constipation problem (not deliberate soiling or "diarrhoea") reduces family conflict and shame, which in turn improves the child's engagement with treatment; relapse is common if maintenance laxatives are stopped too early, so gradual, guided weaning rather than abrupt cessation is the standard approach.

## Phimosis

- **D:** non-retractable foreskin with associated scarring that will not resolve spontaneously.
- **A:** physiological in boys ≤~4 years old; by age 11, prevalence is <8%. Recurrent balanoposthitis, balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO) — foreskin looks pale, thickened, scarred, with repeated forced retraction.
- **P:** circumferential scarring.
- **S/Smx:** non-retractable foreskin — may present with ballooning during micturition. Retention of urine. Paraphimosis (engorgement). Obstruction.
- **Dx/Ix:** clinical.
- **Mx:**
  - Analgesia.

> [!danger] Paraphimosis is a medical emergency — manipulation with ice packs, compression, osmotic agent; puncture technique; surgical reduction + circumcision.

  - Phimosis: wait-and-see approach; topical corticosteroids (especially before age 8); stretching exercises BD for 15 days, then OD for 15 days.
  - General: personal hygiene.

## Cryptorchidism

- **D:** undescended testis — one or both testes are not present within the dependent portion of the scrotal sac by 3 months. Present in ≤5% of boys at birth, 1–2% at 3 months. May be associated with other defects.
- **A:** incomplete migration of the testis during embryogenesis, from the original retroperitoneal position near the kidneys to the final position in the scrotum.
- **Dx/Ix:** physical exam in the supine position.
- **S/Smx:** malpositioned or absent testis; palpable cryptorchid testis (unable to be pulled into the scrotum, or returns to a higher position after pulling); non-palpable testis; ± testicular asymmetry, scrotal hypoplasia.
- **DDx:** retractile testis (where the testis can be manipulated into the scrotum).
- **Mx:** orchidopexy at 6–18 months — inguinal exploration, mobilisation of the testis, and implantation into a dartos pouch. If presenting late (e.g. teenage years), orchidectomy may be preferred due to increased malignancy risk.

> [!info] Reasons for operating: ↓risk of infertility; allows testes to be examined for testicular cancer (undescended testis is 40× as likely to develop seminoma — the higher the testis is in the abdomen, the higher the risk); avoid testicular torsion; cosmesis.

## Hypospadias

- **D:** the urethra is abnormally located on the ventral surface of the penis. 1 in 350 male births. Associated with cryptorchidism (10%), inguinal hernia.
- **R:** family history (further male children risk 5–15%).
- **Features:** ventral (anterior) urethral meatus; hooded prepuce; chordee (ventral curvature of the penis) in more severe forms; urethral meatus may open more proximally in severe variants (25%).
- **Mx:** corrective surgery at ~12 months.

> [!danger] DO NOT circumcise before surgery, as the foreskin may be needed for the repair.

- In boys with very distal disease, no treatment may be needed.

## Horseshoe kidney

- **D:** fusion defect of the kidneys secondary to abnormal position, rotation, and vascular supply of the kidneys. 1 in 500. 90% U-shaped (lower pole). M>F 2:1. Possibly associated with chromosomal disorders.
- **Features:** usually asymptomatic, identified incidentally. May present with abdominal pain, UTI.
- **Dx/Ix:** US or CT/MRI. CT urogram may help identify stones, blockages, and ureteropelvic junction blocks.
- **DDx:** crossed fusion renal ectopia or fused pelvic kidney.
- **Mx:** symptomatic — regular corrective surgery not recommended.
- **↑risk of cancers** including transitional cell tumours (3–4×), Wilms tumour (2×), carcinoid cancer (62–82×).

## Wilms' tumour (nephroblastoma)

- **D:** the most common childhood renal malignancy; undifferentiated mesodermal tumour of the intermediate cell mass. Presents in children <5 years old.
- Associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome, WAGR syndrome, hemihypertrophy. 1/3 associated with loss of function mutation in the WT1 gene on chromosome 11.
- **Features:** abdominal mass (most common) — ballotable, does not move on inspiration. Painless haematuria. Flank pain. Others: anorexia, fever. Unilateral in 95% of cases. Metastases found in 20% of patients (most commonly lung).
- **Dx/Ix:** abdominal US, CT/MRI (staging, planning operation).
- **Mx:** urgent (48h) paediatric review for children with unexplained enlarged abdominal mass. Nephrectomy. Chemotherapy. Radiotherapy if advanced disease.
- **P:** good, with 80–90% cure rate. If associated with other genetic conditions, much poorer prognosis.

> [!info] Staging
> 1. Tumour confined to the kidney.
> 2. Extrarenal spread, but resectable.
> 3. Extensive abdominal disease.
> 4. Distant metastases.
> 5. Bilateral disease.

## Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS)

- **D:** microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. 90% in children, due to Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*.
- **R:** family history, prematurity (15–30%), low birth weight and/or SGA.
- **A:** unknown (sporadic); toxin induces damage to the endothelium of the glomerular capillary bed, causing thrombotic microangiopathy. In familial HUS, dysregulation in the complement cascade triggers atypical HUS.
- **P:** endothelial injury leads to microvascular thrombosis → MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI.
- **Dx/Ix:** FBC (anaemia — Hb <8, Coombs-negative), thrombocytopenia, fragmented blood film (schistocytes and helmet cells), U&Es (AKI), stool culture (looking for STEC infection, PCR for Shiga toxins).
- **Mx:** supportive (fluids, blood transfusion, dialysis).

> [!danger] Antibiotics NOT recommended. Plasma exchange only in severe cases not associated with diarrhoea.

> [!note] The adult/haematology entry is [[10_05_Haemonc_-_Normocytic_Anaemia_and_Sickle_Cell_Disease]] Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), which also lists the non-STEC causes (pneumococcal infection, HIV, SLE, drugs, cancer) relevant outside childhood.

> [!note] Congenital heart disease occurs in 0.8% of live births.

> [!info] Classification by shunt direction
> - **Left→Right shunts / Acyanotic:** VSD (30%), ASD, PDA, coarctation of the aorta, aortic valve stenosis.
> - **Right→Left shunts / Cyanotic:** Tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, tricuspid atresia, truncus arteriosus, total anomalous pulmonary venous drainage (TAPVD), hypoplastic left heart (HLH). *(See [[15_06_Paeds_-_Cyanotic_CHD__Kawasaki_Disease__Murmurs]] for detail on these, not repeated here.)*

## Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

- **D:** defects in the inter-ventricular septum that allow shunting of blood between the left and right ventricles.
- **R:** family history, Down syndrome (35% of Down syndrome patients will have VSDs).
- **P:** small defects may spontaneously close. Moderate to larger defects can lead to pulmonary hypertension due to ↑flow into the left ventricle and thus into the pulmonary circulation.
- **S/Smx:** most present in infancy. Pulmonary hypertension; Eisenmenger's syndrome; murmur — holosystolic at the left parasternal border ± palpable thrill.
- **Dx/Ix:** echo, CXR, ECG, cardiac MRI.
- **Mx:** watch and wait for small defects. Moderate to severe — surgical closure. HF symptoms must be treated before surgery.

> [!danger] If the patient presents with severe pulmonary hypertension & Eisenmenger's syndrome, closure of the VSD is contraindicated. Use pulmonary vasodilators (e.g. bosentan, sildenafil) for supportive treatment; consider heart-lung transplant.

## Atrial septal defect (ASD)

- Interatrial communications: incomplete separation between the left and right atrium.
- 5 types: secundum, ostium primum, sinus venosus, coronary sinus, and vestibular. Only secundum and vestibular defects are true ASDs.
- **R:** F>M (2:1), maternal alcoholism.
- **S/Smx:** the most common congenital heart defect to present in adulthood. Ejection systolic murmur, fixed splitting of S2. Embolism may pass from the venous system to the left side of the heart, causing a stroke.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| Ostium secundum (70% of ASDs) | Associated with Holt-Oram syndrome (tri-phalangeal thumbs); ECG shows RBBB with RAD |
| Ostium primum | Presents earlier than ostium secundum; associated with abnormal AV valves; ECG shows RBBB with LAD, prolonged PR interval |

- **Mx:** young patients with small ASDs — watch and wait, spontaneous closure possible. If larger, or evidence of right atrial enlargement, surgical closure is indicated.

## Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

- **D:** persistence of the ductus arteriosus after birth.
- **R:** prematurity (10× risk), maternal rubella (possibly 50%), F>M (2:1).
- **A:** in the fetus, patency of the ductus arteriosus is maintained by low O2 and circulating PGE2/PGI2. After birth, ↑O2 and ↓prostaglandins (due to removal of the placenta and ↑pulmonary blood flow) lead to closure. Possibly ↓O2 exposure and continued sensitivity to prostaglandins in preterm infants leads to PDA.
- **P:** shunting of blood from the aorta to the pulmonary artery through the PDA (L→R shunt, oxygenated blood goes into the pulmonary circulation). Can eventually result in late cyanosis in the lower extremities (differential cyanosis).
- **S/Smx:** usually present at 2–3 months. Left subclavicular thrill; continuous "machinery" murmur (crescendo-decrescendo); large volume, bounding, collapsing pulse; wide pulse pressure; heaving apex beat.
- **Mx:**
  - **In preterm infants:** indomethacin or ibuprofen (MOA: ↓prostaglandin synthesis, closes the connection in the majority). If associated with another heart defect, prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) is useful to keep the duct open until after surgical repair.
  - **In older children:** observe until age 1 with echo. Transcatheter or surgical closure.

## Coarctation of the aorta

- **D:** narrowing in the aorta, most commonly at the site of insertion of the ductus arteriosus, just distal to the left subclavian artery.
- **R:** M>F, Turner syndrome (50%), DiGeorge syndrome, HLHS, Shone's complex, PHACE syndrome.
- **P:** effects depend on severity of narrowing and resultant ↑afterload on the LV.
- **S/Smx:**
  - Infancy: heart failure.
  - Adult: hypertension — also associated with bicuspid aortic valve, berry aneurysms, and neurofibromatosis.
  - Radio-femoral delay; mid systolic murmur, maximal over the back; apical click from the aortic valve; notching of the inferior border of the ribs (due to collateral vessels — not seen in young children).
- **Mx:** if not severe, watch and wait. If critical + risk of HF, prostaglandin E is used to keep the ductus arteriosus open while waiting for surgery. Definitive treatment: surgery to correct coarctation and ligate the ductus arteriosus.

## Congenital aortic valve stenosis

- **D:** narrow aortic valve that restricts blood flow from the left ventricle into the aorta.
- **S/Smx:** mild stenosis may be asymptomatic. Severe: fatigue, SOB, dizziness, fainting — usually presents within months of birth. Ejection systolic murmur, loudest at the aortic area (crescendo-decrescendo), radiates to the carotids. Ejection click, palpable thrill, slow rising pulse, and narrow pulse pressure.
- **Dx/Ix:** echo, ECG, exercise testing.
- **Mx:** percutaneous balloon aortic valvuloplasty, surgical aortic valvotomy, valve replacement.

## Eisenmenger's syndrome

- **D:** shunt reversal (blood flowing from the RV to the LV) leading to distribution of de-oxygenated blood to the systemic arterial circulation.
- **A:** ASD, VSD, PDA.
- **P:** over time, pulmonary hypertension develops. ↑Pulmonary pressure > systemic pressure → reversal of shunt (R>L). This causes deoxygenated blood to bypass the lungs and enter the body → cyanosis.
- **S/Smx of pulmonary hypertension:** RV heave, loud P2, ↑JVP, peripheral oedema. Also cyanosis, clubbing, SOB, plethoric complexion (associated with polycythaemia).
- **Mx:** only definitive management is heart-lung transplant.

*(See [[15_20a_Paeds_-_Trisomies_and_Sex_Chromosome_Disorders]] Turner syndrome and [[15_21a_Paeds_-_Microdeletion_Syndromes__Cri_du_Chat__DiGeorge__Williams_]] DiGeorge syndrome for detail, not repeated here.)*

> [!info] Related syndromes
> - **Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS):** underdeveloped LV, mitral and aortic valves ± ASD.
> - **Shone's complex:** supravalvular mitral membrane, parachute mitral valve, subvalvular aortic stenosis, and coarctation of the aorta.
> - **PHACE syndrome:** posterior fossa malformations, haemangioma, arterial anomalies, coarctation of the aorta/cardiac defects, and eye abnormalities.

## Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF)

- **D:** congenital cardiac malformation with four classic findings:
  1. Pulmonary stenosis.
  2. RV hypertrophy (concentric).
  3. Over-riding aorta.
  4. VSD.
- **R:** rubella, ↑maternal age (>40), maternal alcoholism, GDM.
- **P:** degree of RV outflow tract obstruction determines severity. Significant pulmonary obstruction results in cyanosis — blood is shunted from the RV to the aorta through the VSD.

> [!danger] "Tet spells" — episodes of severe cyanosis associated with hyperpnoea, due to pulmonary obstruction → ↓pulmonary blood flow, ↑R-L shunting across the VSD.

- **S/Smx:** can be picked up on antenatal scans or newborn check. Tet spells (baby turns blue and may faint; triggered by crying, pooping, etc). Harsh ejection systolic murmur at the LLSE. R-sided aortic arch in 25%. CXR shows a "boot-shaped" heart; ECG shows RV hypertrophy.
- **Dx/Ix:** echo, Doppler, CXR (not often done in practice).

### Management of Tet spells

- Squatting/knees to chest — increases systemic vascular resistance, encouraging blood to enter pulmonary vessels.
- Supplemental O2.
- Beta-blockers — relax the RV.
- IV fluids — increase preload.
- Morphine — decreases respiratory drive, more effective breathing.
- Bicarbonate — buffers metabolic acidosis.
- Phenylephrine infusion — increases systemic vascular resistance.

### Definitive management

- Prostaglandin infusion to maintain the ductus arteriosus.
- Total surgical repair by open heart surgery — a 2-stage procedure at 6 months, with ~5% mortality.

## Transposition of the great arteries (TGA)

- **D:** attachments of the aorta and the pulmonary trunk to the heart are transposed. Incidence 4.7 per 10,000 live births.
- **R:** M>F, diabetic mothers.
- **A/P:** embryological discordance between the aorta and pulmonary trunk — aorta arises from the RV, pulmonary trunk arises from the LV → two parallel circuits, incompatible with life without a shunt.
- **S/Smx:** degree of symptoms depends on other defects (e.g. PDA, ASD, or VSD) that allow a shunt for oxygenation. May be picked up on antenatal ultrasound. Cyanosis, tachypnoea; loud single S2; prominent RV impulse; "egg-on-side" appearance on CXR.
- **Mx:**
  - Prostaglandin E infusion to maintain the ductus arteriosus.
  - Balloon septostomy — inserting a catheter into the foramen ovale via the umbilicus, inflating the balloon to create a large ASD, allowing for shunting.
  - Definitive: open heart surgery.

## Ebstein's anomaly

- **D:** low insertion of the tricuspid valve resulting in a large atrium and small ventricle. Sometimes referred to as "atrialisation" of the RV.
- **R:** maternal use of lithium.
- Associated with a patent foramen ovale or ASD (80%), resulting in a shunt, and WPW syndrome.
- **S/Smx:** often present after a few days of birth, after the ductus arteriosus closes. Evidence of HF (e.g. oedema); gallop rhythm on auscultation (S3 and S4); cyanosis; SOB and tachypnoea; poor feeding; collapse or cardiac arrest.
- **Dx/Ix:** echo.
- **Mx:** medical management of arrhythmias and HF; possibly prophylactic antibiotics to prevent infective endocarditis; definitive management by surgical correction.

## Murmurs and heart sounds in children

- Innocent murmurs occur in ~80% of children at some time (e.g. fever, anxiety, etc).
- A flow murmur is the most common.
- A venous hum is heard above or below the clavicles, continuous, low pitched — abolished by pressing the ipsilateral jugular or lying down.
- Still's murmur — heard at the LLSE to apex, early systolic, vibratory, loudest lying flat; the child is well.

> [!danger] Red flags: signs of HF; faltering growth; chest pain; syncope; unexplained fever; cyanosis; clubbing; diastolic murmur; thrills (grade ≥IV); heave.

> [!warning] Increased suspicion warranted with a family history of congenital heart disease.

## Kawasaki disease

- **D:** systemic vasculitis that occurs almost exclusively in childhood.
- **R:** Asian ancestry (especially Japanese), age 3 months to 4 years.
- **A:** possible infection in a susceptible host. **P:** arterial vasculitis and remodelling.

> [!info] Diagnostic criteria — Classic Kawasaki disease must have 5 days of fever refractory to antibiotic therapy (if given) AND ≥4 of the following:
> - Bilateral conjunctivitis.
> - Polymorphous rash.
> - ≥1 mucous membrane change — red and cracked lips; sore throat/injected pharynx; strawberry tongue.
> - ≥1 extremity change — erythema and desquamation of palms/soles (redness then peeling of skin); periungual desquamation of fingers and toes.
> - Cervical lymphadenopathy.
>
> Atypical Kawasaki disease may present with fewer of these symptoms.

- **Ix:** clinical diagnosis. Following diagnosis, patients will need echos to look for coronary artery aneurysms.
- **Mx:**
  - High-dose aspirin — 80–100mg/kg/day PO given in 4 divided doses for 24–72h after fever stops (up to 14 days), then 3–5mg/kg PO OD for 6–8 weeks.
  - **IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin)** — high-dose, acting as an immunomodulator rather than as passive protection, and given to reduce the risk of coronary artery aneurysms rather than to treat an infection (see [[08_01-03_Infectious_Disease_-_Bacterial_Infections]] Passive Immunisation — Immunoglobulin After an Exposure for the distinction).
  - Echo — if coronary artery aneurysms are spotted, refer to paediatric cardiology.

> [!danger] Complications: coronary artery aneurysms. Highest risk in those who do not receive IVIG within 10 days of fever onset, and patients who have persistent fever and inflammation despite treatment.
