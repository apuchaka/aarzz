## 0.1 Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms

> [!tip] Classify into three groups — it directs the whole assessment
> **· STORAGE symptoms — FREQUENCY, URGENCY, NOCTURIA, urge incontinence.** *(The bladder cannot hold.)*
> **· VOIDING symptoms — HESITANCY, poor or intermittent stream, straining, terminal dribbling.** *(The bladder cannot empty.)*
> **· POST-MICTURITION symptoms — post-void dribbling, sensation of incomplete emptying.**
> **The clinically important point: STORAGE symptoms are usually the MORE BOTHERSOME to patients, and they correlate POORLY with prostate size** — so a man with severe urgency and frequency may have a small prostate, and treating the prostate may not fix him.

> [!warning] "LUTS" replaced "prostatism" for a reason
> **Symptoms correlate poorly with prostate size, with flow rates, and with obstruction.** A large prostate may be asymptomatic; severe symptoms may occur with a small one.
> **And women get lower urinary tract symptoms too** — the older term implied a male, prostatic cause that frequently is not there.
> **Male LUTS differential: benign prostatic hyperplasia · prostate cancer · URETHRAL STRICTURE · bladder stone · bladder cancer · overactive bladder · neurogenic bladder · urinary tract infection and prostatitis · POLYURIA (diabetes mellitus or insipidus, hypercalcaemia, diuretics) · medications · constipation · and obstructive sleep apnoea.**

> [!danger] Nocturia — think beyond the prostate
> **Nocturia has three mechanisms, and the bladder diary distinguishes them:**
> **1. NOCTURNAL POLYURIA — producing too much urine at night.** Causes: **HEART FAILURE and peripheral oedema (fluid redistributes when supine) · OBSTRUCTIVE SLEEP APNOEA (which increases natriuretic peptide release) · diuretics taken late in the day · excessive evening fluid or alcohol · and chronic kidney disease.**
> **2. REDUCED BLADDER CAPACITY — from obstruction, overactivity, or a small fibrotic bladder.**
> **3. GLOBAL POLYURIA — diabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus, hypercalcaemia, primary polydipsia.**
> **4. A SLEEP disorder, where the person wakes for another reason and then voids.**
> **Treating nocturia as prostatic when the cause is heart failure or sleep apnoea achieves nothing** — and both of those are more consequential than the nocturia.

> [!tip] The assessment
> **· A BLADDER DIARY is the single most useful investigation** — recording times, volumes voided, fluid intake and incontinence episodes over several days. **It distinguishes polyuria from frequency, reveals nocturnal polyuria, quantifies functional bladder capacity, and shows the fluid intake pattern**, none of which the history reliably provides.
> **· Symptom score — the IPSS** quantifies severity and bother and tracks response. `UNVERIFIED — the score and its bands.`
> **· Examination: abdomen for a palpable or percussible bladder · DIGITAL RECTAL EXAMINATION for size, consistency, symmetry and nodules · external genitalia for phimosis and meatal stenosis · and a NEUROLOGICAL examination including perineal sensation, anal tone and lower limbs** where a neurogenic cause is possible.
> **· Red flags: HAEMATURIA (cross-refer [[H1]] 0.1) · pain · recurrent urinary infection · a palpable bladder · renal impairment · neurological signs · a hard or irregular prostate · bone pain or weight loss.**

## 0.2 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia

**A/P:** Stromal and epithelial hyperplasia in the **transition zone** of the prostate produces obstruction through **two components**:
**· A STATIC component — the physical mass of tissue.**
**· A DYNAMIC component — smooth muscle tone in the prostate and bladder neck, mediated by ALPHA-1 ADRENERGIC receptors.**
**This two-component model explains the two drug classes exactly: alpha-blockers relax the dynamic component (working within days), and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors shrink the static component (working over months).**
Chronic obstruction then causes detrusor hypertrophy and instability — which is why **storage symptoms often persist after the obstruction is relieved** — and eventually detrusor decompensation.

> [!tip] Conservative measures first, and review the drug chart
> **· Fluid management — reduce evening fluid, caffeine and alcohol; avoid large boluses.**
> **· DOUBLE VOIDING and timed voiding.**
> **· TREAT CONSTIPATION — a loaded rectum genuinely worsens voiding and can precipitate retention.**
> **· REVIEW MEDICATIONS THAT PRECIPITATE RETENTION: ANTICHOLINERGICS (including many antihistamines, tricyclics, oxybutynin, antipsychotics) · OPIOIDS · SYMPATHOMIMETIC DECONGESTANTS (pseudoephedrine, in over-the-counter cold preparations) · and alcohol.**
> **A man in acute retention has frequently just been given an antihistamine, a decongestant or an opioid**, and the drug is the treatable factor.

> [!warning] Alpha-blockers — and the one you must tell the ophthalmologist about
> **Rapid symptomatic benefit, within days.**
> **Adverse effects: postural hypotension and dizziness (particularly with the first dose) · retrograde ejaculation · nasal congestion.**
> **INTRAOPERATIVE FLOPPY IRIS SYNDROME — tamsulosin and other alpha-blockers cause the iris to billow and prolapse during CATARACT SURGERY, with a substantially increased risk of surgical complications.**
> **The effect persists long after the drug is stopped, so stopping it pre-operatively does not solve the problem — what matters is that the OPHTHALMOLOGIST KNOWS IN ADVANCE, so they can modify their technique.**
> **Ask every man about alpha-blocker use before cataract surgery, and document it in the referral.** This is a small, specific, high-value piece of knowledge.

> [!danger] 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors halve the PSA — you must account for it
> **Finasteride and dutasteride reduce prostate volume, take MONTHS to produce symptomatic benefit, and reduce the risk of acute retention and the need for surgery.**
> **THEY REDUCE MEASURED PSA BY APPROXIMATELY HALF.**
> **So a PSA measured in a man on one of these drugs must be DOUBLED to be interpreted against normal ranges — and a PSA that RISES on treatment is concerning regardless of the absolute value.**
> **Failing to account for this delays prostate cancer diagnoses.** `UNVERIFIED — the correction factor and its timing.`
> Other effects: reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, ejaculatory disturbance, gynaecomastia.

> [!tip] The other options and the surgical complications worth knowing
> **· Combination therapy** for larger prostates with significant symptoms.
> **· ANTIMUSCARINICS or MIRABEGRON for persistent storage symptoms** — **safe in men with BPH provided there is no significant post-void residual, so CHECK THE RESIDUAL FIRST.** Mirabegron avoids anticholinergic burden. Cross-refer [[GER1]] 0.4.
> **· Tadalafil** — useful where erectile dysfunction coexists.
> **· SURGERY — transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) and its alternatives (laser enucleation, water vapour therapy, prostatic urethral lift).**
> **TURP complications to be able to counsel on: bleeding · RETROGRADE EJACULATION, which is common and should be explicitly mentioned because it matters to patients and to fertility · urethral stricture · incontinence · erectile dysfunction · and TUR SYNDROME — dilutional HYPONATRAEMIA from absorption of irrigation fluid, presenting with confusion, visual disturbance, bradycardia and seizures.** **TUR syndrome is much less common with modern bipolar and saline systems**, but it remains examinable and is a cause of post-operative confusion.

## 0.3 Acute Urinary Retention

`CF-PAIR` **Three accounts of acute urinary retention sit in this file and none declared the others before 2026-09-02:** `07 §0.12 Urinary Retention` · `H2 §0.3 Acute Urinary Retention` · `NEW_Renal_and_Urology ## Acute Urinary Retention`. **The safety rule is in ONE of them:** `H2 §0.3` carries *"DO NOT CLAMP THE CATHETER intermittently to decompress slowly"* and the discharge-without-monitoring error; the other two mention post-obstructive diuresis without it. **All kept, none reconciled.**

> [!tip] The presentation and the precipitants
> **PAINFUL inability to void, with a palpable and percussible suprapubic bladder** — and the pain is what distinguishes it from chronic retention.
> **It is usually BPH PLUS A TRIGGER, and the trigger is often the treatable part:**
> **· CONSTIPATION · urinary tract infection · MEDICATIONS (anticholinergics, opioids, decongestants) · alcohol · the POST-OPERATIVE state (anaesthesia, opioids, immobility, pain) · immobility · and prolonged fluid restriction followed by a large intake.**
> **Other causes: clot retention (from haematuria) · urethral STRICTURE · phimosis or paraphimosis · prostate cancer · pelvic mass · and NEUROLOGICAL causes.**

> [!danger] Urinary retention can be cauda equina syndrome
> **Painless retention with overflow, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral leg symptoms, back pain or reduced anal tone is CAUDA EQUINA SYNDROME until proven otherwise — and it requires an urgent MRI, not just a catheter.**
> **The catheter relieves the bladder and removes the most obvious sign while the cord compression progresses.** Cross-refer [[L4]] 0.2.
> **In WOMEN, acute retention is uncommon and warrants more thought: consider NEUROLOGICAL causes (cauda equina, multiple sclerosis), pelvic mass, prolapse, post-partum retention, medications, herpes zoster affecting sacral roots, and Fowler syndrome in young women.**

> [!warning] Management — and two practice points
> **· CATHETERISE promptly, and DOCUMENT THE RESIDUAL VOLUME drained.** The volume determines the subsequent management and is the piece of information most often not recorded.
> **· DO NOT CLAMP THE CATHETER intermittently to "decompress slowly".** This was traditional practice to prevent decompression haematuria, and it is **no longer recommended** — the bladder is drained freely. Decompression haematuria is usually self-limiting.
> **· Send urine, treat infection, relieve constipation, review and stop the precipitating drugs.**
> **· TRIAL OF VOID (TWOC) after a period of catheterisation, usually with an ALPHA-BLOCKER started beforehand — which improves the chance of success.** **Failure of a trial of void warrants urology referral.**
> `UNVERIFIED — the duration of catheterisation before a trial, and the alpha-blocker regimen.`

> [!danger] Post-obstructive diuresis
> **After relieving a large-volume or chronic retention, a marked diuresis can follow** — from accumulated solute, impaired concentrating ability in the recovering tubules, and retained fluid.
> **It can cause significant HYPOVOLAEMIA and ELECTROLYTE DISTURBANCE, and is under-recognised.**
> **After draining a large residual: monitor URINE OUTPUT hourly initially, check UEC, monitor weight and postural blood pressure, and REPLACE fluid where the losses are significant.**
> **The error is discharging a patient who has just had a very large volume drained without any monitoring.** `UNVERIFIED — the residual volume and output thresholds that define significant post-obstructive diuresis.`

## 0.4 Chronic Retention and Obstructive Uropathy

> [!danger] High-pressure chronic retention is painless and destroys kidneys quietly
> **· LOW-PRESSURE chronic retention — a large residual with normal upper tracts and normal renal function.**
> **· HIGH-PRESSURE chronic retention — a large residual with raised bladder pressure transmitted upward, causing BILATERAL HYDRONEPHROSIS and RENAL IMPAIRMENT.**
> **The classic missed presentation: a man with PAINLESS overflow incontinence and NOCTURNAL ENURESIS (wetting the bed as an adult), a grossly distended but non-tender abdomen, and an unexplained raised creatinine.**
> **Bedwetting in an adult man is high-pressure chronic retention until proven otherwise**, and it is repeatedly attributed to incontinence, ageing or dementia while the kidneys fail.
> **The abdominal distension is often mistaken for ascites or obesity** — **percuss it, and do a bladder scan.**
> **These patients require catheterisation and careful monitoring for post-obstructive diuresis, which can be substantial.**

> [!warning] Obstructive uropathy — where the obstruction is determines what relieves it
> **Post-renal acute kidney injury requires obstruction of BOTH kidneys, or of a single functioning kidney, or of the bladder outlet.**
> **· BLADDER OUTLET obstruction → relieved by a urethral or suprapubic CATHETER.**
> **· URETERIC obstruction → requires NEPHROSTOMY or a ureteric STENT.** A catheter achieves nothing.
> **Causes of upper tract obstruction: stones · pelvic or retroperitoneal malignancy · retroperitoneal fibrosis · surgical injury · and prostate cancer with ureteric involvement.**
> Cross-refer [[H3]].

> [!danger] An obstructed, infected urinary system is a urological emergency
> **Obstruction plus infection produces pyonephrosis and rapidly progressive sepsis, and ANTIBIOTICS ALONE WILL NOT TREAT IT.**
> **It requires URGENT DECOMPRESSION — nephrostomy or stent — alongside antibiotics and resuscitation.**
> **A septic patient with hydronephrosis needs an urgent urology or interventional radiology conversation the same hour, not a trial of antibiotics overnight.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.10 and [[H4]] 0.4.

## 0.5 Catheters

> [!danger] The indications are narrow, and "incontinence" is not one of them
> **Legitimate indications: acute or chronic urinary retention · accurate output measurement in critical illness · specific perioperative and urological situations · to facilitate healing of a sacral or perineal pressure injury · and for comfort at the very end of life.**
> **NOT indications: incontinence · staff or nursing convenience · reducing the need to assist a patient to the toilet · or immobility alone.**
> **Every catheter should have a DOCUMENTED INDICATION and a PLANNED REMOVAL DATE**, and both should be reviewed daily. **Catheters that nobody planned to remove are a recurring source of harm.** Cross-refer [[GER2]] 0.1.

> [!warning] The complications
> **· CATHETER-ASSOCIATED URINARY TRACT INFECTION — the risk increases with EVERY DAY the catheter remains, and it is among the commonest healthcare-associated infections.**
> **· DO NOT TREAT ASYMPTOMATIC BACTERIURIA in a catheterised patient.** **Every long-term catheter is colonised. Treating a positive culture in an asymptomatic patient selects resistance and achieves nothing** — and it is a major driver of inappropriate antibiotic use. Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.10.
> **· Urethral trauma, false passage and STRICTURE — particularly from forced or repeated attempts.**
> **· Bladder spasm and bypassing (leakage around the catheter), which is usually caused by spasm or blockage rather than by too small a catheter.**
> **· Blockage, encrustation and stone formation.**
> **· It is a TETHER — preventing mobilisation, contributing to deconditioning and delirium — and it affects dignity and body image, which patients feel more than clinicians assume.**
> **· INTERMITTENT SELF-CATHETERISATION has a LOWER infection rate than an indwelling catheter and preserves independence, and it should be considered wherever the patient can manage it.**

> [!danger] Two catheter emergencies
> **1. AUTONOMIC DYSREFLEXIA in spinal cord injury above about T6.** **A blocked catheter — or any noxious stimulus below the level of the lesion, including constipation — triggers an unopposed sympathetic surge, producing SEVERE HYPERTENSION, pounding headache, flushing and sweating ABOVE the lesion with pallor below, and reflex bradycardia.**
> **It can cause seizures, intracranial haemorrhage and death.**
> **Management: SIT THE PATIENT UP · REMOVE THE TRIGGER — unblock or replace the catheter, relieve the constipation, loosen tight clothing · and treat the hypertension.** **Finding and relieving the cause is the treatment, and the catheter is the commonest culprit.**
> **2. PARAPHIMOSIS.** **The foreskin, retracted for catheterisation, is not replaced — and swells behind the glans, becoming irreversibly trapped and causing venous congestion, oedema and ultimately ischaemia.**
> **ALWAYS REPLACE THE FORESKIN AFTER CATHETERISING AN UNCIRCUMCISED MAN**, and check it afterwards. **This is a wholly preventable, wholly iatrogenic emergency.** Cross-refer [[H4]] 0.3.

> [!tip] Difficult catheterisation
> **Do not force.** Repeated forceful attempts cause false passages, bleeding and strictures that turn a simple problem into a complex one.
> **Escalate: a larger or firmer catheter (counterintuitively, a slightly larger catheter often passes more easily past a prostate than a floppy small one) · a coudé-tipped catheter · adequate lubrication and local anaesthetic gel with time to work · senior or urology assistance · and SUPRAPUBIC catheterisation as the alternative.**
> **Suprapubic catheterisation requires a distended, palpable or ultrasound-confirmed bladder, and is contraindicated where there is uncertainty or previous lower abdominal surgery** — bowel injury is the risk.

## 0.6 Incontinence

> [!tip] The types and the branch point
> The full classification is in **[[GER2]] 0.1** and the female-specific management in **[[O5]] 0.6.** The essentials:
> **· STRESS — leakage on cough, laugh, exertion. Pelvic floor weakness in women; post-prostatectomy in men.**
> **· URGE — sudden urgency with leakage; detrusor overactivity.**
> **· MIXED.**
> **· OVERFLOW — continuous dribbling from chronic retention.**
> **· FUNCTIONAL — the bladder works but the person cannot reach the toilet in time, for reasons of mobility, cognition, environment or staffing.**
> **THE BRANCH POINT IS THE BLADDER SCAN.** **Overflow incontinence treated with an antimuscarinic gets worse — and the two look similar on history.** **Scan the bladder before prescribing.**

> [!warning] Male incontinence
> **· POST-PROSTATECTOMY stress incontinence — common after radical prostatectomy, usually improves over months, and responds to SUPERVISED pelvic floor muscle training.** **Persistent severe cases may need an artificial urinary sphincter or sling.** **Men are rarely offered pelvic floor physiotherapy, and they should be.**
> **· Overflow from bladder outlet obstruction — treat the obstruction, not the bladder.**
> **· Urge incontinence and overactive bladder — bladder training first, then pharmacotherapy with attention to anticholinergic burden and to post-void residual.**
> **· Post-micturition dribbling — very common, benign, and helped by urethral milking after voiding, which is a simple piece of advice men are almost never given.**

> [!danger] Ask about it — incontinence is massively under-reported
> **People do not volunteer incontinence.** They manage it privately with pads, fluid restriction (which worsens it) and social withdrawal, often for years, and frequently believe it is an inevitable consequence of age, childbirth or surgery.
> **It is a leading contributor to social isolation, falls (rushing to the toilet), skin breakdown, carer strain and residential placement — and most of it is treatable.**
> **"Do you ever leak urine, or have trouble getting to the toilet in time?" asked routinely finds a problem you can fix.**
> **Then: bladder diary · post-void residual · urinalysis · medication review · and referral to a CONTINENCE NURSE ADVISOR and pelvic floor physiotherapist**, both of which are effective and under-used. **Funded continence aid schemes exist in Australia.** `UNVERIFIED — current schemes and eligibility.`

**Ix:** **BLADDER DIARY over several days** (*why:* the most informative single investigation — it distinguishes polyuria from frequency, reveals nocturnal polyuria, and quantifies capacity; *what:* frequency-volume pattern). **POST-VOID RESIDUAL by bladder scan** (*why:* distinguishes retention and overflow from other causes, takes two minutes, and prevents the antimuscarinic error; *what:* residual volume). **Urinalysis and culture** (*why:* infection, and haematuria requiring separate investigation; *what:* infection, blood). **UEC and eGFR** (*why:* obstructive nephropathy, which may be silent in high-pressure chronic retention; *what:* renal impairment). **Renal tract ultrasound** where residual is high or renal function impaired (*why:* hydronephrosis and bladder wall changes; *what:* upper tract dilatation). **PSA — with informed discussion first, and DOUBLED if the patient is on a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor** (*why:* prostate cancer, with the screening caveats; *what:* level in context — cross-refer [[GER3]]). Glucose or HbA1c and calcium (*why:* polyuria from diabetes or hypercalcaemia; *what:* the metabolic cause). Uroflowmetry and urodynamics in selected cases (*why:* characterises obstruction versus detrusor failure before surgery; *what:* flow rate, pressure-flow relationship).

## 0.1 The Acute Scrotum

`CF-PAIR` **Three accounts of the acute scrotum sit in this file and none declared the others before 2026-09-02:** `07 §0.20 Testicular Torsion` · `H4 §0.1 The Acute Scrotum` · `NEW_Renal_and_Urology ## Acute Scrotal Pain`. **The safety content is split:** `H4 §0.1` alone carries *"A NORMAL DOPPLER ULTRASOUND DOES NOT EXCLUDE TORSION"*, and `NEW_Renal_and_Urology` alone carries the **~6-hour salvage window**. All three say imaging must not delay theatre. **All kept, none reconciled.**

> [!danger] Testicular torsion until proven otherwise — and imaging must not delay theatre
> **Testicular salvage falls steeply with time from onset.** **The window is measured in hours, and it is the single most time-critical urological diagnosis.**
> **A NORMAL DOPPLER ULTRASOUND DOES NOT EXCLUDE TORSION.** **Flow may be preserved in partial or intermittent torsion, in early torsion, and where the study is technically difficult — and a reassuring scan has repeatedly delayed exploration until the testis was lost.**
> **If the clinical suspicion is torsion, the patient goes to THEATRE. Imaging is for cases where the diagnosis is genuinely doubtful and it does not delay surgery.**
> **Never let "the ultrasound showed flow" override a convincing history and examination.**

> [!tip] The features of torsion
> **· SUDDEN, SEVERE unilateral pain — frequently waking the patient from sleep, or after activity or minor trauma.**
> **· NAUSEA AND VOMITING — a useful feature, and unusual in epididymo-orchitis.**
> **· A HIGH-RIDING testis with a HORIZONTAL lie.**
> **· ABSENT CREMASTERIC REFLEX on the affected side — one of the more useful signs.**
> **· NEGATIVE PREHN SIGN — elevating the testis does NOT relieve the pain (in epididymo-orchitis it does).**
> **· Age: bimodal — NEONATES and PERIPUBERTAL boys — but it occurs at ANY age, and an adult presentation is not a reason to dismiss it.**
> **The anatomical basis is the BELL-CLAPPER DEFORMITY — a high investment of tunica vaginalis allowing the testis to rotate freely — and it is usually BILATERAL, which is why the contralateral testis is fixed at the same operation.**

> [!warning] Intermittent torsion, and the appendix testis
> **INTERMITTENT TORSION — recurrent episodes of sudden severe pain that resolve spontaneously over minutes to hours.** **The history is the diagnosis, the examination between episodes is normal, and it warrants ELECTIVE bilateral fixation** — because the next episode may not resolve.
> **TORSION OF THE APPENDIX TESTIS — a small embryological remnant.** Typically in younger boys, with **less severe, more gradual pain, a normal testicular lie and cremasteric reflex, LOCALISED tenderness at the UPPER POLE, and sometimes a visible "BLUE DOT" sign through the scrotal skin.** **Self-limiting and managed with analgesia** — but it should only be diagnosed where torsion has been genuinely excluded.

> [!tip] Epididymo-orchitis — and the age determines the organism
> **Gradual onset over hours to days, fever, dysuria or urethral discharge, a tender swollen epididymis, PRESENT cremasteric reflex, and a POSITIVE PREHN sign (relief on elevation).**
> **· UNDER about 35 — sexually transmitted: CHLAMYDIA and GONORRHOEA.** **Take a sexual history, test appropriately, treat for STI, and arrange PARTNER NOTIFICATION.** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.2.
> **· OVER about 35 — enteric organisms, associated with urinary tract infection, bladder outlet obstruction or instrumentation.**
> **MUMPS ORCHITIS — usually after parotitis, often bilateral, and a reason to check immunisation status.**
> **The rule that matters: NEVER diagnose epididymo-orchitis in an adolescent or young man without genuinely excluding torsion.** The presentations overlap, and torsion is the one that costs a testis.

> [!danger] The other causes of an acute scrotum
> **· STRANGULATED INGUINOSCROTAL HERNIA — cross-refer 0.5.**
> **· FOURNIER GANGRENE — necrotising infection of the perineum and scrotum, with pain out of proportion, systemic toxicity, and crepitus. A surgical emergency, and higher risk in diabetics and the immunosuppressed.** Cross-refer [[K2]] 0.2.
> **· Testicular TUMOUR with haemorrhage or infarction.**
> **· Trauma** — see 0.6.
> **· IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) — scrotal involvement occurs in children and can mimic torsion.** Cross-refer [[L2]] 0.5.
> **· Idiopathic scrotal oedema in children** — striking erythema and swelling with surprisingly little tenderness, and a normal testis.

**Ix:** **Urgent surgical assessment — the examination is the investigation** (*why:* torsion is a clinical diagnosis and salvage depends on time to theatre; *what:* lie, cremasteric reflex, Prehn sign). **Doppler ultrasound ONLY where the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain and it will not delay surgery** (*why:* it has meaningful false negatives and false reassurance is the mechanism of testicular loss; *what:* flow, echotexture). Urinalysis and urine culture (*why:* supports epididymo-orchitis; *what:* pyuria, organism). **Chlamydia and gonorrhoea NAAT (first-void urine) in younger men** (*why:* determines treatment and triggers partner notification; *what:* organism). FBC and CRP (*why:* systemic infection; *what:* inflammatory response).

## 0.2 Scrotal Lumps

> [!tip] Four questions sort almost every scrotal lump
> **1. CAN YOU GET ABOVE IT?** **No → it is inguinoscrotal, i.e. a HERNIA (or extends from the abdomen).**
> **2. IS IT SEPARATE FROM THE TESTIS, or arising from it?** **Arising from the testis is the concerning answer.**
> **3. DOES IT TRANSILLUMINATE?** **Yes → fluid: hydrocele or epididymal cyst.**
> **4. IS IT TENDER?**
> **· Separate, cystic, transilluminating, surrounding the testis → HYDROCELE.**
> **· Separate, cystic, ABOVE and BEHIND the testis → EPIDIDYMAL CYST or spermatocele.**
> **· "Bag of worms", above the testis, DISAPPEARS ON LYING DOWN → VARICOCELE.**
> **· ARISING FROM THE TESTIS, hard, usually painless → TESTICULAR TUMOUR until proven otherwise.**

> [!danger] Two lumps that require a second thought
> **1. A HYDROCELE CAN CONCEAL A TUMOUR.** **A new hydrocele in a young man, or any hydrocele where the testis cannot be palpated confidently, requires an ULTRASOUND.** Reassuring a young man about a hydrocele without imaging the testis is how tumours are missed.
> **2. VARICOCELES ARE PREDOMINANTLY LEFT-SIDED** — because the left testicular vein drains into the left renal vein at a right angle, whereas the right drains obliquely into the inferior vena cava.
> **Therefore: a RIGHT-SIDED varicocele, a varicocele that does NOT decompress on lying down, or a NEW varicocele in an older man raises obstruction of the testicular vein — most importantly by a RENAL CELL CARCINOMA invading the renal vein.** **These warrant abdominal imaging.**

> [!danger] Testicular cancer — young men, and three things not to get wrong
> **The commonest solid malignancy in men aged roughly 15 to 40, and among the most curable — which makes early diagnosis genuinely consequential.**
> **Presentation: a PAINLESS, HARD lump arising from the testis** — though a substantial minority have pain, which is why pain does not exclude it. Also: a dragging sensation, a new hydrocele, gynaecomastia (from hCG), or symptoms of metastatic disease (back pain from retroperitoneal nodes, respiratory symptoms).
> **Risk factors: CRYPTORCHIDISM — including a testis that was surgically brought down, since orchidopexy reduces but does not eliminate the risk and also makes the testis examinable · a previous contralateral tumour · family history · infertility and testicular atrophy.**
> **THE THREE THINGS:**
> **1. TUMOUR MARKERS — AFP, beta-hCG and LDH, taken BEFORE orchidectomy.** **A PURE SEMINOMA does NOT raise AFP — a raised AFP means non-seminomatous elements are present regardless of what the histology says.** This distinction determines treatment.
> **2. DO NOT BIOPSY THROUGH THE SCROTUM.** **The scrotum and the testis have different lymphatic drainage — scrotal violation risks seeding into inguinal nodes and alters the staging and management.** **The operation is a RADICAL INGUINAL ORCHIDECTOMY.**
> **3. OFFER SPERM BANKING BEFORE TREATMENT.** **Chemotherapy and radiotherapy impair fertility, the window is before treatment starts, and it is a well-documented omission in young men who are focused on survival at the point of diagnosis.**

**Ix:** **Scrotal ULTRASOUND** (*why:* the primary imaging test — characterises the lesion, distinguishes intratesticular from extratesticular, and intratesticular solid lesions are malignant until proven otherwise; *what:* location, echotexture, vascularity). **Tumour markers — AFP, beta-hCG, LDH — BEFORE orchidectomy** (*why:* they inform diagnosis, staging, prognosis and monitoring, and the pre-operative value cannot be recovered afterwards; *what:* elevation and pattern). CT chest, abdomen and pelvis where malignancy is confirmed (*why:* staging, particularly retroperitoneal nodes; *what:* nodal and distant disease). **Abdominal imaging for a right-sided or non-decompressing varicocele** (*why:* renal tumour obstructing the testicular vein; *what:* renal mass).

## 0.3 Penile and Foreskin Problems

> [!danger] Paraphimosis — and it is usually iatrogenic
> **The retracted foreskin becomes trapped behind the glans, causing venous congestion, progressive oedema, and eventually arterial compromise and necrosis.**
> **The commonest cause is a healthcare worker retracting the foreskin to catheterise or clean, and NOT REPLACING IT.**
> **ALWAYS REPLACE THE FORESKIN AFTER CATHETERISATION OR EXAMINATION, and check it afterwards.** Cross-refer [[H2]] 0.5.
> **Treatment: analgesia, then sustained MANUAL COMPRESSION of the oedematous foreskin and glans to reduce the swelling, followed by reduction.** Adjuncts (osmotic agents, puncture techniques, dorsal slit) where that fails. **Urgent urology if it cannot be reduced.**

> [!danger] Priapism — the distinction determines the urgency
> **· ISCHAEMIC (low-flow) priapism — PAINFUL, rigid, with a soft glans. This is a COMPARTMENT SYNDROME of the corpora and a TRUE EMERGENCY.** **Beyond a few hours it causes progressive smooth muscle necrosis and fibrosis, with permanent erectile dysfunction.**
> **Causes: SICKLE CELL DISEASE (an important cause, particularly in children and adolescents — cross-refer [[J2]]) · haematological malignancy · INTRACAVERNOSAL agents and PDE5 inhibitors · drugs including trazodone, antipsychotics, and COCAINE · and idiopathic.**
> **Management: urgent urology, ASPIRATION of the corpora with or without intracavernosal phenylephrine, and treatment of the underlying cause.** `UNVERIFIED — the time thresholds and the phenylephrine regimen.`
> **· NON-ISCHAEMIC (high-flow) priapism — PAINLESS, not fully rigid, following perineal or penile TRAUMA with an arterial-cavernosal fistula.** **NOT an emergency, and often managed conservatively or with embolisation.**
> **A painful rigid erection is the emergency; a painless one after trauma is not.**

> [!tip] The foreskin and the rest
> **· PHYSIOLOGICAL PHIMOSIS in young boys — the foreskin is normally non-retractile in infancy and separates over years.** **DO NOT FORCIBLY RETRACT — it causes pain, scarring and true phimosis.** Reassurance and hygiene advice.
> **· PATHOLOGICAL PHIMOSIS — scarred, white, non-retractile, often from LICHEN SCLEROSUS (balanitis xerotica obliterans).** Topical steroid or circumcision. **Lichen sclerosus carries a risk of penile SCC and warrants follow-up.** Cross-refer [[O5]] 0.5.
> **· BALANITIS — candidal, bacterial or irritant. RECURRENT balanitis should prompt a check for DIABETES.**
> **· PENILE CANCER — uncommon; a non-healing ulcer, plaque or warty lesion. Associated with HPV, phimosis, lichen sclerosus and smoking.** **Any persistent penile lesion requires biopsy, not repeated antifungal creams.**
> **· PEYRONIE DISEASE — a fibrous plaque causing curvature, pain and functional difficulty.**
> **· Erectile dysfunction — cross-refer [[O6]] 0.5, including that it is a cardiovascular sentinel event.**

## 0.5 Groin Lumps and Hernias

> [!tip] The differential, and the landmark that matters
> **Inguinal hernia · FEMORAL hernia · lymph nodes · SAPHENA VARIX · femoral artery aneurysm · psoas abscess · lipoma of the cord · hydrocele of the cord · undescended or ectopic testis · and soft tissue tumour.**
> **THE PUBIC TUBERCLE IS THE LANDMARK:**
> **· INGUINAL hernia — the neck lies ABOVE and MEDIAL to the pubic tubercle.**
> **· FEMORAL hernia — the neck lies BELOW and LATERAL to it.**
> **· SAPHENA VARIX — a saphenofemoral junction varicosity: it is soft and compressible, has a cough impulse (so it mimics a hernia), DISAPPEARS COMPLETELY on lying down, has a bluish tinge, and there are usually varicose veins in the leg.** **It is easily mistaken for a hernia and does not need repair.**

> [!danger] Femoral hernias strangulate
> **They are more common in WOMEN, have a NARROW, RIGID neck bounded by the inguinal ligament and lacunar ligament, and carry a HIGH RISK OF STRANGULATION.**
> **They frequently present for the first time WITH strangulation and bowel obstruction**, in an older woman whose small groin lump was never noticed or was assumed to be a node.
> **A femoral hernia warrants PROMPT surgical repair rather than watchful waiting.**
> **Any patient with bowel obstruction needs BOTH groins examined** — a small, tender, irreducible femoral hernia is easily missed under a pannus, and it is a classic cause of "obstruction of unknown cause". Cross-refer [[C5]] 0.5.

> [!warning] Strangulation, and what not to do
> **Features: an IRREDUCIBLE, TENDER hernia, with overlying erythema, systemic upset, and signs of bowel obstruction.** **This is a surgical emergency.**
> **DO NOT ATTEMPT FORCEFUL REDUCTION of a tender irreducible hernia** — you risk **reducing dead or compromised bowel back into the abdomen ("reduction en masse"), where it perforates unobserved.**
> Gentle reduction of a recently incarcerated but non-tender hernia is reasonable; force is not.

> [!tip] Groin lymphadenopathy
> **· Infective — lower limb infection or trauma, genital infection, and SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED causes including syphilis, herpes, chancroid and LYMPHOGRANULOMA VENEREUM (which causes prominent, sometimes suppurative inguinal nodes).** Cross-refer [[O6]] 0.2.
> **· Malignant — MELANOMA and squamous cell carcinoma of the lower limb, and ANAL, VULVAL, PENILE and CERVICAL cancers, all of which drain to the inguinal nodes.** **A hard, fixed or persistent inguinal node warrants examination of the entire lower limb, perineum, genitalia and anus — including between the toes and the soles, where melanomas hide.**
> **· Lymphoma.**
> Cross-refer [[B6]] 0.8.

## 0.4 Renal Colic and Urolithiasis

> [!danger] Rule out an abdominal aortic aneurysm before diagnosing first-episode renal colic over 50
> **A ruptured or symptomatic AAA presents with acute loin, flank or back pain radiating to the groin, sometimes with haematuria — and it is repeatedly misdiagnosed as renal colic.**
> **This is a classic lethal diagnostic error.**
> **In anyone over about 50 with a FIRST presentation of "renal colic", and particularly with cardiovascular risk factors, hypotension, or a pulsatile mass — think aorta, and image accordingly.** Cross-refer [[B5]] 0.4 and [[C1]] 0.7.

> [!tip] The presentation, and one useful discriminator
> **Sudden, severe, COLICKY loin-to-groin pain, with nausea and vomiting, and haematuria (visible or non-visible).**
> **THE PATIENT CANNOT KEEP STILL** — they writhe, pace, and cannot find a comfortable position.
> **This contrasts sharply with PERITONITIS, where the patient lies rigidly still because movement hurts.** **"Can't keep still" versus "won't move" is a genuinely useful bedside discriminator.**
> Radiation follows the stone's position: loin pain higher up, and groin, testicular or labial pain with distal ureteric stones, often with urinary urgency and frequency as the stone nears the bladder.

> [!info] Stone types and what they imply
> **· CALCIUM OXALATE — the commonest. Radio-opaque.**
> **· Calcium phosphate — associated with renal tubular acidosis and hyperparathyroidism.**
> **· URIC ACID — RADIOLUCENT, so INVISIBLE on plain radiographs (though visible on CT). Forms in ACIDIC urine. Associated with gout, high purine intake, obesity, diabetes, and TUMOUR LYSIS.** **They can be dissolved with urinary alkalinisation** — the only stone type where medical dissolution works.
> **· STRUVITE (magnesium ammonium phosphate) — INFECTION stones, formed by urease-producing organisms (Proteus, Klebsiella). They form STAGHORN calculi, harbour infection, and require complete removal.**
> **· CYSTINE — rare, hereditary, presenting young with recurrent stones.**

> [!warning] Imaging and analgesia
> **NON-CONTRAST CT of the kidneys, ureters and bladder is the investigation of choice** — high sensitivity for stones of all composition, and it identifies the alternative diagnoses (including the aneurysm).
> **ULTRASOUND is preferred in PREGNANCY and in CHILDREN**, and is useful for detecting hydronephrosis when radiation is a concern.
> **A plain KUB radiograph misses radiolucent uric acid stones** and has poor sensitivity generally.
> **ANALGESIA: NSAIDs are FIRST-LINE and are MORE EFFECTIVE than opioids for renal colic** — they reduce ureteric smooth muscle activity and renal capsular pressure as well as providing analgesia. **Reaching for an opioid first is common and is not the better choice**, though opioids are used where NSAIDs are contraindicated or insufficient. `UNVERIFIED — agents and doses.`

> [!danger] Obstruction plus infection is a urological emergency
> **An obstructed, infected kidney (pyonephrosis) causes rapidly progressive sepsis, and ANTIBIOTICS ALONE ARE NOT SUFFICIENT.**
> **It requires URGENT DECOMPRESSION — a nephrostomy or a ureteric stent — the same day.**
> **Fever with an obstructing stone is not "a stone plus a urinary tract infection to be treated with antibiotics overnight". It is a drainage problem.** Cross-refer [[F0.3]] 0.10 and [[H2]] 0.4.
> **Other indications for urgent intervention: acute kidney injury from obstruction · obstruction of a SINGLE functioning kidney · bilateral obstruction · intractable pain or vomiting · and a stone unlikely to pass.**

> [!tip] Prevention — and the counterintuitive one about calcium
> **· FLUID INTAKE is the single most effective preventive measure** — enough to produce a consistently dilute urine.
> **· DO NOT RESTRICT DIETARY CALCIUM.** **This is counterintuitive and important: restricting dietary calcium INCREASES stone risk, because calcium in the gut binds oxalate and prevents its absorption. Low-calcium diets increase oxalate absorption and urinary oxalate.** **Normal dietary calcium intake is protective.**
> **· Reduce SODIUM (which increases urinary calcium), reduce animal protein, and reduce oxalate-rich foods in oxalate stone formers.**
> **· THIAZIDE diuretics reduce urinary calcium in hypercalciuria · ALLOPURINOL for uric acid stones and hyperuricosuria · POTASSIUM CITRATE for hypocitraturia and for alkalinising urine in uric acid stones.**
> **· Investigate recurrent or young stone formers metabolically — calcium, urate, PTH, and a 24-hour urine collection — and analyse the stone if one is retrieved.**

## 0.6 Urological Trauma

> [!danger] Blood at the meatus means no catheter
> **Signs of urethral injury: BLOOD AT THE URETHRAL MEATUS · a HIGH-RIDING or impalpable prostate · perineal or scrotal BRUISING (butterfly haematoma) · and inability to void with a palpable bladder.**
> **DO NOT ATTEMPT URETHRAL CATHETERISATION** — it can convert a partial urethral tear into a complete disruption.
> **Perform a RETROGRADE URETHROGRAM first, and use a SUPRAPUBIC catheter if drainage is needed.**
> **Urethral injury is strongly associated with PELVIC FRACTURE.** Cross-refer [[L8]] 0.5.

> [!tip] The other injuries
> **· RENAL TRAUMA — the DEGREE of haematuria correlates POORLY with the severity of injury; significant renal injury can occur with minimal or no haematuria, particularly with deceleration injuries and renal pedicle avulsion.** **CT with a DELAYED (excretory) phase is required to assess the collecting system** — a standard portal venous phase misses urinary extravasation. **Most renal injuries are managed conservatively in the haemodynamically stable patient.**
> **· BLADDER RUPTURE — intraperitoneal (typically from blunt force to a full bladder; requires surgical repair) versus extraperitoneal (typically with pelvic fracture; usually managed with catheter drainage).** **CT cystogram distinguishes them.**
> **· TESTICULAR TRAUMA — ultrasound to assess for rupture of the tunica albuginea, which requires exploration and repair; a haematocele may also warrant drainage.**
> **· IATROGENIC injury — urethral trauma from catheterisation, and URETERIC injury during pelvic, colorectal and gynaecological surgery, which may present late with a urinoma, ileus, flank pain, or a rising creatinine.**

> [!danger] Penile fracture — a surgical emergency that patients delay presenting with
> **Rupture of the tunica albuginea of the corpus cavernosum, almost always during vigorous intercourse.**
> **The history is characteristic: an AUDIBLE CRACK or POP, IMMEDIATE DETUMESCENCE, severe pain, and rapid swelling and bruising producing the "AUBERGINE (eggplant) DEFORMITY".**
> **It is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY — prompt exploration and repair produces substantially better functional outcomes than conservative management, which risks permanent curvature and erectile dysfunction.**
> **Around a third have an associated URETHRAL INJURY**, so ask about haematuria and difficulty voiding, and examine for blood at the meatus.
> **Patients frequently delay presenting through embarrassment**, and may present late or with a vague history — **which makes it worth asking directly and without judgement about the mechanism.**

**Ix:** **Non-contrast CT KUB for suspected renal colic** (*why:* the test of choice, and it also identifies the alternatives including aortic aneurysm; *what:* stone, hydronephrosis, alternative pathology). Ultrasound in pregnancy and children (*why:* avoids radiation; *what:* hydronephrosis, stone). **Urinalysis** (*why:* haematuria supports a stone, and pyuria with fever suggests infection requiring drainage; *what:* blood, leucocytes, nitrites). UEC (*why:* obstructive renal impairment; *what:* creatinine rise). **FBC, CRP and blood cultures where febrile** (*why:* an obstructed infected system requires urgent decompression; *what:* sepsis). **Retrograde urethrogram BEFORE catheterisation where urethral injury is suspected** (*why:* prevents converting a partial to a complete injury; *what:* extravasation). **CT with a DELAYED phase for renal trauma** (*why:* the excretory phase is needed to see collecting system injury; *what:* laceration, urinary extravasation, vascular injury). Scrotal ultrasound in trauma (*why:* testicular rupture requires exploration; *what:* tunica disruption, haematocele). **Stone analysis and metabolic workup in recurrent formers** (*why:* determines specific prevention; *what:* stone composition, urinary abnormality).

## Acute Scrotal Pain

`CF-PAIR` **Three accounts of the acute scrotum sit in this file and none declared the others before 2026-09-02:** `07 §0.20 Testicular Torsion` · `H4 §0.1 The Acute Scrotum` · `NEW_Renal_and_Urology ## Acute Scrotal Pain`. **The safety content is split:** `H4 §0.1` alone carries *"A NORMAL DOPPLER ULTRASOUND DOES NOT EXCLUDE TORSION"*, and `NEW_Renal_and_Urology` alone carries the **~6-hour salvage window**. All three say imaging must not delay theatre. **All kept, none reconciled.**

- **D:** acute pain in the scrotum or testis. **The entire presentation exists to answer one question: is this testicular torsion?** Everything else in the differential can wait; torsion cannot.
- **DDx:**
  - **Testicular torsion** — twisting of the spermatic cord causing ischaemia. **Peak incidence is bimodal: the neonatal period and adolescence around puberty**, but it occurs at any age and **age is never a reason to dismiss it**. Classically sudden, severe, unilateral pain, often with nausea and vomiting, sometimes waking the patient from sleep, and sometimes with a history of previous self-resolving episodes (intermittent torsion) — a history worth asking for specifically.
  - **Torsion of a testicular appendage (hydatid of Morgagni)** — commoner in pre-pubertal boys, usually less severe, with a tender upper pole and sometimes a **"blue dot" sign**. Benign, but **it cannot reliably be distinguished from torsion clinically**, which is why suspicion still triggers surgical assessment.
  - **Epididymo-orchitis** — more gradual onset, often with urinary symptoms, fever, or urethral discharge. In sexually active younger men consider **chlamydia and gonorrhoea**; in older men and those with urinary tract abnormality, coliforms. **Its main clinical danger is that it is the diagnosis torsion gets mislabelled as.**
  - **Incarcerated or strangulated inguinal hernia** — examine the groin in every case.
  - **Trauma** — including testicular rupture and haematocele.
  - **Fournier's gangrene** — necrotising fasciitis of the perineum; systemic toxicity, pain out of proportion, crepitus, skin necrosis. More common in diabetes, immunosuppression and the elderly. A surgical emergency.
  - **Other:** testicular tumour with haemorrhage, varicocele, hydrocele, epididymal cyst, referred pain from **ureteric colic** (a stone at the vesicoureteric junction classically refers to the testis — and the reverse error, calling a torsion "renal colic", also occurs), **ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm** referring to the groin in an older man, **Henoch–Schönlein purpura** with scrotal involvement in a child, idiopathic scrotal oedema, mumps orchitis.
- **Focused Hx:** **time of onset, to the hour** — this determines salvage and must be documented; speed of onset (**abrupt suggests torsion; over days suggests infection**); severity; nausea and vomiting (common in torsion, uncommon in epididymitis); **previous identical episodes that resolved spontaneously**; trauma, including trivial trauma, which frequently precedes torsion and can mislead; **urinary symptoms — dysuria, frequency, discharge** (favouring infection); **sexual history**, taken properly; fever; recent mumps or parotitis; known undescended testis or previous testicular surgery; abdominal pain; in a child, a full account from the parent and a **developmental and safeguarding-aware history**.
- **Examination:** **examine the patient standing and lying, with consent and a chaperone, and examine the normal side first.**
  - **Inspect** for swelling, erythema, a **high-riding or transversely lying testis** (torsion), the **blue dot sign** (appendage torsion), skin necrosis or crepitus (Fournier's).
  - **Palpate** gently: **diffuse testicular tenderness suggests torsion; tenderness localised to the epididymis suggests epididymitis** — a useful but **not reliable** discriminator.
  - **Cremasteric reflex** — **absence supports torsion**, but **its presence does not exclude it**, and this is a commonly over-trusted sign.
  - **Prehn's sign** (relief of pain on elevating the testis, said to favour epididymitis) — **explicitly unreliable and should not be used to exclude torsion.**
  - **Examine the abdomen and both inguinal regions** for a hernia, a mass, or an aneurysm.
  - **Vital signs**, and in a child, a full examination including for a purpuric rash.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **If torsion is clinically suspected, the investigation is surgical exploration.** Multiple sources agree that **imaging must not delay theatre** when the history and examination are suggestive — a scrotal ultrasound obtained "to be sure" is a recognised cause of testicular loss.
  - **Scrotal Doppler ultrasound** where the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain and it will not delay surgery. **A normal ultrasound does not exclude torsion**, particularly with intermittent or partial torsion.
  - **Urinalysis and urine culture**; **first-pass urine NAAT for chlamydia and gonorrhoea** in sexually active patients; **urethral swab** if discharge present.
  - **FBC, CRP, UEC**; **blood cultures** if febrile and unwell.
  - **Group and hold** and fasting from presentation if surgery is likely — practical intern actions that prevent delay in theatre.
  - **β-hCG, AFP and LDH** if a testicular tumour is suspected on examination.

> [!danger] **The time window, which is why this is a Tier 1 topic.** Sources consistently report **salvage rates above 90% when surgical exploration occurs within about 6 hours of pain onset, falling to roughly 50% beyond about 12 hours**, with progressive loss thereafter. Three independent sources agreed on this pattern.
> The intern-level actions that follow are: **document the time of onset explicitly**, **refer to urology or surgery immediately on suspicion rather than after investigation**, **keep the patient fasted**, **give analgesia**, and **do not order an ultrasound if it will delay exploration**. Note also that **torsion can present with abdominal or groin pain and no scrotal complaint at all** — so **every male presenting with acute lower abdominal pain needs his scrotum examined**, which is the omission that most often produces the missed diagnosis.

## Acute Urinary Retention

`CF-PAIR` **Three accounts of acute urinary retention sit in this file and none declared the others before 2026-09-02:** `07 §0.12 Urinary Retention` · `H2 §0.3 Acute Urinary Retention` · `NEW_Renal_and_Urology ## Acute Urinary Retention`. **The safety rule is in ONE of them:** `H2 §0.3` carries *"DO NOT CLAMP THE CATHETER intermittently to decompress slowly"* and the discharge-without-monitoring error; the other two mention post-obstructive diuresis without it. **All kept, none reconciled.**

- **D:** the sudden inability to pass urine, with a painfully distended bladder. **It is painful — and painless retention is a different and more sinister problem**, suggesting chronic retention or a neurological cause such as cauda equina syndrome or spinal cord compression.
- **DDx:**
  - **Obstructive:** **benign prostatic hyperplasia — much the commonest in men**, prostate cancer, urethral stricture, phimosis or paraphimosis, meatal stenosis, **blocked or displaced urinary catheter** (check this first in any catheterised patient), bladder or urethral stone, clot retention after haematuria, pelvic mass, **faecal impaction — common, reversible and frequently overlooked**, and in women, pelvic organ prolapse, gravid uterus or ovarian mass.
  - **Neurological:** **cauda equina syndrome** and **spinal cord compression** — the diagnoses that must be excluded, particularly when retention is painless or accompanied by back pain, leg symptoms or saddle numbness; multiple sclerosis; diabetic autonomic neuropathy; stroke; post-spinal or epidural anaesthesia.
  - **Pharmacological — check the chart, because this group is reversible:** **anticholinergics** (oxybutynin, amitriptyline, antihistamines, antipsychotics), **opioids**, **alpha-agonists including over-the-counter decongestants (pseudoephedrine)**, benzodiazepines, general and spinal anaesthesia, NSAIDs, and **alcohol**.
  - **Infective and inflammatory:** urinary tract infection and prostatitis, **herpes zoster or herpes simplex affecting the sacral dermatomes**, urethritis, vulvovaginitis in children.
  - **Postoperative retention** — very common, multifactorial (anaesthesia, opioids, immobility, pain, fluid load), and an intern's routine problem.
- **Focused Hx:** time since last void and volume passed; **pain — present or absent**; preceding **lower urinary tract symptoms** (hesitancy, poor stream, terminal dribbling, incomplete emptying, frequency, nocturia); **haematuria and clots**; **back pain, leg weakness, saddle numbness, faecal incontinence or constipation** — the cauda equina screen, asked in every case; fever, dysuria and urethral discharge; **complete drug history including over-the-counter decongestants and antihistamines**; recent surgery, anaesthesia and immobility; alcohol; known prostate disease, previous retention, previous catheterisation or urethral instrumentation; neurological history; and in women, obstetric and prolapse history.
- **Examination:** **palpate and percuss for a distended bladder** — dull to percussion, tender, arising from the pelvis; **abdominal examination** for masses and for constipation; **external genitalia** — phimosis, paraphimosis, meatal stenosis; **digital rectal examination** for prostate size and character, faecal loading, and **anal tone** (reduced tone is a cauda equina sign); **perineal and saddle sensation**; **lower limb neurological examination including reflexes and plantar responses**; **spinal palpation**; and in a catheterised patient, **inspect the catheter and tubing for kinking, blockage or displacement before assuming a new pathology**.
- **First-line Ix:**
  - **Bladder scan (post-void residual volume) to confirm — and record the volume.**
  - **Urethral catheterisation is both diagnostic and therapeutic**, and **the drained volume should be measured and documented**, since it guides everything afterwards.
  - **UEC and creatinine** — to identify **obstructive acute kidney injury**, which changes management and disposition; **FBC, CRP**; **glucose**; **calcium**.
  - **Urinalysis and urine culture** — sent after catheterisation.
  - **PSA — but not during acute retention or immediately after catheterisation**, since both falsely elevate it; defer it, and this is a genuinely common error.
  - **Ultrasound of the renal tract** where the residual is large, the creatinine is raised, or hydronephrosis is suspected.
  - **Urgent MRI of the spine** where any cauda equina or cord compression feature is present — **and this takes priority over urological work-up**.

> [!warning] **Two things to watch after relieving the obstruction.** **(1) Post-obstructive diuresis** — a large-volume diuresis can follow decompression of a chronically obstructed bladder, causing hypovolaemia and electrolyte disturbance, so urine output, fluid balance and electrolytes need monitoring rather than a catheter and discharge. **(2) Haematuria ex vacuo** — decompression bleeding, usually self-limiting. *(Specific advice on whether to decompress gradually or completely, and the volume thresholds involved, is not stated here: it did not meet the three-source standard, and practice varies. Drain the bladder, monitor the output, and follow local guidance.)*
