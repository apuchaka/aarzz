## Pruritus vulvae (vaginal itch)

**Causes**: allergy (e.g. washing powder, dyes), skin disease (e.g. psoriasis, lichen sclerosis), infection (e.g. candida), infestations (e.g. scabies), vulval dystrophy.

**Mx**
- Treat cause if possible
- Avoid sensitisers; showers rather than baths (baths may worsen it)
- Clean vulval area with emollients, only 1x/day
- Short course of topical steroids ± topical antifungal may help
- If no response to treatment: investigate for vulval intraepithelial neoplasia or carcinoma

---

## Lichen sclerosis

**D**: autoimmune condition characterised by skin atrophy and hypopigmentation, most commonly affecting genitalia.

- Affects F>M, elderly; 40% develop other autoimmune disorders

**S/smx**: itchy white patches ± scarring ± pain (intercourse or urination).

> [!info] "Bruised" red, purpuric signs may appear early and can be mistaken for abuse — this is a universal clinical teaching point, not jurisdiction-specific guidance; maintain clinical awareness of this differential.

**Ix**: clinical diagnosis ± biopsy if doubt (no response to treatment or cancer suspected).

**Mx**: topical steroids and emollients; topical tacrolimus if no response.

> [!info] Long-term monitoring required — these are premalignant lesions.

---

## Leukoplakia

**D**: white vulval patches due to skin thickening and hypertrophy (may appear on other mucosal surfaces, e.g. mouth).

**S/smx**: white patches that do not rub off, itchy.

**Ix**: biopsy (premalignant lesion).

**Mx**: topical corticosteroids; other options include UV phototherapy, methotrexate, ciclosporin.

---

## Lichen planus

> [!note] See [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Lichen planus for the fuller general/cutaneous presentation (the "6 Ps" mnemonic, Wickham's striae, oral involvement, and the malignant transformation risk of oral disease), not repeated here — this entry covers the genital-specific presentation.

**D**: chronic inflammatory dermatosis from keratinocyte apoptosis; can affect various body sites including genitalia.

**R**: hepatitis C infection.

**S/smx**: pain > itch ("planus for pain"). In mouth/genital area, can be erosive with a well-demarcated glazed appearance around the introitus.

**Ix**: clinical diagnosis.

**Mx**: topical potent corticosteroid; tacrolimus.

> [!tip] **Graded vaginal dilators alongside topical treatment**, to prevent the adhesions and stenosis that erosive vulvovaginal lichen planus can cause — the scarring is the outcome that matters long-term, and treating the inflammation without addressing it can leave a structurally narrowed vagina after the disease is controlled. *(This recommendation is attributed to **ACOG, a United States college**; no equivalent RANZCOG statement was identified. The practice is standard and not jurisdictionally contentious, but the attribution is US and is labelled as such rather than presented as the local position.)*

> [!note] See [[09_08_Dermatology_-_Miscellaneous]] Lichen planus for the cutaneous form — the "6 Ps" morphology, distribution and natural history (often self-limiting over 1–2 years) — which this vulvovaginal entry does not repeat. Note the two differ in emphasis for a real reason: cutaneous disease is typically **itchy**, while the vulval/oral form is characteristically **painful and erosive**, and it is the erosive form that scars.

---

## Lichen simplex (chronicus)

**D**: ≈ neurodermatitis; cutaneous disorder with well-circumscribed erythematous, often hyperpigmented plaques of thickened lichenified skin. May be primary, or 2° chronic rubbing/scratching from another pruritic disorder.

**S/smx**: chronic intractable itching, especially at night; non-specific vulval inflammation ± mons pubis and inner thighs; exacerbated by stress, sensitising chemicals, low body iron stores.

**Ix**: clinical diagnosis ± biopsy if unsure.

**Mx**: vulval care; steroids to break the itch-scratch cycle ± antihistamines.

---

## Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN)

**D**: non-invasive squamous lesion; precursor to vulval squamous cell carcinoma.

**R**: HPV 16 & 18, smoking, HSV2, lichen planus.

**S/smx**: itching/burning of vulva; raised, well-defined skin lesions.

**Mx**: surveillance + biopsy of suspicious lesions (recurrence common). Imiquimod cream may help regress disease — applied 2–3x/week for 12w.

---

## Genital warts

> [!note] See [[08_08_Infectious_Disease_-_Genitourinary_Infections_and_STIs]] Genital warts for the equivalent entry, not repeated in full here — content is consistent between both.

🐞 HPV 6 & 11 (most common); HPV 16, 18, 33 (cancer-associated).

**S/smx**: small (2–5mm) fleshy protuberances, slightly pigmented; may bleed or itch.

**Mx**
- Multiple, non-keratinised warts: topical podophyllum
- Solitary, keratinised: cryotherapy; topical imiquimod
- Can be resistant and recurrent; most clear without intervention within 1–2 years

---

## Genital herpes

> [!note] See [[08_08_Infectious_Disease_-_Genitourinary_Infections_and_STIs]] Genital herpes for a complementary, slightly less detailed version of this entry, not repeated here — content is consistent between both (the pregnancy C-section timing threshold was previously inconsistent between the two entries; both are now corrected to "within 6 weeks of the due date").

🐞 HSV1, HSV2.

**S/smx**: painful genital ulceration; associated with dysuria and pruritus. Primary infection often more severe than recurrent episodes (systemic features — headache, fever, malaise — more common in primary infection). ± tender inguinal lymphadenopathy, urinary retention.

**Ix**: NAAT; ?HSV serology.

**Mx**
- Strong analgesia (e.g. lidocaine gel)
- Salt baths (including urinating in the bath)
- Aciclovir shortens symptom duration (higher doses if immunocompromised)
- If >6 episodes/year: consider suppressive aciclovir for 6–12 months

**Pregnancy**: elective C-section if first episode occurs during pregnancy within 6w of due date or at time of delivery; high-dose aciclovir required to ↓fetal infection risk. *(See also Obstetrics 16.07.)*

---

## Vulval carcinoma

**D**: cancer of the vulva — 80% squamous cell carcinoma, 5% vulval melanoma.

**R**: ↑age, HPV infection (16, 18, 33), vulval intraepithelial neoplasia, lichen sclerosis, immunosuppression.

**S/smx**: lump/ulcer on labia majora; inguinal lymphadenopathy; ± itching or irritation (or asymptomatic). Vulval melanoma often presents late, with bleeding, mass, ulceration.

**Ix**: biopsy + histology.

**Mx**: surgical excision ± adjuvant radiation and/or chemotherapy.

---

## Other vulval lumps and bumps

| Condition | Notes |
|---|---|
| Urethral caruncle | Small red swelling at urethral orifice, 2° meatal prolapse; may be tender, pain on urination; Mx: excision or diathermy |
| Bartholin's cyst and abscess | Bartholin's gland under labia minora; blocked duct → painless cyst; infected → abscess (very painful, patient cannot sit, hot red labium); Mx: incise + drain |
| Vulval ulcers | Painless genital ulcer — primary syphilis (chancre); condylomata lata (secondary syphilis) |
| Other | Local varicose veins, uterine prolapse, uterine polyp, inguinal hernia, molluscum contagiosum |

## Vaginal discharge — differential diagnosis

**Physiological discharge**: due to pregnancy, sexual arousal, puberty, COCP.

### Bacterial vaginosis (BV)

🐞 Anaerobic *Gardnerella vaginalis*.

**MOA**: overgrowth of *G. vaginalis* → fall in aerobic lactobacilli producing lactic acid → ↑pH → symptoms.

> [!info] Amsel's criteria for diagnosis (≥3 of 4)
> - Thin, white, homogeneous discharge
> - Clue cells on microscopy (stippled vaginal epithelial cells)
> - Vaginal pH >4.5
> - +ve whiff test (KOH addition → fishy odour)

> [!info] Verified against the WA Health "Quick guide to STI and BBV management" (2026 edition), Aug 2026 — **the duration and dose are consistent with Australian practice**: metronidazole 400mg orally 12-hourly for 7 days (a 2g single dose is also an option but less effective), or topical metronidazole/clindamycin — the file's original figures don't need correcting.
> **Mx**: PO metronidazole 400mg BD for 5–7 days (70–80% initial cure, relapse >50% within 3 months); or topical metronidazole/clindamycin.

**Complications in pregnancy**: preterm labour, low birthweight, chorioamnionitis, late miscarriage. Treat still with low-dose PO metronidazole.

### Trichomonas vaginalis

**S/smx**: offensive, yellow/green, frothy vaginal discharge; vulvovaginitis with "strawberry cervix"; pH >4.5. In males: often asymptomatic or urethritis.

**Ix**: microscopy of wet mount shows mobile trophozoites.

> [!info] Verified against the WA Health "Quick guide to STI and BBV management" (2026 edition), Aug 2026 — **the dosing below already matches current Australian practice exactly** (metronidazole 2g single dose, or 400mg BD for 7 days) — no correction needed.
> **Mx**: **PO metronidazole 400mg BD for 7 days, or a one-off 2g dose** — the dose is now stated here rather than only in the box above, which named it while the line beneath said just "5–7 days".

### Chlamydia trachomatis

🐞 Obligate intracellular *Chlamydia trachomatis*.

**S/smx**: asymptomatic in 70% of females; incubation 7–21 days. Cervicitis (discharge, bleeding), dysuria.

**Ix**: vulvovaginal swab in females, carried out 2w post-exposure.

> [!info] Verified against the WA Health "Quick guide to STI and BBV management" (2026 edition), Aug 2026 — **a genuine correction to the azithromycin course**: Australian first-line for uncomplicated genital chlamydia is doxycycline 100mg BD for 7 days, with azithromycin **1g as a single dose** (not "3 days") as the alternative — the file's "azithromycin 3 days" was incorrect; single-dose azithromycin is the standard alternative regimen. For anorectal infection specifically, doxycycline extends to 21 days if symptomatic (7 days if asymptomatic), or azithromycin 1g followed by a second 1g dose 12–24 hours later. In pregnancy, azithromycin 1g single dose is first-line.
> **Mx**: doxycycline 100mg BD for 7 days; or azithromycin 1g single dose. If pregnant — azithromycin 1g single dose. Partner notification and treatment of most recent partner, then testing based on exposure.

**Complications**: PID → perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis), endometritis, ↑ectopic pregnancy risk, infertility, reactive arthritis.

### Gonorrhoea

🐞 Gram-negative diplococcus *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*.

**S/smx**: genitourinary, rectal, pharyngeal sites; incubates 2–5 days; cervicitis, vaginal discharge; rectal/pharyngeal infection often asymptomatic.

**Ix**: swabs (NAAT and culture).

> [!info] Verified against the WA Health "Quick guide to STI and BBV management" (2026 edition), Aug 2026 — **a genuine, clinically meaningful correction**: Australian first-line for uncomplicated (including anorectal) gonorrhoea is **ceftriaxone 500mg IM** (with 1% lignocaine) **plus azithromycin 1g PO, given together as a single treatment** — not the file's "1g ceftriaxone" (double the correct dose) or the cefixime-based alternative, which is not standard Australian first-line. For pharyngeal gonorrhoea specifically, the azithromycin dose increases to 2g (ceftriaxone dose unchanged at 500mg). Dual therapy (ceftriaxone + azithromycin together) is the key structural point — not a choice between single agents.
> **Mx**: ceftriaxone 500mg IM (in 1% lignocaine) **plus** azithromycin 1g PO, given together as a single treatment (uncomplicated genital or anorectal infection). For pharyngeal infection: same ceftriaxone dose, azithromycin increased to 2g.

**Complications**: disseminated gonococcal infection — tenosynovitis, migratory polyarthritis, dermatitis (maculopapular or vesicular).

### Candidiasis (thrush)

🐞 *C. albicans* (95%) or *C. glabrata*.

**R**: DM, drugs (antibiotics, steroids), pregnancy, HIV.

**S/smx**: cottage cheese discharge, not offensive-smelling; vulvitis (red, fissured, sore vulva/vagina) — may cause superficial dyspareunia and dysuria; itchy; may have thrush elsewhere (e.g. mouth).

**Ix**: clinical diagnosis; high vaginal swab not routinely indicated.

> [!info] Verified — fluconazole 150mg PO single dose is confirmed standard first-line Australian practice for uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis (consistent with the WA Health STI management guide's broader statement that imidazole/triazole preparations, whether oral or topical, are all effective), and clotrimazole pessary as the pregnancy-safe topical alternative is likewise standard — no correction needed to this entry.
> **Mx**: fluconazole 150mg PO single dose; or clotrimazole 500mg intravaginal pessary if PO therapy contraindicated (e.g. pregnancy). Add topical imidazole if vulval symptoms.

**If recurrent (≥4 episodes/year)**: ensure treatment compliance; confirm diagnosis with high vaginal swab ± check for DM; rule out similar conditions (e.g. lichen sclerosis); induction-maintenance regime — induction with fluconazole PO every 3 days for 3 doses, maintenance with fluconazole PO weekly for 6 months.

### Discharge in children
- May be infection from faecal flora associated with prepubertal atrophic vaginitis
- Likely causative agents: Staph, Strep, threadworms (pruritus)

> [!danger] Always consider sexual abuse in a child presenting with vaginal discharge.

- **Ix**: vulval ± vaginal swab, MSU (checking for DM)
- **Mx**: hygiene ± antibiotics (erythromycin)

## Urinary incontinence

**D**: involuntary loss of urine.

> [!info] Clinical classification
> - **Stress incontinence**: leakage on effort, exertion, sneezing, or coughing
> - **Urgency incontinence**: accompanied by or immediately preceded by urgency
> - **Overactive bladder** (detrusor overactivity): urgency ± frequency ± incontinence
> - **Mixed incontinence**
> - **Nocturia**: sleep interruption to urinate ≥1x/night
> - **Nocturnal enuresis**: involuntary urine loss during sleep
> - **Continuous incontinence**
> - **Overflow incontinence**: leakage from an over-distended bladder
> - **Functional incontinence**: patient unable to reach a bathroom in time (e.g. dementia, hemiplegia)

**R**: ↑age, previous pregnancy/childbirth, high BMI, hysterectomy, FHx.

**Ix**: rule out UTI; urodynamics to confirm diagnosis (guides Mx options).

**Mx (general)**: avoid excessive fluid intake, caffeine, fizzy drinks, squash, alcohol (bladder irritants).

**Mx of urge incontinence**
- Bladder retraining — ↑intervals between voiding, ≥6 weeks training
- Antimuscarinics (e.g. oxybutynin, tolterodine, darifenacin)
- Mirabegron — useful if concerns about anticholinergic side effects in frail elderly patients

**Mx of stress incontinence**
- Pelvic floor muscle training — ≥8 contractions performed 3x/day for ≥3 months
- Surgical: e.g. retropubic mid-urethral tape procedures
- If patient declines surgery: offer duloxetine (SNRI)

## Pelvic organ prolapse

Also known as urogenital prolapse — general term encompassing:
- **Cystocele** (bladder prolapse)
- **Rectocele** (prolapse of rectum/large bowel)
- **Enterocele** (small bowel prolapse)
- **Uterine prolapse** (prolapse of uterus)

**R**: vaginal delivery (↑risk with more births), ↑age, ↑BMI, previous prolapse surgery, FHx, white ancestry, spina bifida.

**A/P**: damage to pelvic musculature, ligaments, and nerves → weakness of pelvic floor muscles ± levator ani.

**S/smx**
- May be asymptomatic
- Sensation of pressure/heaviness, dragging feeling, feeling of a lump coming down
- Others: dyspareunia, backache
- Urinary symptoms: incontinence, frequency, urgency, incomplete bladder emptying
- Rectocele: constipation, difficulty with defecation

**Ix**: exam to rule out pelvic masses; urodynamic studies if urinary incontinence present.

**Mx**
- **Conservative**: weight loss, pelvic floor muscle exercise, avoid straining
- **Pessaries**: usually ring-shaped, placed between the posterior aspect of the symphysis pubis and posterior vaginal fornix; change every 6 months
- **Surgery**: indicated for severe symptoms, sexually active patients, or pessary failure — redundant tissue excision + support strengthening (may reduce vaginal width); severity guides options

> [!danger] Vaginal mesh procedures have been banned due to a high complication rate — verify current Australian TGA position on mesh use, as this has also been subject to significant regulatory action in Australia.

> [!info] Localised for Australia — verified against the Australian Government's National Cervical Screening Program (NCSP), Aug 2026. Australia's programme genuinely differs from the UK's in age range, interval structure, and terminology (Cervical Screening Test, not "smear") — not just in specific numbers.

## Cervical cancer screening

`CF-PAIR` **A second account is in `OBGYN_06_Investigations`, at `NEW_Investigations_Obstetrics_and_Gynaecology §0.1 Cervical Screening Test and Cervical Screening Abnormality (Australian NCSP)`**, which is the test-led version — how the CST is read and what each result triggers. **Both kept in full, neither reconciled.**

**D**: the **Cervical Screening Test (CST)** — a sample collected from the ecto-/endocervical junction, tested primarily for high-risk HPV; if HPV-positive, the same sample is reflexed to cytological (liquid-based cytology) examination without needing a second visit. Self-collection (a vaginal swab the patient takes themselves, rather than a speculum examination) is a validated, available option in Australia for eligible patients, and is a genuinely important access feature, particularly for patients who find a speculum examination distressing or who are under-screened.

> [!danger] A genuine, severe Australian health equity issue with a specific, proven, actionable intervention — not a generic epidemiological footnote. **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women experience cervical cancer at roughly 2 times the incidence and up to 4 times the mortality rate of non-Indigenous women** (incidence ~23 per 100,000 vs the non-Indigenous rate in the same period; mortality ~8 per 100,000, almost 4-fold higher) — despite Australia being on track to become the first country to formally eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem, a target that genuinely cannot be met unless this specific gap closes. Nationally, over 70% of all cervical cancers occur in women who have never been screened or have screened only occasionally, meaning **participation itself — not treatment once diagnosed — is the single biggest lever** for closing this gap.
> - **Self-collection is a genuinely proven, specific intervention, not just a generic access improvement**: Australian Department of Health data show patients offered HPV self-collection are **more than twice as likely to participate** in screening compared with only being offered a clinician-collected sample. Since **July 2022**, self-collection has been available to **all** eligible patients as a genuine choice (not restricted to under-screened groups specifically) — a significant policy expansion worth knowing the date of, given it represents a real and relatively recent shift in Australian screening access.
> - **Uptake in remote areas confirms this is genuinely reaching the population that needs it most**: national data show self-collection accounts for roughly two-thirds of samples from screening participants in very remote areas specifically — evidence the option is being used precisely where standard clinician-collected screening access is hardest.
> - **A genuinely important nuance for actually closing the gap, not just offering the option**: survey data of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women found never-screened participants were **less likely to have heard of self-collection** than those already engaged with screening, despite the majority saying awareness of it would make them more likely to screen — meaning proactively **informing** eligible patients that self-collection exists, not just passively making it available, is itself a meaningful part of closing this gap.

> [!info] Australian NCSP schedule — a single 5-yearly interval across the whole eligible age range, structurally simpler than the UK's split-interval-by-age-band approach.
> - Eligible age range: **25–74 years** (not the UK's 25–64) — screening starts later than the UK given evidence that screening under 25 doesn't reduce cervical cancer cases/deaths in this age group (given cervical cancer is rare here and abnormalities usually resolve naturally), and continues later given ongoing risk in older women.
> - **Every 5 years** for the entire eligible range if HPV-negative — Australia does not use the UK's split 3-year (25–49yo) vs 5-year (50–64yo) structure; it's a single 5-yearly interval throughout, reflecting the higher sensitivity of primary HPV testing over cytology.
> - An "exit test" between 70–74 years of age is recommended before leaving the programme.
> - If pregnant: cervical screening can generally still be performed if due, though timing in pregnancy should be discussed with the treating clinician — check current NCSP guidance for pregnancy-specific timing rather than assuming the UK's fixed "delay until 3 months postpartum" rule applies.
> - Never sexually active: very low HPV/cervical cancer risk, can reasonably opt out, consistent with the original note.

**Pathway after testing**
- HPV -ve: continue normal 5-yearly schedule
- HPV +ve & cytology normal: repeat test in 12 months — if HPV -ve, continue normal schedule; if HPV +ve again, colposcopy
- HPV +ve & deranged cytology: refer for colposcopy
  - Colposcopy -ve: continue normal schedule

  - CIN I detected: repeat smear in 12 months
  - CIN II or III: refer for large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ)
- Moderate dyskaryosis with CIN being treated: repeat smear in 6 months as test of cure

> [!danger] If cervical cancer is detected on smear: **refer urgently for colposcopy** — via the National Cervical Screening Program pathway, which specifies the referral timeframe.

- Inadequate sample: repeat in 3 months; if 2 consecutive inadequate samples, refer for colposcopy

## Cervical cancer

**D**: HPV-related malignancy of the uterine cervical mucosa. 80% squamous cell carcinoma, 20% adenocarcinoma. Two age peaks: 30s and >70yo.

**R**: HPV (16, 18, 33 highest risk), HIV, early onset of sexual activity, multiple sexual partners, ↑parity, COCP, smoking, lower socioeconomic status, immunosuppression.

**A/P**: HPV activates oncoproteins driving CIN and eventually invasive cancer.

**S/smx**
- Asymptomatic — incidental finding during CIN treatment
- Postcoital ± postmenopausal bleeding, or watery vaginal discharge
- Features of advanced disease: heavy vaginal bleeding, ureteric obstruction, weight loss, bowel disturbance, vesicovaginal fistula, pain

**Ix**: pap smear, biopsy.

**Mx by stage**
- Stage I (confined to cervix): hysterectomy + lymphadenectomy
- Stage II (local spread to vagina): chemoradiotherapy
- Stage IV (spread to bladder, rectum, or distant organs): palliative radiotherapy may control bleeding

**P**: 5-year survival ~67% overall (best prognosis for Stage I — 91%). Most recurrences occur within 2–3 years.

## Vaginal cancer

**D**: cancer of the vagina. Most are metastatic spread from cervical, uterine, or vulval cancer. True primary vaginal cancer is mostly SCC, mean age 60yo.

**R**: similar to cervical cancer (especially HPV).

**S/smx**: many asymptomatic; vaginal bleeding (usually postcoital or postmenopausal); vaginal mass.

**Ix**: biopsy.

**Mx**: as per cervical cancer — lower stage tumours may be resectable, higher stages require chemoradiotherapy.

---

## Endometrial cancer

**D**: epithelial malignancy of the uterine corpus mucosa, usually adenocarcinoma.

**R**
- ↑age (>50yo)
- Excess oestrogen: nulliparity, early menarche, late menopause, unopposed oestrogen (adding progesterone ↓risk if on oestrogen)
- Metabolic syndrome: obesity, DM, PCOS
- Tamoxifen
- Hereditary non-polyposis colorectal carcinoma (HNPCC/Lynch syndrome)

**Protective factors**: multiparity, COCP, smoking (uncertain).

**S/smx**
- Postmenopausal bleeding — slight, intermittent → heavier
- Premenopausal: may present as menorrhagia or intermenstrual bleeding
- Pain — may indicate extensive disease
- Vaginal discharge — not common

> [!danger] Red flag
> ≥55yo with postmenopausal bleeding → **urgent referral to gynaecology**.

**Ix**: transvaginal US (normal endometrial thickness <4mm has a -ve predictive value); hysteroscopy with endometrial biopsy.

**Mx**
- Localised disease: total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy ± adjuvant radiotherapy
- Later-stage disease: chemotherapy, immunotherapy ± radiotherapy
- If not a surgical candidate: progesterone therapy may be used

---

## Endometrial hyperplasia

**D**: abnormal proliferation of the endometrium — may be a precursor to endometrial cancer.
- Divided into: with atypia vs without atypia (with atypia = endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia, EIN — precancerous)
- Further divided into simple or complex

**S/smx**: EIN usually presents with abnormal uterine bleeding.

**Ix**: as per endometrial cancer.

**Mx of EIN**: hysterectomy, due to high risk of concurrent or progressive endometrial carcinoma. If not a surgical candidate, progesterone therapy is indicated.
